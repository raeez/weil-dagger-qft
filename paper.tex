\begin{abstract}
The zeta explicit formula defines a Hermitian functional whose positivity
on a nonunital convolution algebra is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis.
We derive the complete formula in a fixed Fourier convention and construct
the operator representations supplied by positive functionals on
complex nonunital $*$-algebras.  The graph completion is the intersection of all
multiplier closure domains; a sum-of-squares estimate proves simultaneous
approximation and invariance.  Concentrating compactly supported tests
exclude finite positive unitization and every finite-norm vector
implementing the full functional on a common invariant $*$-domain.
The normalized Gaussian has Weil mass
$(4\sqrt\pi)^{-1}a^{-1/2}\log(1/a)+O(a^{-1/2})$.
Every fixed finite family of distinct Gaussian scales gives a positive
subspace at sufficiently small common scale.  On compact support windows,
a weighted Schur inequality bounds the full logarithmic form by finite
moments and its complete residual Gram matrix.  Explicit finite
inequalities prove positivity on windows of radius at most $81/100$.
Explicit cofinal indices
and finite rational tail maps make all approximation errors vanish.
The remaining signed matrix estimate is equivalent to full positivity,
and a positive power-law margin is impossible even on a cofinal sequence.
Independently positive prime translation forms give
Fourier representations, infinite weights, and Fock current operators.
For the complete Euler coefficients, a weighted diagonal on ordinary
$L^2(\R)$ exists for every vector exactly when
$\sum_p|\alpha_p|^2\log p/(\sqrt p-1)<\infty$; otherwise its domain is
zero.  Bounded multiplication and closed number currents persist beyond
this creation-vector restriction.  Their exact residual comparison
preserves the open problem of an independently positive realization of
the full Weil functional.
\end{abstract}

\section{The arithmetic form and the representation problem}
\label{sec:native-form}
\label{chap:weil-operator-domains}

The prime powers in the zeta explicit formula occur with a negative sign.
The two polar terms and the archimedean term must compensate for them if
the formula is to give a nonnegative value on every convolution square.
Determining whether that compensation always occurs is equivalent to the
Riemann hypothesis.  A positive realization constructed directly from the
local arithmetic terms would therefore have a consequence that the
explicit formula alone does not supply.

The comparison requires the full form, including its topology.  All algebras and
vector spaces are over \(\C\).  Inner products and sesquilinear forms are
linear in the first argument.  Integrals in the variable \(u\) use
Lebesgue measure \(du\); Fourier-space Hilbert integrals use
\(dt/(2\pi)\).
For \(a>0\), define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \A_a&=\{f\in C^\infty(\R):p_{a,k}(f)<\infty
                                  \text{ for every }k\geq0\},\\
 p_{a,k}(f)&=\int_\R e^{a|u|}|f^{(k)}(u)|\,du,\qquad
 \cA_\zeta=\A=\bigcup_{a>1/2}\A_a.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:test-class}
\end{equation}
Each \(\A_a\) has the locally convex topology defined by its displayed
seminorms.  On \(\A\) we use the locally convex inductive-limit topology
of the increasing sequence \(\A_{1/2+1/n}\), \(n\geq1\): it is the
finest locally convex topology making all these inclusions continuous.
The union is the same as the union in \eqref{eq:test-class}.
No assertion that this inductive limit is strict is needed.
The seminorms and the inclusions specify the topology independently of
any zero distribution or positivity hypothesis.

For \(f,g\in\A\), put
\begin{equation}
 (f\star g)(u)=\int_\R f(v)g(u-v)\,dv,
 \qquad f^*(u)=\overline{f(-u)}.
 \label{eq:operations}\alsoeq{eq:conv}
\end{equation}
The Fourier convention, together with the inverse formula proved below,
is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat f(z)=\int_\R f(u)e^{izu}\,du,
 \qquad f(u)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R\widehat f(t)e^{-itu}\,dt.
 \label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
The transform of \(f\in\A_a\) is defined for
\(|\operatorname{Im}z|<a\).  Its centered Mellin variable is
\begin{equation}
 F_f(s)=\widehat f\bigl(-i(s-1/2)\bigr)
       =\int_\R f(u)e^{(s-1/2)u}\,du
       =\int_0^\infty f(\log x)x^{s-1/2}\,\frac{dx}{x}.
 \label{eq:centered-mellin}\alsoeq{eq:mellin}
\end{equation}
In particular \(F_f(0)=\widehat f(i/2)\) and
\(F_f(1)=\widehat f(-i/2)\).  These are two distinct evaluations for a
general complex test function.

Write \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)=\log p\) if \(n=p^r\), with \(p\)
prime and \(r\geq1\), and zero otherwise.  For \(\operatorname{Re}z>0\)
let
\[
 \Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{z-1}\,dx,
 \qquad \psi(z)=\Gamma'(z)/\Gamma(z),
 \qquad \Gamma_\R(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2).
\]
The nonvanishing and the bounds that justify these logarithmic
derivatives are proved in Section~\ref{sec:euler-gamma}.
Define the full Weil functional by
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 L_\zeta(g)={}&\widehat g(i/2)+\widehat g(-i/2)\\
 &+\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R\widehat g(t)
   \left[\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi\right]dt\\
 &-\sum_{n\geq2}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}
                \bigl(g(\log n)+g(-\log n)\bigr).
\end{split}
\label{eq:weil-functional}
\end{equation}
We also write \(L=L_\zeta\).  The first line is the polar contribution,
the integral is the archimedean contribution, and the last line is the
negative prime-power contribution.  Every term converges on \(\A\), as
proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:explicit}.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 2\operatorname{Re}\frac{\Gamma_\R'}{\Gamma_\R}(1/2+it)
   =\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi.
 \label{eq:gamma-normalization}
\end{equation}
We use completeness of Lebesgue spaces,
Fubini's theorem for absolutely integrable functions, and dominated and
monotone convergence.  Absolute majorants are supplied at their uses.
Fourier--Plancherel and approximation in the weighted Hilbert spaces
are treated when those Hilbert spaces are introduced.

The associated form is
\(B_\zeta(f,g)=L_\zeta(g^*\star f)\).  A sesquilinear form is Hermitian
if reversing its arguments conjugates its value; it is positive if all
its diagonal values are nonnegative real numbers.  We shall prove that
\(B_\zeta\) is Hermitian unconditionally and positive exactly when all
nontrivial zeros of \(\zeta\) lie on the line \(\operatorname{Re}s=1/2\).

There is a concrete positive construction near this problem.  For
\(h\in\R\), let \(\tau_h f(u)=f(u-h)\).  If
\(q=f^*\star f\), with the fixed convolution and involution,
then
\[
 2q(0)-q(h)-q(-h)=\|f-\tau_h f\|_{L^2(\R)}^2.
\]
Thus adding a diagonal term turns each negative pair of prime-power
evaluations into a squared norm.  The resulting positive forms have
faithful representations and well-defined current operators.  Their
diagonal compensation grows when more primes are included.  The exact
size of that growth decides which infinite sums exist and why this
particular construction does not yet give the full Weil form.

The proof begins with the two convergent descriptions of
\(\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}\).  Prime factorization gives its Euler
product in \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\), while periodizing a Gaussian gives
continuation and reflection.  Their logarithmic derivatives enter one
contour integral.  The complex estimates in the next section give the
residue formula and the bounds needed to pass to an infinite contour.
The Fourier algebra then supplies the test functions on which the
arithmetic identity and its positivity criterion can both be proved.

Once positivity is stated as a hypothesis, multiplication of test
functions becomes an operator representation through the nonunital GNS
construction.  The common graph domain records all products at once.
Concentrating compact tests exclude a finite representing vector and
finite positive unitization.  The Gaussian calculation also gives
positive finite-dimensional subspaces without assuming full Weil positivity.
The compact-window construction in Sections~\ref{sec:compact-weil}--\ref{sec:window-cofinal}
then compares the full form with a diagonal Legendre operator.  Its
weighted Schur theorem and finite tail solve distinguish a lower
enclosure from the true omitted-mode correction.  Exact finite arithmetic
gives a positive lower bound on supports of radius at most $81/100$.
For growing supports, explicit tail maps have approximation errors that
vanish at prescribed rates, leaving the signed
low matrix as the exact positivity problem.  Continuity in the original
form topology passes termwise obstructions to compact smooth tests.
The prime constructions
then give independently positive examples with the same translation
operations and a precise comparison with \eqref{eq:weil-functional}.
The final question retains the full functional, its products and
involution, and the independence of its positivity.


\section{Elementary complex estimates}
\label{sec:complex}

A holomorphic function is a complex-differentiable function on an open set.
Differentiability implies continuity, but we will not assume continuity
of the derivative in deriving the integral formulas.
A meromorphic function is locally a quotient of holomorphic functions,
with denominator not identically zero.
A zero or pole has order $m$ if its local expression contains a factor
$(z-z_0)^m$ or $(z-z_0)^{-m}$ with nonzero remaining factor.
The residue is the coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$.

For a piecewise continuously differentiable path $\gamma:[a,b]\to\C$,
define
\[
 \int_\gamma h(w)\,dw
   =\int_a^b h(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt,
\]
adding the integrals over its differentiable pieces.
Reversing a path reverses the sign of its integral.

\begin{lemma}[Integration around a triangle]
\label{lem:weil-triangle-integral}
If $h$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of a closed triangle $T$, then
$\int_{\partial T}h(w)\,dw=0$, with the boundary oriented counterclockwise.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Joining the edge midpoints divides $T$ into four triangles.
Their oriented boundary integrals sum to the original integral because
every internal edge occurs in both directions.
Choose a subtriangle whose integral has modulus at least one quarter
of that of its parent, and repeat.  The resulting nested closed triangles
$T_n$ have diameter $2^{-n}D$ and perimeter $2^{-n}P$, where $D,P$ are
those of $T$.  Points chosen in these triangles form a Cauchy sequence.
Completeness gives a limit $w_0$ belonging to every $T_n$, and their
shrinking diameters make this common point unique.

Differentiability at $w_0$ gives
\[
 h(w)=h(w_0)+h'(w_0)(w-w_0)+(w-w_0)\epsilon(w),
 \qquad \epsilon(w)\longrightarrow0\quad(w\to w_0).
\]
The first two terms have polynomial primitives, so their integrals around
each triangle vanish by the real fundamental theorem of calculus on each
edge.  Put $\epsilon_n=\sup_{w\in T_n}|\epsilon(w)|$, defining
$\epsilon(w_0)=0$.  Then $\epsilon_n\to0$, and
\[
 \left|\int_{\partial T}h(w)\,dw\right|
 \leq4^n\left|\int_{\partial T_n}h(w)\,dw\right|
 \leq4^n\epsilon_n(2^{-n}D)(2^{-n}P)
 =\epsilon_nDP\longrightarrow0.
\]
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Contour formulas]
\label{lem:cauchy}
Let a function be holomorphic on a neighborhood of a closed disc.
For any interior point $z$,
\[
 h(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|w-z_0|=R}\frac{h(w)}{w-z}\,dw,
 \qquad
 |h^{(k)}(z_0)|\leq \frac{k!}{R^k}\max_{|w-z_0|=R}|h(w)|.
\]
The contour has counterclockwise orientation.
For a meromorphic function on a rectangle with no boundary poles, its
boundary integral is $2\pi i$ times the sum of its interior residues.
Locally uniform limits of holomorphic functions are holomorphic.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Summing the triangle identity gives zero around any polygonal region
on whose neighborhood the integrand is holomorphic, including a region
with polygonal holes.  To obtain a finite subdivision, draw parallel
lines through its finitely many vertices, divide the resulting strips
into trapezoids and triangles, and split each trapezoid along a diagonal.
The internal edges cancel, leaving the outer boundary counterclockwise
and the inner boundaries clockwise.

The same identity holds for a circular boundary by polygonal
approximation.  Linear interpolation of its smooth parametrization
converges uniformly in position; on each interpolation interval its
derivative is the average of the original derivative.  Uniform continuity
of that derivative gives convergence of tangents in the integral norm.
Continuity of the integrand, uniformly on a compact neighborhood of the
circle, then gives convergence of the boundary integrals.

Apply this identity to $h(w)/(w-z)$ between the original circle and a
small circle centered at $z$.  Its neighborhood contains no singularity
of this integrand.  On the small circle, write
$h(w)=h(z)+O(|w-z|)$ and let its radius tend to zero.
The constant term integrates to $2\pi i h(z)$ and the error tends to zero.
This proves the first formula without a regularity assumption on $h'$.
Expansion of $(w-z)^{-1}$ as a geometric series gives the derivative formula
and the displayed estimate, as well as a convergent local power series
for every holomorphic function.
For the residue formula, remove disjoint small discs around the poles.
The same boundary argument applies to the remaining region.
The finite principal part at each pole follows by factoring the first
nonzero Taylor term of the denominator in a local quotient.
On its small circle, only the term $(w-w_0)^{-1}$ has nonzero integral,
namely $2\pi i$ times its coefficient.  Adding the circles proves the
residue statement.
For a locally uniform limit, pass to the limit in the circle formula.
The resulting expression is differentiable in the interior point and gives
its convergent power series.  These arguments also justify the formulas
on discs and rectangles with finitely many small discs removed.
\end{proof}

The circle formula implies the identity theorem: a nonzero holomorphic
function has isolated zeros, since its first nonzero Taylor coefficient
factors out the entire order of a zero.  It also implies the maximum
modulus principle.  If an interior modulus is maximal, equality in the
circle average forces constant argument and modulus on a small circle.
Its Taylor coefficients of positive degree then vanish, and the function
is constant on the connected domain.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Logarithms and counting zeros]
\label{lem:jensen}
A zero-free holomorphic function on a disc has a holomorphic logarithm.
If an entire function $h$ satisfies $h(0)\ne0$ and
\[
 \log\max_{|z|\leq R}|h(z)|\leq C R\log(R+2)\qquad(R\geq2),
\]
then the number $n_h(R)$ of its zeros in $|z|\leq R$, with multiplicity,
satisfies $n_h(R)=O(R\log(R+2))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a zero-free function, the integral of $h'/h$ around any closed contour
in the disc is zero by Lemma~\ref{lem:cauchy}.  Its path integral is thus a
primitive $\ell$.  Differentiation shows that $e^{-\ell}h$ is constant.
Choosing the additive constant gives $h=e^\ell$.

Choose $r>R$ so that $h$ has no zero on $|z|=r$.
Factor out its finitely many zeros $a_j$ inside that circle.
The remaining nonzero factor has a logarithm whose real part has its value
at zero equal to its circle average.
For $|a|<r$, the power series of $\log(1-ae^{-i\theta}/r)$ gives
the circle average $\log|re^{i\theta}-a|=\log r$.
Subtracting the value at zero gives
\[
 \sum_{|a_j|<r}\log\frac r{|a_j|}
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|h(re^{i\theta})|\,d\theta
       -\log|h(0)|.
\]
Take $2R<r<2R+1$, avoiding the isolated radii of zeros.
Each zero in $|z|\leq R$ contributes at least $\log2$.
The asserted growth bound proves the count.
\end{proof}

The contour will need a logarithmic derivative estimate, not only a count.
The next lemma supplies that extra information locally.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[A local logarithmic derivative bound]
\label{lem:local-log}
Let $h$ be holomorphic on a neighborhood of $|z|\leq R$, where
$5\leq R\leq6$, and suppose $h(0)\ne0$ and $h$ has no boundary zero.
Suppose
\[
 |h(z)|\leq e^M\quad(|z|\leq R),\qquad |h(0)|\geq e^{-M},\qquad M\geq1.
\]
If $h$ has $N$ zeros in the disc and $|z|\leq3$ is at distance at least
$\delta>0$ from each zero, then
\[
 \left|\frac{h'(z)}{h(z)}\right|
 \leq C M+N(\delta^{-1}+1/2),
\]
where $C$ is an absolute constant.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each zero $a$, including multiplicities, use the factor
$b_a(z)=R(z-a)/(R^2-\bar a z)$.
It has modulus one on $|z|=R$ and no denominator zero in the disc.
Divide $h$ by their product and remove the canceled singularities.
The quotient $v$ is holomorphic and zero-free.
The maximum principle gives $|v|\leq e^M$.
Since $|b_a(0)|<1$, also $|v(0)|\geq|h(0)|\geq e^{-M}$.
Choose $\ell=\log v$ with $\ell(0)$ real after multiplying $v$ by a constant
of modulus one.  The function $p=M+1-\ell$ has positive real part and
$1\leq p(0)\leq2M+1$.

The function $q=(p-p(0))/(p+p(0))$ maps the disc into the unit disc and
vanishes at zero.  Applying the maximum principle to $q(z)/z$ on smaller
circles and increasing their radii to $R$ gives $|q(z)|\leq|z|/R$.
Hence $|p(z)|\leq9p(0)$ on $|z|\leq4$.
The derivative estimate on radius-one circles about $|z|\leq3$ yields
$|\ell'(z)|=|p'(z)|\leq9p(0)\leq27M$.
Finally
\[
 \frac{h'}h=\ell'+\sum_a
 \left(\frac1{z-a}+\frac{\bar a}{R^2-\bar a z}\right).
\]
For $|z|\leq3$, the second fraction has modulus at most $1/(R-3)\leq1/2$.
The assumed distance bound controls the first.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\section{The test algebra and Gaussian Fourier inversion}
\label{sec:tests}

\begin{lemma}[Decay, boundary values, and transforms]
\label{lem:test-bounds}
If $g\in\A_a$, then $e^{a|u|}g^{(k)}(u)\to0$ at both ends of $\R$,
and $|g^{(k)}(u)|\leq C_k e^{-a|u|}$.
The transform $\widehat g$ is holomorphic for $|\operatorname{Im}z|<a$.
For every $0<b<a$ and every integer $N\geq0$,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq b}(1+|z|)^N|\widehat g(z)|<\infty.
 \label{eq:strip-decay}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $F_g$ is holomorphic for $1/2-a<\operatorname{Re}s<1/2+a$
and decreases faster than any inverse power of $|\operatorname{Im}s|$
on every smaller closed vertical strip.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On $u\geq0$, the function $h(u)=e^{au}g^{(k)}(u)$ and its derivative
$e^{au}(ag^{(k)}+g^{(k+1)})$ are integrable.
The integral of the derivative makes $h$ have a finite limit at infinity.
Integrability of $h$ forces that limit to be zero.
Moreover $|h(u)|\leq\int_u^\infty|h'(v)|\,dv$.
The negative half-line has the same proof after reflection.

For $b<a$, the functions $|u|^m e^{b|u|}|g(u)|$ are integrable for every
$m$, because $|u|^m e^{-(a-b)|u|}$ is bounded.
These bounds permit termwise complex differentiation of the integral.
Repeated integration by parts has zero boundary terms by the first assertion
and gives
\[
 (-iz)^N\widehat g(z)=\int_{\R}g^{(N)}(u)e^{izu}\,du.
\]
Its right side is bounded on $|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq b$.
Combining this with the bound for $N=0$ proves \eqref{eq:strip-decay}.
Substitution in \eqref{eq:centered-mellin} proves the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[The normalized Gaussian]
\label{lem:gaussian}
For $A>0$, put
\[
 g_A(u)=(4\pi A)^{-1/2}e^{-u^2/(4A)}.
\]
Then $g_A\in\A_a$ for every $a>0$, and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat g_A(z)=e^{-Az^2},\qquad
 g_A\star g_B=g_{A+B},\qquad \int_{\R}g_A(u)\,du=1.
 \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
For $g\in\A$ one has the inverse formula
\begin{equation}
 g(u)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)e^{-itu}\,dt.
 \label{eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
The same inverse formula holds for a smooth function $g$ with
$g,g',g''\in L^1(\R)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Squaring $\int e^{-x^2}\,dx$ and using polar coordinates gives its value
$\sqrt\pi$.  Scaling proves the mass assertion.
Every derivative of $g_A$ is a polynomial times this Gaussian, proving
membership and vanishing of every exponential-weighted boundary value.
For real $t$, differentiate $J(t)=\widehat g_A(t)$ under the integral.
Since $u g_A=-2A g_A'$, integration by parts gives
$J'(t)=-2AtJ(t)$.  The initial value $J(0)=1$ gives $J(t)=e^{-At^2}$.
Both sides are entire, so the identity theorem extends the formula to $z\in\C$.
Completing the square in the convolution integral gives $g_{A+B}$.

For inversion, insert the factor $e^{-At^2}$ on the right of
\eqref{eq:inverse}.  Absolute integrability permits reversing the integrals.
The Gaussian transform just proved gives
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}e^{-At^2}\widehat g(t)e^{-itu}\,dt
       =(g_A\star g)(u).
\]
The left side tends to the proposed inverse by \eqref{eq:strip-decay}.
On the right, continuity of $g$ controls $|v|<\eta$ in the convolution
difference, and the Gaussian mass outside $|v|<\eta$ tends to zero.
Boundedness of $g$ controls that tail.  Thus $(g_A\star g)(u)\to g(u)$.
For the stated larger class, integrability of $g,g',g''$ makes $g,g'$
bounded with zero limits at infinity, by the half-line argument of
Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds} with zero weight.
Two integrations by parts give an integrable bound for $\widehat g$.
The same proof then applies.
\end{proof}

The parameter $A$ is also a heat parameter.  Direct differentiation gives
\[
 \partial_A g_A(u)=\left(-\frac1{2A}+\frac{u^2}{4A^2}\right)g_A(u)
                  =\partial_u^2g_A(u).
\]
Thus convolution by $g_A$ solves heat evolution on the real line for the
smooth integrable initial functions considered here.
The factor $e^{-At^2}$ damps high Fourier frequencies, and
$g_A\star g_B=g_{A+B}$ expresses addition of elapsed heat times.
This supplies an explicit analytic regularization for the inverse transform
and for the arithmetic identities below.

A complex $*$-algebra is an associative complex algebra with a conjugate-linear
map $a\mapsto a^*$ such that $(a^*)^*=a$ and $(ab)^*=b^*a^*$.
For the $*$-algebras in this article, a unit is a nonzero element $1$
with $1a=a1=a$ for every $a$;
nonunital means that no such element is present.
In particular, the zero algebra is nonunital under this convention.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[Algebra and change of exponential convention]
\label{prop:algebra}
Each $\A_a$ for $a>0$, and also $\A$, is a commutative nonunital
$*$-algebra under \eqref{eq:operations}, and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{f\star g}(z)=\widehat f(z)\widehat g(z),\qquad
 \widehat{f^*}(z)=\overline{\widehat f(\bar z)}.
 \label{eq:star-transform}\alsoeq{eq:transform}
\end{equation}
These equations hold on every common strip of holomorphy.  On the real axis,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{f^*\star f}(t)=|\widehat f(t)|^2.
 \label{eq:square-transform}
\end{equation}
Each fixed-rate space $\A_a$ is complete for the seminorms $p_{a,k}$;
convolution is jointly continuous there and involution is continuous.
The involution and multiplication by each fixed $f\in\A$ are continuous
on the stated inductive-limit algebra.
The union in \eqref{eq:test-class} is unchanged if weighted $L^1$ bounds
are replaced by weighted supremum bounds, with the exponential rate allowed
to decrease strictly.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $a>0$ and $f,g\in\A_a$, use
$e^{a|u|}\leq e^{a|v|}e^{a|u-v|}$ to obtain
$p_{a,k}(f\star g)\leq p_{a,k}(f)p_{a,0}(g)$.
If $f,g\in\A$, they admit a common $a>1/2$.
Differentiation, reassociation, and exchange of factors are justified by
these absolute integral bounds.  Reflection proves the involution rule,
and reversing the absolutely convergent double integral proves the first
transform equation.  The second follows from $u\mapsto-u$ and conjugation.
If an identity $e$ existed in any one of these algebras, convolution with
$g_A$ would imply $\widehat e(t)=1$, contradicting \eqref{eq:strip-decay}.

Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds} changes weighted $L^1$ bounds into weighted
supremum bounds at the same rate.  Conversely, if
$|g^{(k)}(u)|\leq C_k e^{-a|u|}$, then for $1/2<b<a$,
$p_{b,k}(g)\leq2C_k/(a-b)$.
This proves the union equality without identifying its individual fixed-rate
spaces or removing the loss in the rate.

For completeness, let $(f_j)$ be Cauchy in every $p_{a,k}$.
On a compact interval, the elementary estimate
\[
 \sup_{[r,s]}|h|\leq (s-r)^{-1}\int_r^s|h|+\int_r^s|h'|
\]
follows by comparing $h(x)$ with $h(y)$ and then averaging in $y$.
It makes each derivative sequence uniformly Cauchy locally.
The limits are successive derivatives by the fundamental theorem of
calculus.  Completeness of weighted $L^1$ identifies these limits with
the weighted $L^1$ limits, so the resulting smooth function is in
$\A_a$ and convergence holds in every seminorm.
The convolution bound already proved and
$p_{a,k}(f^*)=p_{a,k}(f)$ give the fixed-rate continuity.
For multiplication by fixed $f\in\A_b$, each source step $\A_a$
maps continuously into $\A_{\min(a,b)}$, whose inclusion into $\A$
is continuous.  The defining property of the inductive-limit topology
proves the asserted separate continuity on $\A$.
\end{proof}

\section{Prime factors and the gamma factor}
\label{sec:euler-gamma}

For positive $x$ and complex $s$, write $x^s=\exp(s\log x)$ with the real
logarithm of $x$.  This convention fixes every power used below.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[The convergent prime product]
\label{lem:euler}
The series $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}$ defines a holomorphic nonzero
function for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.  In that half-plane,
\begin{equation}
 \zeta(s)=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-1},\qquad
 \frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(s)=-\sum_{n\geq2}\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)n^{-s}.
 \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
Both the logarithmic series and its derivative converge absolutely on every
smaller closed half-plane.  On $s=2+it$, $|\zeta(s)|\geq\zeta(2)^{-1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Existence and uniqueness of prime factorization follow by induction from
the fact that a prime dividing a product divides a factor.  The latter
follows from the integer Euclidean algorithm and its identity for the
greatest common divisor.
Expanding a finite prime product into geometric series therefore gives the
sum over integers supported on those primes.
For $\sigma>1$, the integral comparison
$\sum n^{-\sigma}\leq1+\int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}\,dx$ is finite.
It bounds the omitted tails and proves the product identity.

The series
$\sum_p\sum_{m\geq1}p^{-ms}/m$ and its derivative are locally uniformly
absolutely convergent: their absolute values are bounded by constants times
$\sum_{n\geq2}(1+\log n)n^{-\sigma}$.
Exponentiating that series gives the product, so the product is nonzero.
Differentiation gives \eqref{eq:euler}.
Finally
\[
 |\zeta(2+it)|^{-1}
 \leq\prod_p(1+p^{-2})\leq\prod_p(1-p^{-2})^{-1}=\zeta(2).
\]
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Gamma product and vertical bounds]
\label{lem:gamma}
For $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, the integral
\[
 \Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{z-1}\,dx
\]
is holomorphic and nonzero.  Its reciprocal extends to the entire function
\begin{equation}
 E(z)=\frac1{\Gamma(z)}
 =z e^{\gamma z}\prod_{n\geq1}(1+z/n)e^{-z/n},
 \label{eq:gamma-product}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma=\lim_{N\to\infty}(\sum_{n=1}^N1/n-\log N)$.
Its zeros are simple and are exactly $0,-1,-2,\ldots$.
For $R\geq2$,
\begin{equation}
 |E(z)|\leq\exp(CR\log(R+2))\quad(|z|\leq R).
 \label{eq:reciprocal-growth}
\end{equation}
The logarithmic derivative satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \psi(z)=-\gamma+\sum_{n\geq0}
           \left(\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{n+z}\right),
 \qquad
 \psi(\sigma+it)=O_{\alpha,\beta}(\log(2+|t|))
 \label{eq:digamma}
\end{equation}
uniformly for $0<\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The integral and its derivatives have integrable majorants on compact
subsets of $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.
Repeated real integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^1 x^{z-1}(1-x)^N\,dx
       =\frac{N!}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+N)}.
\]
After multiplying by $N^z$ and substituting $u=Nx$, the integrand tends to
$u^{z-1}e^{-u}$.  The inequality $(1-u/N)^N\leq e^{-u}$ for $0\leq u<N$
is an integrable bound, also after any fixed number of $z$ derivatives
on compact subsets.  Thus
\[
 \Gamma(z)=\lim_{N\to\infty}
        \frac{N^zN!}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+N)}.
\]
The sequence $\sum_{n=1}^N1/n-\log N$ decreases and is bounded below
by the comparison of $1/x$ with its left and right rectangle sums.
Taking reciprocals of the finite expressions gives the finite products
in \eqref{eq:gamma-product}.
For $|w|\leq1/2$, the power series of the logarithm gives
$|\log(1+w)-w|\leq C|w|^2$.
Hence their tails converge uniformly on compact sets to a nonzero factor.
The finite initial factors identify the zeros and their orders.
Multiplication of the two limits gives $\Gamma E=1$ on the right half-plane,
proving nonvanishing and the reciprocal formula.

For $|z|\leq R$, split the product at $n=\lceil2R\rceil$.
The logarithms of absolute values of the initial factors are bounded above by
\[
 \sum_{n\leq\lceil2R\rceil}
       \bigl(\log(1+R/n)+R/n\bigr)=O(R\log(R+2)).
\]
The remaining logarithmic tail is $O(R^2\sum_{n>2R}n^{-2})=O(R)$.
Including $z e^{\gamma z}$ proves \eqref{eq:reciprocal-growth}.

Logarithmic differentiation gives the series in \eqref{eq:digamma}.
For $|t|\geq1$, split that series at $n=\lceil2(|t|+\beta+1)\rceil$.
The sum of $1/(n+1)$ before that point is $O(\log(2+|t|))$,
and the sum of $|n+z|^{-1}$ is $O(1)$ because $|n+z|\geq|t|$.
After that point, each difference is bounded by $C|z-1|/(n+1)^2$,
whose sum is $O(1)$.
The compact part with $|t|\leq1$ has no pole and is bounded.
\end{proof}

Define
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma_{\R}(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2),\qquad
 H_\Gamma(s)=\frac{\Gamma_{\R}'}{\Gamma_{\R}}(s)
          =-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\psi(s/2).
 \label{eq:gamma-real}
\end{equation}
The half in $s/2$ and the real factor $\pi^{-s/2}$ will produce respectively
the argument $1/4+it/2$ and the term $-\log\pi$ in \eqref{eq:weil-functional}.

\section{Gaussian reflection and the completed function}
\label{sec:theta}

The completion of the Dirichlet series will come from a lattice sum.
The identity needed for that sum is a direct consequence of the Gaussian
Fourier transform, with its normalization kept explicit.

The normalization in Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian} also gives the Fourier
integral used by periodization.  The substitution
$A=1/(4\pi x)$ and $z=-2\pi t$ relates the two conventions.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Gaussian Fourier transform]
\label{lem:gaussian-real-transform}
For $x>0$ and real $t$,
\[
 \int_{\R}e^{-\pi xv^2}e^{-2\pi itv}\,dv
       =x^{-1/2}e^{-\pi t^2/x}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}, take $A=1/(4\pi x)$.
Then $g_A(v)=\sqrt{x}\,e^{-\pi xv^2}$.
Evaluating its transform at $-2\pi t$ and dividing by $\sqrt{x}$
gives exactly the displayed formula.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Periodization of a Gaussian]
\label{lem:theta}
For $x>0$, put $\theta(x)=\sum_{n\in\Z}e^{-\pi n^2x}$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \theta(x)=x^{-1/2}\theta(1/x).
 \label{eq:theta-reflection}
\end{equation}
For $x\geq1$, one has $0<\theta(x)-1\leq C e^{-\pi x}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The periodized function
$P_x(u)=\sum_{n\in\Z}e^{-\pi x(u+n)^2}$ and all its derivatives converge
uniformly for $0\leq u\leq1$.
Its $k$th Fourier coefficient, with period one, is
\[
 \int_0^1P_x(u)e^{-2\pi iku}\,du
 =\int_{\R}e^{-\pi xv^2}e^{-2\pi ikv}\,dv
 =x^{-1/2}e^{-\pi k^2/x},
\]
by Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}.
These coefficients are absolutely summable and define a continuous
periodic Fourier series.

For completeness, a continuous periodic function with all Fourier
coefficients zero is zero.  Indeed, the kernel
\[
 K_N(u)=\frac1N\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi iju}\right|^2
\]
is nonnegative, has integral one over a period, and is a finite Fourier sum.
Away from the integers it is at most $1/(N\sin^2\pi u)$.
Uniform continuity and this bound show that convolution with $K_N$ converges
uniformly to any continuous periodic function.
When all coefficients vanish, each convolution is zero.
Apply this uniqueness statement to $P_x$ minus its displayed Fourier series,
then set $u=0$ to obtain \eqref{eq:theta-reflection}.
Finally
$\theta(x)-1=2e^{-\pi x}\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-\pi(n^2-1)x}$,
and the last sum is bounded by its value at $x=1$.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}[Continuation and reflection]
\label{thm:completion}
The function $\mathcal L(s)=\Gamma_{\R}(s)\zeta(s)$, initially defined for
$\operatorname{Re}s>1$, extends meromorphically to $\C$ by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L(s)=-\frac1s+\frac1{s-1}
   +\frac12\int_1^\infty(\theta(x)-1)
                   \left(x^{s/2}+x^{(1-s)/2}\right)\frac{dx}{x}.
 \label{eq:theta-mellin}
\end{equation}
It has simple poles at zero and one, with residues $-1$ and $1$.
It satisfies $\mathcal L(s)=\mathcal L(1-s)$ and
$\mathcal L(\bar s)=\overline{\mathcal L(s)}$.
The function
\begin{equation}
 X(s)=\frac12 s(s-1)\mathcal L(s)
 \label{eq:entire-completion}
\end{equation}
is entire, obeys the same symmetries, and satisfies
$X(0)=X(1)=1/2$ and
\begin{equation}
 \log\max_{|s|\leq R}|X(s)|\leq C R\log(R+2)\qquad(R\geq2).
 \label{eq:entire-growth}
\end{equation}
Every zero of $X$ lies in $0\leq\operatorname{Re}s\leq1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, absolute convergence gives
\[
 \frac12\int_0^\infty(\theta(x)-1)x^{s/2}\frac{dx}{x}
 =\sum_{n\geq1}\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi n^2x}x^{s/2}\frac{dx}{x}
 =\Gamma_{\R}(s)\zeta(s).
\]
For the integral from zero to one, use
$\theta(x)-1=x^{-1/2}-1+x^{-1/2}(\theta(1/x)-1)$.
The first two terms integrate to $1/(s-1)-1/s$ after the factor $1/2$.
Substitution $y=1/x$ in the third gives the second term in the integral
of \eqref{eq:theta-mellin}.

The exponential bound in Lemma~\ref{lem:theta} makes that integral entire:
on every compact set, powers of $x$ and any fixed powers of $\log x$ are
bounded by an integrable exponential majorant.
The pole, residue, and symmetry assertions now follow directly.
In particular
\[
 X(s)=\frac12+\frac{s(s-1)}4
       \int_1^\infty(\theta(x)-1)
             \left(x^{s/2}+x^{(1-s)/2}\right)\frac{dx}{x}.
\]
For $|s|\leq R$, the integral is bounded by a constant times
$\int_1^\infty e^{-\pi x}x^{(R+1)/2}\,dx$.
If $r=(R+1)/2$, then
$x^r e^{-\pi x/2}\leq(2r/(\pi e))^r$, by maximizing its logarithm.
The remaining exponential is integrable.  This proves
\eqref{eq:entire-growth}, including the polynomial prefactor.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the gamma factor and the prime product are
nonzero, so $X$ is nonzero there.  Reflection excludes zeros for
$\operatorname{Re}s<0$.
\end{proof}

The continuation of the original Dirichlet series is now the specific
meromorphic function $\zeta(s)=\pi^{s/2}E(s/2)\mathcal L(s)$.
It has a simple pole at one.  At zero the zero of $E(s/2)$ cancels the
pole of $\mathcal L$ and gives $\zeta(0)=-1/2$.
At each negative even integer, $E(s/2)$ has a simple zero and
$\mathcal L(s)=\mathcal L(1-s)\ne0$.
These are its trivial zeros.  Its other zeros are precisely the zeros of
$X$, with the same multiplicities.  We call them the nontrivial zeros.
No assertion about their real parts inside the closed strip has been used.

The closed strip can be sharpened by a positive trigonometric identity.
The argument uses the pole of $\zeta$ at one and holomorphy at every
other point of the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[The pole and the boundary germs]
\label{lem:boundary-germs}
The continuation
$\zeta(s)=\pi^{s/2}E(s/2)\mathcal L(s)$ is holomorphic on
$\C\setminus\{1\}$ and has a simple pole of residue one at $s=1$.
For every nonzero real $t$, it is holomorphic on neighborhoods of
$1+it$ and $1+2it$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} makes $\mathcal L$ holomorphic away from
zero and one.  Lemma~\ref{lem:gamma} makes $E$ entire, with
$E(z)=z+O(z^2)$ at zero.
Thus $\pi^{s/2}E(s/2)\mathcal L(s)=-1/2+O(s)$ there, and its
singularity at zero is removable.
The Gaussian integral gives
\[
 \Gamma(1/2)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du=\sqrt\pi.
\]
Consequently $\pi^{1/2}E(1/2)=1$.
The residue of $\mathcal L$ at one is one, so
\begin{equation}
 \zeta(s)=\frac1{s-1}+H(s)
 \label{eq:boundary-pole}
\end{equation}
on a neighborhood of one, with $H$ holomorphic.
The asserted local holomorphy follows because $t\ne0$ places both
$1+it$ and $1+2it$ away from the only pole.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[A positive prime sum]
\label{lem:boundary-prime}
For every real $\sigma>1$ and real $t$,
\begin{equation}
 \zeta(\sigma)^3
 |\zeta(\sigma+it)|^4|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\geq1.
 \label{eq:boundary-product}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
On $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, define the holomorphic function
\[
 B(s)=\sum_p\sum_{r\geq1}\frac{p^{-rs}}r.
\]
Here $p$ runs over positive primes and $p^{-rs}=e^{-rs\log p}$.
For every $\delta>0$, its series converges absolutely and uniformly on
$\operatorname{Re}s\geq1+\delta$, since
\[
 \sum_p\sum_{r\geq1}\frac{p^{-r(1+\delta)}}r
 \leq\frac1{1-2^{-1-\delta}}\sum_{n\geq2}n^{-1-\delta}<\infty.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:euler} gives $e^{B(s)}=\zeta(s)$.
Taking absolute values yields $\operatorname{Re}B(s)=\log|\zeta(s)|$,
with the real logarithm of a positive number.
This operation needs no choice of a logarithm at a boundary point.
Absolute convergence permits the three series to be combined.
The identity
\[
 3+4\cos\theta+\cos(2\theta)=2(1+\cos\theta)^2\geq0
 \qquad(\theta\in\R)
\]
therefore gives
\begin{align*}
 &3\log\zeta(\sigma)+4\log|\zeta(\sigma+it)|
                      +\log|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\\
 &\quad=\sum_p\sum_{r\geq1}\frac{p^{-r\sigma}}r
       \bigl(3+4\cos(rt\log p)+\cos(2rt\log p)\bigr)\geq0.
\end{align*}
The Dirichlet series makes $\zeta(\sigma)>0$.
Exponentiation proves \eqref{eq:boundary-product}.
\end{proof}

The exponents in \eqref{eq:boundary-product} decide the boundary case.
A simple pole contributes order $-3$, while even a simple zero in the
middle factor contributes order $4$.
The third factor is bounded at the limiting point whenever $t\ne0$.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}[Nonvanishing on the boundary and the strict strip]
\label{thm:strict-strip}
For every real $t\ne0$, one has $\zeta(1+it)\ne0$.
At $s=1$, the function $\zeta$ has a simple pole of residue one.
Every zero $\rho$ of $X$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 0<\operatorname{Re}\rho<1,
 \qquad
 z_\rho=-i(\rho-1/2),
 \qquad
 |\operatorname{Im}z_\rho|<\frac12.
 \label{eq:strict-strip}
\end{equation}
The zeros of $X$ are exactly the nontrivial zeros of $\zeta$, with
the same multiplicities.
Reflection and complex conjugation preserve these multiplicities.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $t\in\R\setminus\{0\}$ and suppose $\zeta(1+it)=0$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:boundary-germs}, this is a zero of a holomorphic
function.  It has some finite order $m\geq1$.
Indeed, a zero of infinite order would make the function vanish on a
disc, contrary to its nonzero Euler product in the same disc's
intersection with $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.
Taylor expansion gives a holomorphic function $a$, with $a(0)\ne0$,
such that
\[
 \zeta(1+it+w)=w^m a(w)
\]
for sufficiently small complex $w$.
For positive real $h$ tending to zero, the pole expansion and local
holomorphy at $1+2it$ give constants $C_0,C_1,C_2>0$ such that
\[
 0<\zeta(1+h)\leq C_0h^{-1},\qquad
 |\zeta(1+h+it)|\leq C_1h^m,\qquad
 |\zeta(1+h+2it)|\leq C_2.
\]
The constants can depend on $t$ and on the chosen neighborhoods.
In particular, boundedness of the third factor allows a zero at $1+2it$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:boundary-prime} now implies
\[
 1\leq\zeta(1+h)^3|\zeta(1+h+it)|^4|\zeta(1+h+2it)|
 \leq C_0^3C_1^4C_2 h^{4m-3}\longrightarrow0.
\]
This contradiction proves nonvanishing for $t\ne0$.
When $t=0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:boundary-germs} gives the pole directly.
Thus the preceding limiting argument never treats the pole as a
holomorphic value of $\zeta$.

For $s=1+it$ with $t\ne0$, the factors $s$, $s-1$, and
$\Gamma_{\R}(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)$ are finite and nonzero.
The nonvanishing just proved therefore gives $X(1+it)\ne0$.
The value $X(1)=1/2$ handles $t=0$.
On the other boundary, the exact reflection identity gives
$X(it)=X(1-it)\ne0$ for every real $t$, including $X(0)=1/2$.
The closed-strip statement in Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} now yields
$0<\operatorname{Re}\rho<1$ for every zero $\rho$ of $X$.

On this open strip, $s(s-1)\Gamma_{\R}(s)/2$ is holomorphic and
nonzero.  Multiplication by it preserves each zero order, so the zeros
of $X$ and $\zeta$ there have the same multiplicities.
At $s=0$, one has $\zeta(0)=-1/2$.
For $\operatorname{Re}s<0$, reflection gives
$\mathcal L(s)=\mathcal L(1-s)\ne0$.
The reciprocal gamma factor has simple zeros exactly at
$s=0,-2,-4,\ldots$.
It follows that the zeros of $\zeta$ in $\operatorname{Re}s<0$ are
exactly the simple zeros at $s=-2,-4,\ldots$.
These are the trivial zeros.
On $\operatorname{Re}s=0$ with $s\ne0$, both $E(s/2)$ and
$\mathcal L(s)$ are nonzero.  The Euler product excludes zeros for
$\operatorname{Re}s>1$.
This proves the stated classification of all other zeros.

If $\rho=\beta+i\gamma$ with $\beta,\gamma\in\R$, then
\[
 z_\rho=\gamma+i(1/2-\beta).
\]
Thus $0<\beta<1$ is exactly the strict bound in
\eqref{eq:strict-strip}.
The affine map $s\mapsto-i(s-1/2)$ has nonzero derivative.
Consequently a zero of order $m$ at $s=\rho$ is a zero of order $m$
of $z\mapsto X(1/2+iz)$ at $z=z_\rho$.
Finally the identities $X(s)=X(1-s)$ and
$X(\bar s)=\overline{X(s)}$ preserve orders in the local power series.
In centered variables their images are
\[
 z_{1-\rho}=-z_\rho,\qquad
 z_{\bar\rho}=-\overline{z_\rho},\qquad
 z_{1-\bar\rho}=\overline{z_\rho}.
\]
All three parameters therefore occur with the same multiplicity as
$z_\rho$, with coincident parameters counted only according to their
actual zero order.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds} supplies exponential decay for every test in $\A$.
The same centered coordinate specifies where the Weil transform is
evaluated.  Suppose $a>1/2$ and $g:\R\to\C$ is continuous with
$|g(u)|\leq C e^{-a|u|}$.
Define
\[
 \widehat g(z)=\int_{\R}g(u)e^{izu}\,du,
 \qquad
 F_g(s)=\widehat g(-i(s-1/2))
       =\int_{\R}g(u)e^{(s-1/2)u}\,du.
\]
For each $0\leq b<a$ and integer $k\geq0$, the absolute value of the
$k$th derivative integrand is bounded on $|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq b$ by
\[
 C|u|^k e^{-(a-b)|u|}.
\]
This is integrable on $\R$.
Termwise differentiation therefore makes $\widehat g$ holomorphic on
$|\operatorname{Im}z|<a$.
It makes $F_g$ holomorphic on
$1/2-a<\operatorname{Re}s<1/2+a$.
Every nontrivial zero lies in this domain, and
$F_g(\rho)=\widehat g(z_\rho)$ uses the same multiplicity under the
invertible affine change of variable.

The strict strip permits zeros with $\operatorname{Re}\rho\ne1/2$.
The assertion that all $z_\rho$ are real is the Riemann hypothesis.
It remains an additional assertion beyond boundary nonvanishing.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{corollary}[Zero count and absolute test sums]
\label{cor:zero-count}
The number of nontrivial zeros with $|\rho|\leq R$, counted with multiplicity,
is $O(R\log(R+2))$.
For every $g\in\A$, the series $\sum_\rho|F_g(\rho)|$ converges.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:jensen} to $X$, using $X(0)=1/2$.
Since $0\leq\operatorname{Re}\rho\leq1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds}
gives $|F_g(\rho)|\leq C_N(1+|\operatorname{Im}\rho|)^{-N}$ for every $N$.
The zeros in $2^j\leq|\operatorname{Im}\rho|<2^{j+1}$ contribute at most
$C(j+1)2^{j(1-N)}$.  Taking $N=3$ gives a convergent series.
The bounded region has only finitely many zeros.
\end{proof}

\section{Heights at which the horizontal integrals vanish}
\label{sec:heights}

Write $H(s)=\mathcal L'(s)/\mathcal L(s)$.
It has residue equal to the multiplicity at each nontrivial zero.
Its residue at each of zero and one is $-1$.
These signs follow by differentiating the logarithm of a local factor
$(s-s_0)^m$ or $(s-s_0)^{-1}$.
The identity
\begin{equation}
 H(s)=\frac{X'(s)}{X(s)}-\frac1s-\frac1{s-1},
 \qquad H(1-s)=-H(s)
 \label{eq:log-reflection}
\end{equation}
will control both the poles and the left vertical line.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Admissible rectangle heights]
There are heights\label{lem:heights} $T_m\in[m,m+1]$, for all sufficiently large integers $m$,
such that no zero lies on either horizontal segment
$-1/2\leq\operatorname{Re}s\leq3/2$, $\operatorname{Im}s=\pm T_m$,
and
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{-1/2\leq\sigma\leq3/2}|H(\sigma\pm iT_m)|
       =O(T_m^2\log^2(T_m+2)).
 \label{eq:horizontal-H}
\end{equation}
For $g\in\A_a$ and $1<c<\min(a+1/2,3/2)$, both horizontal integrals
of $H(s)F_g(s)$ between the lines $1-c$ and $c$ tend to zero at these heights.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $N_m$ count all zeros in $|s|\leq m+8$, with multiplicity, and set
$\delta_m=1/(8(N_m+1))$.
Remove from $[m,m+1]$ the intervals of radius $\delta_m$ about the absolute
values of their imaginary parts.
The removed length is at most $2\delta_mN_m<1/4$, so a remaining $T_m$ exists.
Zeros outside this disc have $|\operatorname{Im}\rho|>m+1+\delta_m$
for large $m$, because $0\leq\operatorname{Re}\rho\leq1$.
Thus both heights have distance at least $\delta_m$ from every zero ordinate.
Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-count} gives
$\delta_m^{-1}=O(m\log(m+2))$.

Fix $T=T_m$ and center a disc at $s_0=2+iT$.
By \eqref{eq:entire-growth},
$|X(s_0+z)|\leq\exp(C(T+8)\log(T+8))$ for $|z|\leq6$.
At its center, the prime-product lower bound and
\eqref{eq:reciprocal-growth} give
\[
 |X(2+iT)|
 =\frac{|(2+iT)(1+iT)|}{2\pi}
       |\Gamma(1+iT/2)|\,|\zeta(2+iT)|
 \geq\exp(-C(T+8)\log(T+8)).
\]
Choose $R\in[5,6]$ with no zero on the circle.
The number of zeros in it is $O(T\log(T+2))$ by the global count.
Every point of the desired horizontal segment has $|s-s_0|\leq5/2$,
and its distance from every zero is at least $\delta_m$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:local-log} now gives
\[
 |X'(s)/X(s)|
 \leq C T\log(T+2)(1+\delta_m^{-1})
 =O(T^2\log^2(T+2)).
\]
The two rational terms in \eqref{eq:log-reflection} are bounded at these
heights.  Conjugation gives the same estimate on the bottom segment.

The chosen vertical strip lies strictly inside
$1/2-a<\operatorname{Re}s<1/2+a$.
Its test transform is $O(T^{-4})$ uniformly by
Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds}.
The horizontal lengths are bounded, so their integrals are
$O(T^{-2}\log^2(T+2))$, which tends to zero.
\end{proof}

The proof uses a polynomial bound, which suffices for every test in $\A$.
It does not require a sharper local zero count, a product over all zeros,
or an asymptotic formula for the number of zeros.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\section{The normalized explicit formula}
\label{sec:formula}

\begin{theorem}[The full arithmetic functional]
\label{thm:explicit}
For every $g\in\A$, all terms of \eqref{eq:weil-functional} converge
absolutely, the functional is continuous in the stated test-function
topology, and
\begin{equation}
 L_\zeta(g)=\sum_\rho F_g(\rho)
           =\sum_\rho\widehat g\bigl(-i(\rho-1/2)\bigr).
 \label{eq:target}\alsoeq{eq:explicit}
\end{equation}
The zero sum is absolutely convergent, with multiplicities, and
\begin{equation}
 L(g^*)=\overline{L(g)}.
 \label{eq:functional}\alsoeq{eq:hermitian}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose $g\in\A_a$ and $1<c<\min(a+1/2,3/2)$.
The prime terms converge because Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds} gives
\[
 \sum_{n\geq2}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}
                    (|g(\log n)|+|g(-\log n)|)
 \leq C\sum_{n\geq2}(\log n)n^{-a-1/2}<\infty.
\]
The gamma integral converges by \eqref{eq:digamma} and
\eqref{eq:strip-decay}.  The polar values are inside the transform strip,
and the zero sum converges by Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-count}.

These estimates also prove continuity.  For fixed $a>1/2$, the
half-line estimate bounds each prime evaluation by
$(a p_{a,0}(g)+p_{a,1}(g))e^{-a|u|}$.
The two polar evaluations are bounded by $p_{a,0}(g)$.
On the real Fourier axis, integration by parts bounds
$(1+t^2)|\widehat g(t)|$ by a constant times
$p_{a,0}(g)+p_{a,2}(g)$.
The integral of $\log(2+|t|)/(1+t^2)$ is finite.
Thus $L|_{\A_a}$ is bounded by finitely many defining seminorms.
Its continuity on every step gives continuity on the locally convex
inductive limit.  No Hilbert completion is used in this assertion.


Integrate $H(s)F_g(s)$ around the rectangle with vertical sides $1-c,c$
and heights $\pm T_m$, counterclockwise.  Its right side is traversed upward.
There are no poles on the vertical sides, since the zeros lie in the
closed critical strip.
The residue formula gives the zero sum inside the rectangle minus
$F_g(0)+F_g(1)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:heights} eliminates the horizontal integrals.
On $\operatorname{Re}s=c$, equations~\eqref{eq:euler} and
\eqref{eq:digamma} give $H(c+it)=O(\log(2+|t|))$.
Reflection gives the same bound on $1-c$, so both vertical integrals
converge absolutely.
In the left integral, substitute $s=1-w$ and use $H(1-w)=-H(w)$.
Both vertical-line integrals in the resulting expression are oriented upward.
The outcome is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\rho F_g(\rho)-F_g(0)-F_g(1)
 =\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=c}
           H(s)\bigl(F_g(s)+F_g(1-s)\bigr)\,ds.
 \label{eq:spine-contour}
\end{equation}

On this line, split $H=H_\Gamma+\zeta'/\zeta$.
The series for $\zeta'/\zeta$ can pass through the integral because
$\sum\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)n^{-c}<\infty$ and both test factors are integrable
on the vertical line.
For the first factor, Fourier inversion applied to
$u\mapsto e^{(c-1/2)u}g(u)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\operatorname{Re}s=c}n^{-s}F_g(s)\,ds
 =n^{-c}e^{(c-1/2)\log n}g(\log n)
 =n^{-1/2}g(\log n).
 \label{eq:prime-inversion}
\end{equation}
This weighted function has exponential decay with all derivatives, since
$c-1/2<a$.  The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian} therefore applies to it.
For $F_g(1-s)$, use $u\mapsto e^{(c-1/2)u}g(-u)$ instead.
It gives $n^{-1/2}g(-\log n)$.
The minus sign in \eqref{eq:euler} proves exactly the prime-power term in
\eqref{eq:weil-functional}.

It remains to evaluate the gamma contribution.
The function $H_\Gamma$ is holomorphic for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$.
Shift each gamma integral from $c$ to $1/2$.
There is no pole between these lines, and the logarithmic bound
\eqref{eq:digamma} times the rapid test decay eliminates the horizontal sides.
Set $s=1/2+it$.  The two factors are $\widehat g(t)$ and $\widehat g(-t)$.
Changing $t$ to $-t$ in the second term gives
\begin{align*}
 &\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)
         \bigl(H_\Gamma(1/2+it)+H_\Gamma(1/2-it)\bigr)\,dt\\
 &\hspace{1cm}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)
     \left[\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi\right]dt.
\end{align*}
Here conjugation of $\Gamma$ follows from its defining real integral and
analytic continuation.  Together with \eqref{eq:spine-contour}, this proves
\eqref{eq:target}.

Finally the gamma bracket is real and even.
For real $t$, \eqref{eq:star-transform} gives
$\widehat{g^*}(t)=\overline{\widehat g(t)}$.
The involution interchanges the two polar values and the two values at
$\pm\log n$, followed by conjugation.
Thus all three local terms obey the Hermitian identity in
\eqref{eq:functional}.
\end{proof}

The proof explains the normalization directly.
The poles of $\mathcal L$ each contribute residue $-1$ to its logarithmic
derivative, giving the two positive polar values after rearrangement.
The logarithmic derivative of each Euler factor gives the negative prime
term.  The reflection combines the two gamma factors at $1/2\pm it$.
These operations preserve the original complex test function throughout.

\section{Gaussian tests and their boundary terms}
\label{sec:polynomial-gaussians}

For $g_A$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}, substitution in the formula gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 \sum_\rho e^{A(\rho-1/2)^2}
 ={}&2e^{A/4}
   +\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}e^{-At^2}
      \left[\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi\right]dt\\
 &-\frac1{\sqrt{\pi A}}\sum_{n\geq2}
       \frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}
                       e^{-(\log n)^2/(4A)}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:gaussian-explicit}
\end{equation}
If every nontrivial zero has real part $1/2$, each term in this Gaussian
zero sum is positive.  The identity itself is unconditional.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[Polynomial--Gaussian compatibility]
\label{prop:polynomial-gaussian}
Let $A>0$, $\tau\in\R$, and $P\in\C[w]$.  The function
\begin{equation}
 f(u)=e^{-i\tau u}P(i\partial_u)g_A(u)
 \label{eq:gaussian-inverse}
\end{equation}
belongs to $\A_a$ for every $a>0$, and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat f(z)=P(z-\tau)e^{-A(z-\tau)^2}.
 \label{eq:polynomial-transform}
\end{equation}
For $P\ne0$, every fixed $b>0$, and $z=t+iy$ with $|y|\leq b$,
\begin{equation}
 |\widehat f(z)|\leq C_{P,A,b,\tau}(1+|t|)^{\deg P}
                             e^{-A(t-\tau)^2+A b^2}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-strip}
\end{equation}
Consequently every real integration-by-parts boundary value and every
horizontal contour term used in Theorem~\ref{thm:explicit} vanishes for
$f$ and $f^*\star f$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $P=0$ one has $f=0$.  Otherwise, each derivative in
\eqref{eq:gaussian-inverse} is a polynomial in $u$ times
$e^{-u^2/(4A)}$, multiplied by $e^{-i\tau u}$.
This description holds after any further derivative.
Multiplication by $e^{a|u|}$ still leaves an integrable function tending
to zero at both infinities, for every $a>0$.
Integration by parts gives
$\widehat{i\partial_u h}(z)=z\widehat h(z)$, and modulation gives
$\widehat{e^{-i\tau u}h}(z)=\widehat h(z-\tau)$.
Together these prove \eqref{eq:polynomial-transform}.
The exact Gaussian modulus is
$|e^{-A(t-\tau+iy)^2}|=e^{-A(t-\tau)^2+Ay^2}$,
which proves \eqref{eq:gaussian-strip}.
This bound times \eqref{eq:horizontal-H} tends to zero at both heights.
The convolution square lies in every $\A_a$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:algebra}; its transform is the product in
\eqref{eq:star-transform}, with Gaussian decay of twice the exponent.
Thus it has the same boundary properties.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{example}[The first polynomial and a missing conjugation]
For $P(w)=w$, the function and its transform are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 f(u)&=-iu\,e^{-i\tau u}g_A(u)/(2A),\\
 \widehat f(z)&=(z-\tau)e^{-A(z-\tau)^2}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Its convolution square has transform
\[
 \widehat{f^*\star f}(z)=(z-\tau)^2e^{-2A(z-\tau)^2}.
\]
At $z=\tau+ib$, this value is $-b^2e^{2Ab^2}$.
The correct analytic expression is
$\overline{\widehat f(\bar z)}\widehat f(z)$.
Replacing it by $|\widehat f(z)|^2$ would erase the negative sign and would
not give a holomorphic function of $z$.
\end{example}

\section{A separate positivity argument}
\label{sec:positivity}

The explicit formula compares linear functionals.
Positivity concerns its value on every convolution square and requires a
different argument.  Define the Riemann hypothesis to mean that every
nontrivial zero $\rho$ satisfies $\operatorname{Re}\rho=1/2$.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}[Positivity on the exact test algebra]
\label{thm:positivity}
Weil's positivity criterion \cite{Weil1952}, in the present convention, is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RH}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(f^*\star f)\geq0\qquad\text{for every }f\in\A.
 \label{eq:positivity}\alsoeq{eq:weil-criterion}
\end{equation}
Under the Riemann hypothesis, writing $\rho=1/2+i\gamma_\rho$ gives
\begin{equation}
 L(f^*\star f)=\sum_\rho|\widehat f(\gamma_\rho)|^2.
 \label{eq:zero-norm}
\end{equation}
If there is a zero off the critical line, a function of the form
\eqref{eq:gaussian-inverse} has strictly negative convolution-square value.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $z_\rho=-i(\rho-1/2)$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:completion} gives $|\operatorname{Im}z_\rho|\leq1/2$.
Its reflection and conjugation symmetries show that $z_\rho$ and
$\bar z_\rho$ occur with equal multiplicity.
Under the Riemann hypothesis all these parameters are real.
Then Theorem~\ref{thm:explicit} and \eqref{eq:star-transform} give the
asserted nonnegative sum.

For the converse, suppose $z_0=\tau+ib$ occurs, where $b\ne0$.
Its conjugate has the same multiplicity $m$.
Center the parameter by $w=z-\tau$.
There are finitely many distinct centered zero parameters with
$|\operatorname{Re}w|\leq2$.
This follows from the global zero count and the bound on their imaginary
parts.  Form the monic polynomial $Q$ whose roots are these distinct points
except $ib$ and $-ib$.  If there are none, set $Q=1$.
This root set is invariant under conjugation, so $Q$ has real coefficients
and $Q(ib)Q(-ib)=|Q(ib)|^2\ne0$.
Set
\[
 P(w)=wQ(w)Q(-w),\qquad
 \widehat f_A(z)=P(z-\tau)e^{-A(z-\tau)^2},\qquad A\geq1.
\]
The polynomial $P$ is real and odd.
Proposition~\ref{prop:polynomial-gaussian} constructs $f_A\in\A$ exactly.
Since $\tau$ and the coefficients are real,
$\overline{\widehat f_A(\bar z)}=\widehat f_A(z)$.
The two selected zero parameters therefore contribute
\begin{equation}
 -2m b^2|Q(ib)|^4e^{2Ab^2}
 \label{eq:negative-pair}
\end{equation}
to $L(f_A^*\star f_A)$.
All other centered zero parameters with $|\operatorname{Re}w|\leq2$
give zero because $Q$ vanishes there.

For a remaining parameter $w=x+iy$, one has $|x|>2$, $|y|\leq1/2$,
and $D=x^2-y^2\geq15/4$.
For $A\geq1$, $2AD\geq15A/4+(x^2-1/4)$.
Thus the absolute value of the remaining sum is bounded by
\begin{equation}
 e^{-15A/4}
 \sum_{|\operatorname{Re}w|>2}|P(w)|^2e^{-(x^2-1/4)}.
 \label{eq:separator-tail}
\end{equation}
The final series is finite by the polynomial growth of $P$ and the zero
count.  It is independent of $A$.
Expression~\eqref{eq:negative-pair} tends to minus infinity while
\eqref{eq:separator-tail} tends to zero.
The Hermitian identity in \eqref{eq:functional} makes the full value real,
so it is strictly negative for sufficiently large $A$.
This contradicts \eqref{eq:positivity} and proves the converse.
\end{proof}

The conclusion is a criterion, not a proof of the Riemann hypothesis.
The transform estimates, the continuation, and the contour formula were
proved before invoking any sign condition.
Their exact comparison identifies the remaining arithmetic question:
whether the full local expression is nonnegative on every convolution square.
The negative separator describes what any off-line zero would force.




\section{Positive functionals and their representations}

Positivity would make the arithmetic convolution square a squared norm.
Repeated convolution would then have to act on vectors without leaving the
domain of the next operation.  These requirements are already visible for
an arbitrary complex algebra, and their construction does not require a unit.

A $*$-homomorphism is a complex linear map preserving
products and involution; no preservation of a unit is imposed here.
All inner products are linear in the first argument.
For a positive sesquilinear form, expansion of the diagonal at
$x+\lambda y$ gives
Hermitian symmetry and
\[
 |\langle x,y\rangle|^2\leq\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle.
\]
Indeed, when $\langle y,y\rangle>0$, minimize that quadratic expression in
$\lambda$; if it is zero, arbitrarily large multiples force the cross term
to vanish.  A positive definite form gives a norm
$\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$.  Its space is a pre-Hilbert space.
Completing Cauchy sequences modulo sequences tending to zero gives a
Hilbert space; continuity extends the inner product to that completion.
An isometry preserves this inner product, and a surjective isometry is
unitary.  An operator is bounded if $\|Sx\|\leq C\|x\|$ on its domain;
the least such constant is its operator norm.

Orthogonality means vanishing of the inner product.  For a linear subspace
$M$ of a Hilbert space $H$, write
\[
 M^\perp=\{v\in H:\langle v,m\rangle=0\text{ for every }m\in M\}.
\]
Closed subspaces have the following decomposition, which will distinguish
a dense operator range from a proper one.

\begin{lemma}[Projection onto a closed subspace]
\label{lem:weil-orthogonal-decomposition}
If $M\subset H$ is a closed linear subspace, every $v\in H$ has a unique
expression $v=m+n$ with $m\in M$ and $n\in M^\perp$.
In particular, $M^\perp=\{0\}$ implies $M=H$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $d^2=\inf_{m\in M}\|v-m\|^2$ and choose $m_j\in M$ with
$\|v-m_j\|^2\to d^2$.  Expansion of inner products gives the
parallelogram identity and hence
\[
 \|m_j-m_k\|^2
 =2\|v-m_j\|^2+2\|v-m_k\|^2
      -4\left\|v-\frac{m_j+m_k}{2}\right\|^2
 \leq2\|v-m_j\|^2+2\|v-m_k\|^2-4d^2.
\]
Thus $(m_j)$ is Cauchy.  Completeness and closedness give a limit
$m\in M$ attaining the infimum.
For $y\in M$, the real function $t\mapsto\|v-m-ty\|^2$ is minimized
at zero, so its linear coefficient gives
$\operatorname{Re}\langle v-m,y\rangle=0$.
Replacing $y$ by $iy$ also makes the imaginary part zero.
Therefore $n=v-m$ lies in $M^\perp$.
The intersection $M\cap M^\perp$ is zero, since an element of that
intersection has squared norm zero.  This proves uniqueness and the
last assertion.
\end{proof}

An operator on a Hilbert space $H$ means a linear map $S:D\to H$ on a
specified dense linear subspace.  Its graph is $\{(x,Sx):x\in D\}$.
It is closed when its graph is closed in $H\oplus H$, and closable when
the graph closure is again a graph.  Equivalently, $x_n\to0$ and
$Sx_n\to y$ must imply $y=0$; this equivalence follows by subtracting two
possible limiting graph values at the same vector.
The graph norm is $(\|x\|^2+\|Sx\|^2)^{1/2}$.  A core for a closable
operator is a subspace of its domain dense in this norm.
The adjoint has domain
\[
 \mathcal D(S^*)=\{y\in H:\text{there is }z\in H\text{ with }
           \langle Sx,y\rangle=\langle x,z\rangle\text{ for all }x\in D\},
 \qquad S^*y=z.
\]
Density makes $z$ unique.  Passing to limits in this identity shows that
the adjoint is closed.  If this adjoint domain is dense, the preceding
sequence test proves closability: a proposed $y$ pairs to zero with every
adjoint-domain vector.  A symmetric operator satisfies $S\subseteq S^*$;
a self-adjoint operator satisfies equality, including its domain.

A representation on $D$ is an algebra homomorphism into linear maps
$D\to D$.  It is a $*$-representation when
$\langle\pi(a)x,y\rangle=\langle x,\pi(a^*)y\rangle$ for $x,y\in D$.
Thus the algebraic domain must be invariant before the adjoint identity
can be used in repeated products.  We now construct such a representation
from a positive functional; this is the nonunital GNS construction
\cite{Segal1947}.

Let $\mathfrak A$ be a complex $*$-algebra, with no norm or unit assumed,
and let $W:\mathfrak A\to\C$ be positive:
\[
 W(a^*a)\geq0\qquad(a\in\mathfrak A).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Nonunital algebraic GNS]
\label{thm:gns}
Put
\[
 \langle a,b\rangle_W=W(b^*a),
 \qquad
 \cN_W=\{a:W(a^*a)=0\}.
\]
Then $\cN_W$ is a left ideal, meaning a linear subspace preserved by
left multiplication by every algebra element, and
\begin{equation}
 D_W=\mathfrak A/\cN_W,
 \qquad
 \pi_W(a)[b]=[ab],
 \label{eq:gns-action}
\end{equation}
is a $*$-representation on a pre-Hilbert space.  Regard $D_W$ as a
common invariant dense domain in its Hilbert completion $\mathcal H_W$.
For every $a\in\mathfrak A$,
\begin{equation}
 \pi_W(a^*)\subseteq\pi_W(a)^*,
 \label{eq:adjoint-inclusion}
\end{equation}
so $\pi_W(a)$ is closable.  It extends boundedly to $\mathcal H_W$ if
and only if there is a constant $C_a$ such that
\begin{equation}
 W(b^*a^*ab)\leq C_a^2W(b^*b)
 \qquad(b\in\mathfrak A).
 \label{eq:multiplier-bound}
\end{equation}
The least such $C_a$ is the norm of the extension.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Positivity and polarization make $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_W$ Hermitian and
give Cauchy--Schwarz.  If $b\in\cN_W$, then
\[
 W((ab)^*ab)=\langle a^*ab,b\rangle_W=0,
\]
so $\cN_W$ is a left ideal and \eqref{eq:gns-action} is well defined.
For classes in $D_W$,
\[
 \langle\pi_W(a)[b],[c]\rangle_W
 =W(c^*ab)
 =\langle[b],\pi_W(a^*)[c]\rangle_W.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:adjoint-inclusion}.  The adjoint domain contains
the dense subspace $D_W$, so $\pi_W(a)$ is closable.  Finally,
\[
 \|\pi_W(a)[b]\|^2=W(b^*a^*ab),
\]
so the bounded-operator criterion is exactly
\eqref{eq:multiplier-bound}.
\end{proof}

For $a=a^*$, the operator $\pi_W(a)$ is symmetric and closable.
The following example determines whether this conclusion can be strengthened
without another hypothesis on the common domain.

\begin{example}[A GNS multiplier without a self-adjoint extension]
\label{ex:gns-half-line}
For a densely defined symmetric operator $S$, its deficiency indices are
$n_\pm(S)=\dim\ker(S^*\mp iI)$.  Equality of these dimensions is
necessary for a self-adjoint extension, as the following argument shows.  For a closed symmetric operator
$S$, expansion gives
$\|(S\pm i)x\|^2=\|Sx\|^2+\|x\|^2$.
Consequently both ranges are closed, and
\[
 V:(S+i)x\longmapsto(S-i)x
\]
is an isometry from $\operatorname{ran}(S+i)$ onto
$\operatorname{ran}(S-i)$.  Their orthogonal complements are respectively
$\ker(S^*-i)$ and $\ker(S^*+i)$, by the definition of the adjoint.
If a self-adjoint $T$ extends $S$, each $T\pm i$ has closed dense range:
its orthogonal complement is $\ker(T\mp i)=0$ by the norm identity.
Lemma~\ref{lem:weil-orthogonal-decomposition} therefore makes each range
all of $H$.
Thus $(T-i)(T+i)^{-1}$ is a unitary extension of $V$.
Its restrictions to the two range complements must be unitary too, so
these complements must have the same dimension.

Take
\[
 P=-i\frac{d}{dx},\qquad \mathcal D(P)=C_c^\infty(0,\infty)
 \subset L^2(0,\infty).
\]
Here $C_c^\infty(0,\infty)$ consists of smooth functions with compact
support inside the half-line.  It is dense in $L^2$: truncate the support
and size of an $L^2$ function, approximate by step functions, and replace interval endpoints
by smooth ramps on sets of arbitrarily small length.  The operator is
symmetric by integration by parts, and hence closable by the dense
adjoint-domain criterion.  A locally integrable function $v$ is the weak
derivative of $u$ when
$\int u\varphi'=-\int v\varphi$ for every
$\varphi\in C_c^\infty(0,\infty)$.  Its full adjoint domain is
\[
 \mathcal D(P^*)=\{u\in L^2(0,\infty):u'
                  \text{ exists weakly and belongs to }L^2(0,\infty)\},
 \qquad P^*u=-iu'.
\]
Indeed, testing the adjoint identity against $C_c^\infty(0,\infty)$
is exactly the definition of the displayed weak derivative; conversely
that weak identity supplies the required Hilbert adjoint pairing.
The equations $(P^*-i)u=0$ and $(P^*+i)u=0$ therefore say weakly
that $u'=-u$ and $u'=u$.
Multiplication by the integrating factors $e^x$ and $e^{-x}$ gives a weak
zero derivative.  A locally integrable function with weak zero derivative
is constant: every compact test function of integral zero is the derivative
of another compact test function, so the resulting distribution depends
only on the integral of its test.  The solutions are therefore $ce^{-x}$
and $ce^x$, respectively.  Only the first belongs to $L^2(0,\infty)$.
Passing to the closure does not change the adjoint, since its defining
identity passes through graph limits.  Its two range complements thus
have dimensions one and zero, which excludes a self-adjoint extension.

This operator is itself a GNS multiplier.  Put $D=\mathcal D(P)$ and use
on $L^2(0,\infty)$ the inner product linear in its first argument.
For $f,g\in D$, define the rank-one operator
\[
 R_{f,g}x=\langle x,g\rangle f\qquad(x\in D).
\]
Let $\mathfrak A_D$ be the unital algebra of operators $D\to D$
generated by $P$ and all $R_{f,g}$.  Every generator preserves $D$;
integration by parts and the inner product identity give adjoints on $D$
by $I^*=I$, $P^*=P$, and $R_{f,g}^*=R_{g,f}$.  For a finite linear combination of
words, conjugate the coefficients and reverse each word with these
adjoints.  The resulting operator $B^*$ satisfies
$\langle Bx,y\rangle=\langle x,B^*y\rangle$ for $x,y\in D$.
If two expressions define the same operator $B$, this identity and density
of $D$ make their adjoint operators equal.  Thus the involution is
well-defined on $\mathfrak A_D$, and is the restriction of the Hilbert
adjoint to $D$.  It makes $\mathfrak A_D$ a $*$-algebra with invariant
domain $D$.

Choose $\Omega\in D$ with $\|\Omega\|=1$ and put
$W(B)=\langle B\Omega,\Omega\rangle$ for $B\in\mathfrak A_D$.
Then
\[
 W(B^*B)=\|B\Omega\|^2,\qquad
 \mathcal N_W=\{B:B\Omega=0\}.
\]
The map $U_0:[B]\mapsto B\Omega$ from its GNS quotient to $D$ is an
isometry, since
$W(C^*B)=\langle B\Omega,C\Omega\rangle$.
It is onto: $R_{f,\Omega}\Omega=f$ for every $f\in D$.
Its Hilbert completion is therefore all of $L^2(0,\infty)$, and
\[
 U_0\pi_W(C)[B]=CB\Omega=C U_0[B]
 \qquad(B,C\in\mathfrak A_D).
\]
In particular, the symmetric algebra element $P$ acts in this GNS
representation as exactly the operator $P$ with domain $D$.
The domain is invariant under the entire algebra and is a core for
$\overline P$ by the definition of graph closure.  The preceding
range-complement calculation gives $n_+(\overline P)=1$ and
$n_-(\overline P)=0$.  This symmetric GNS multiplier therefore has no
self-adjoint extension.
\end{example}

Products of closures require a separate common-domain construction.
We give that construction in Section~\ref{sec:graph-domain}.

\begin{example}[An unbounded positive GNS multiplier]
Let $\mathfrak A=\C[x]$, let $x^*=x$, and set
\[
 W(p)=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\R}p(t)e^{-t^2}\,dt.
\]
Then $W$ is positive, but multiplication by $x$ is unbounded, since
\[
 \frac{\|\pi_W(x)[x^n]\|^2}{\|[x^n]\|^2}
 =\frac{\int_{\R}t^{2n+2}e^{-t^2}\,dt}
        {\int_{\R}t^{2n}e^{-t^2}\,dt}
 =n+\frac12.
\]
The last equality follows by integrating the derivative of
$t^{2n+1}e^{-t^2}$; its boundary values vanish.
\end{example}

\section{Closed forms and the common invariant domain}
\label{sec:graph-domain}

For a linear operator $S$ on a dense subspace $D\subset H$, the quadratic
form $q(x)=\|Sx\|^2$ is nonnegative.  Its form norm is
$(\|x\|^2+q(x))^{1/2}$.  Such a form is closable if $x_n\to0$ in $H$ and
$q(x_n-x_m)\to0$ imply $q(x_n)\to0$; the implication follows from
closability of $S$.  A closed form has a complete form norm, and the closure
is obtained by completing this norm and identifying the Hilbert limits.
A finite form value permits one operator application.  Repeated products
require control of every algebra element.

For sequence examples, $\ell^2(\mathbb N,w)$ consists of complex sequences
with $\sum_{n\geq1}w_n|x_n|^2<\infty$, for strictly positive weights $w_n$.
Its inner product is $\sum_n w_nx_n\bar y_n$.  Finite sequences, denoted
$c_{00}(\mathbb N)$, are dense by coordinate truncation; $e_n$ denotes the
$n$th coordinate vector.  Unspecified weights are one.
The distinction between a single form and all products already occurs for
a diagonal operator.

\begin{example}[A maximal form domain need not be invariant]
\label{ex:maximal-domain}
On $\ell^2(\mathbb N)$, let $Ne_n=ne_n$, initially on $c_{00}(\mathbb N)$.
Its closure and the closed form $q(v)=\|Nv\|^2$ have domain
\[
 V=\left\{v:\sum_{n\geq1}n^2|v_n|^2<\infty\right\}.
\]
Coordinate convergence proves that the maximal diagonal operator is closed.
Finite coordinate truncations approximate every vector in its graph norm,
so they prove the asserted closure.  The vector $v_n=n^{-2}$ belongs to $V$,
but $Nv\notin V$, since $\sum n^4|v_n|^2=\infty$.
For the polynomial algebra generated by $N$, the common domain is instead
\begin{equation}
 V_\infty=\bigcap_{k\geq0}
 \left\{v:\sum_{n\geq1}n^{2k}|v_n|^2<\infty\right\}.
 \label{eq:all-moments}
\end{equation}
Multiplication by each polynomial preserves $V_\infty$.
Truncations converge in every displayed seminorm.
\end{example}

Return to Theorem~\ref{thm:gns}.  Write $D=D_W$, $H=\mathcal H_W$,
and $A_a=\overline{\pi_W(a)}$ for $a\in\mathfrak A$.
The graph topology on $D$ is generated by $\|\xi\|$ and
$\|\pi_W(a)\xi\|$, for all $a\in\mathfrak A$.
A neighborhood bounds finitely many of these seminorms, so the topology
is locally convex.  A representation is called closed when its domain is
complete for this topology.  Completion can require nets, namely families
indexed by a directed set, when there is no countable family of controlling
seminorms.  A net is Cauchy here when it is Cauchy in every graph seminorm.

\begin{theorem}[The exact common graph domain]
\label{thm:graph-domain}
The completion of $D$ in its graph topology identifies with
\begin{equation}
 D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}=\bigcap_{a\in\mathfrak A}\mathcal D(A_a)
 \subset H.
 \label{eq:common-domain}
\end{equation}
On this domain, the maps $\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)=A_a|_{D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}}$
form an invariant $*$-representation.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(b)\xi
 =\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(ab)\xi,
 \qquad
 \langle\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)\xi,\eta\rangle
 =\langle\xi,\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a^*)\eta\rangle.
 \label{eq:closed-products}
\end{equation}
Each action is continuous in the graph topology.
For each $a$, the nonnegative form
\[
 q_a([b])=W(b^*a^*ab)=\|\pi_W(a)[b]\|^2
\]
is closable on $H$.  Its closure is $\overline q_a(\xi)=\|A_a\xi\|^2$
on $\mathcal D(A_a)$.  Thus \eqref{eq:common-domain} is exactly the
intersection of these closed form domains.
Each $\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)$ has closure $A_a$.  This is the
minimal extension of $\pi_W$ whose common graph topology is complete.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A graph Cauchy net $(\xi_i)$ has a Hilbert limit $\xi$ and limits
$\eta_a=\lim_i\pi_W(a)\xi_i$.  Closedness of $A_a$ gives
$\xi\in\mathcal D(A_a)$ and $A_a\xi=\eta_a$.
If $\xi=0$, closability gives $\eta_a=0$ for every $a$.
Hence the graph completion injects into the right side of
\eqref{eq:common-domain}.

To prove the reverse inclusion, fix $\xi$ in that intersection and a finite
set $F\subset\mathfrak A$.  Set $c=\sum_{a\in F}a^*a$.
For $u\in D$, the adjoint identity gives the estimate
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{a\in F}\|\pi_W(a)u\|^2
 =\langle\pi_W(c)u,u\rangle
 \leq\|\pi_W(c)u\|\,\|u\|.
 \label{eq:graph-control}
\end{equation}
Since $\xi\in\mathcal D(A_c)$, choose $u_j\in D$ with
$u_j\to\xi$ and $\pi_W(c)u_j\to A_c\xi$.
Apply \eqref{eq:graph-control} to $u_j-u_k$.
Every $\pi_W(a)u_j$, $a\in F$, is Cauchy.  Closedness identifies its
limit with $A_a\xi$.  Thus $D$ approximates $\xi$ in any prescribed
finite collection of graph seminorms.
Directing these approximations by finite sets and positive tolerances
produces one net converging in all graph seminorms.

Let this net be $(\xi_i)$ and fix $b\in\mathfrak A$.
Then $\pi_W(b)\xi_i\to A_b\xi$ and
$\pi_W(a)\pi_W(b)\xi_i=\pi_W(ab)\xi_i\to A_{ab}\xi$ for every $a$.
Closedness gives $A_b\xi\in D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}$ and
$A_aA_b\xi=A_{ab}\xi$.
Linearity follows by the same approximation.
Approximating both vectors in the algebraic adjoint identity proves
the second equality in \eqref{eq:closed-products}.
Continuity follows from
$\|A_aA_b\xi\|=\|A_{ab}\xi\|$ and $\|A_b\xi\|$ itself.

The form norm for $q_a$ is
$(\|\xi\|^2+\|\pi_W(a)\xi\|^2)^{1/2}$.
Its completion is the graph of $A_a$, which proves the form assertion.
Finally $\pi_W(a)\subseteq\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)\subseteq A_a$,
so taking closures proves the last statement.
Any closed $*$-representation extending $\pi_W$ contains the limits of
these graph Cauchy nets and agrees with their multiplier limits.
Thus it extends $\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}$, which proves minimality.
\end{proof}

The word ``closed'' refers to completeness in the common graph topology.
It does not assert that each restriction to
$D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}$ is a closed operator or is self-adjoint.
Example~\ref{ex:maximal-domain} separates the common domain from the
maximal domain of one multiplier.  The argument uses only an invariant
$*$-representation, so the same construction applies to such a representation
without a specified functional.  This is the common-domain notion of
closed representation in \cite[Definition~2.4 and Lemma~2.6]{Powers1971}.

\section{Norm and order in a complete involutive algebra}
\label{sec:cstar-prerequisites}

Bounded target algebras impose estimates which an arbitrary algebraic
representation need not satisfy.  We derive those estimates from the norm
before using them in transfer or unitization.
A Banach space is a complete normed vector space.
A $C^*$-algebra is a complex $*$-algebra complete in a norm satisfying
$\|ab\|\leq\|a\|\|b\|$ and $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$.
The involution is isometric: the latter identity first gives
$\|a\|\leq\|a^*\|$, and application to $a^*$ gives equality.
No representation by bounded operators is assumed in this definition.

\begin{lemma}[Adjoining a unit with its norm]
\label{lem:cstar-adjoin}
For a nonunital $C^*$-algebra $A$, the algebra $A^+=A\oplus\C1$ with
\[
 (a+\lambda1)(b+\mu1)=ab+\lambda b+\mu a+\lambda\mu1,
 \qquad (a+\lambda1)^*=a^*+\bar\lambda1
\]
has a complete $C^*$-norm extending the norm of $A$.
If $A\ne\{0\}$, that norm is
\[
 \|a+\lambda1\|_+=\sup_{\|b\|\leq1}\|ab+\lambda b\|.
\]
For $A=\{0\}$ take $A^+=\C$ with the absolute value.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $A=\{0\}$ the assertion is the usual $C^*$-norm on $\C$.
Assume henceforth that $A\ne\{0\}$.
Write $L_xb=xb$ for $x\in A^+$ and $b\in A$.
These are bounded maps on the Banach space $A$.
For $a\in A$, the choice $b=a^*/\|a\|$ shows
$\|L_a\|=\|a\|$ when $a\ne0$.
If $L_{a+\lambda1}=0$ with $\lambda\ne0$, then $-a/\lambda$ is a left
identity.  Its adjoint is a right identity; multiplying the two identities
shows that they agree and give a unit in $A$, a contradiction.
Thus $x\mapsto L_x$ is injective and the displayed expression is a norm.

For $b\in A$,
\[
 \|xb\|^2=\|b^*x^*xb\|
 \leq\|b\|\|x^*xb\|\leq\|L_{x^*x}\|\|b\|^2.
\]
Taking suprema gives
$\|L_x\|^2\leq\|L_{x^*x}\|\leq\|L_{x^*}\|\|L_x\|$.
Applying this also to $x^*$ proves equality of the two adjoint norms and
then the $C^*$-identity.

To check completeness directly, one has $|\lambda|\leq\|L_{a+\lambda1}\|$.
Otherwise $L_a=L_{a+\lambda1}-\lambda I$ would be invertible by the
convergent geometric series.  Choose $e\in A$ with $ae=a$.
Then $L_aL_e=L_a$ forces $L_e=I$, again giving a unit in $A$.
It follows that
\[
 \|a+\lambda1\|_+\leq\|a\|+|\lambda|
 \leq3\|a+\lambda1\|_+.
\]
A Cauchy sequence therefore has Cauchy components in $A$ and $\C$, whose
limits supply its limit in $A^+$.
\end{proof}

For an element $h$ of a nonzero unital Banach algebra, its spectrum $\sigma(h)$
is the set of complex $\lambda$ for which $\lambda1-h$ has no two-sided
inverse.  In a nonunital $C^*$-algebra we use the unitization just constructed;
in particular, the spectrum for $A=\{0\}$ is computed in $A^+=\C$.
In every one of these nonzero ambient algebras, $\lambda1$ is invertible
exactly when $\lambda\ne0$: its inverse is $\lambda^{-1}1$, while zero
cannot have an inverse because $1\ne0$.  Thus $\sigma(0)=\{0\}$.
A self-adjoint element is called positive when its spectrum is contained in
$[0,\infty)$.  The next two lemmas show that this is precisely the algebraic
notion of a square $c^*c$.

\begin{lemma}[The calculus of a self-adjoint element]
\label{lem:cstar-calculus}
For a self-adjoint $h$ in a $C^*$-algebra, the spectrum is real and contained
in $[-\|h\|,\|h\|]$.  Every continuous complex function $f$ on this interval
has a well-defined value $f(h)$ obtained by norm limits of polynomials.
The construction preserves sums, products, constants, and conjugation, and
\[
 \|f(h)\|=\max_{t\in\sigma(h)}|f(t)|.
\]
If $A$ is nonunital, $h\in A$, and $f(0)=0$, then $f(h)\in A$.
A continuous function specified on a smaller interval containing the spectrum
may be extended constantly past its endpoints.  Its value is independent
of that extension by the norm identity.
Every positive element has a positive square root in the same algebra.
Every self-adjoint element is a difference $h=h_+-h_-$ of positive elements
with $h_+h_-=0$ and $\|h_\pm\|\leq\|h\|$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $h=0$, take $f(h)=f(0)1$.  We may therefore assume $\|h\|>0$.
We supply the spectral facts used in the polynomial construction.
In a nonzero unital Banach algebra, the series
$(\lambda1-x)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq0}\lambda^{-n-1}x^n$ converges for
$|\lambda|>\|x\|$; the same series about any inverse proves openness of the
invertible set and holomorphy of the resolvent.
Thus the spectrum is compact.  It is nonempty: an everywhere-defined
resolvent would be an entire bounded Banach-valued function tending to zero
at infinity.  The circle derivative estimate makes such a function constant,
hence zero, contradicting its inverse identity.
The circle formulas of Lemma~\ref{lem:cauchy} hold for Banach-valued
functions by the same uniform Riemann sums and norm estimates; completeness
supplies their limits.

Polynomial factorization shows $\sigma(p(x))=p(\sigma(x))$.
Indeed, the factors of $p(x)-\lambda1$ commute, and a product of commuting
elements is invertible exactly when all its factors are invertible.
Existence of complex roots for a nonconstant polynomial follows from the
same circle estimate: otherwise its reciprocal would be bounded entire and
constant.  Repeated division gives the factorization used here.
If $r(x)=\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}$ and $R>r(x)$, deformation
of a circle from radius greater than $\|x\|$ to radius $R$ gives
\[
 x^n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\lambda|=R}
           \lambda^n(\lambda1-x)^{-1}\,d\lambda.
\]
The norm estimate and the polynomial spectral identity imply
$r(x)=\lim_n\|x^n\|^{1/n}$.
For a normal element $x$, meaning $x^*x=xx^*$, the $C^*$-identity gives
$\|x^2\|=\|x\|^2$.  Iteration at powers $2^n$ therefore gives
$r(x)=\|x\|$.

For self-adjoint $h$, the exponential series gives
$(e^{ith})^*e^{ith}=1$, so $\|e^{ith}\|=1$ for real $t$.
When $\operatorname{Im}\lambda>0$, the norm-convergent integral
\[
 i\int_0^\infty e^{-it(h-\lambda1)}\,dt
\]
is an inverse of $h-\lambda1$, by integration of its derivative.
Conjugation gives an inverse in the lower half-plane.  Hence the spectrum
of $h$ is real.  For any polynomial $p$, the element $p(h)$ is normal;
thus $\|p(h)\|=\max_{\sigma(h)}|p|$.

Continuous functions on a compact real interval admit uniform polynomial
approximation.  An explicit proof on $[0,1]$ uses
$B_nf(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n f(k/n)\binom nk t^k(1-t)^{n-k}$.
The binomial coefficients with these weights have total mass one, mean
$t$, and variance $t(1-t)/n$.  Splitting the sum into $|k/n-t|<\delta$
and its complement bounds the approximation error by
\[
 \sup_{|u-v|<\delta}|f(u)-f(v)|+
       \frac{2\|f\|_\infty}{4n\delta^2}.
\]
Uniform continuity, followed by $n\to\infty$, proves the assertion;
an affine change of variable treats the stated interval.
The polynomial norm identity now defines $f(h)$ by completion and proves
all its displayed properties.  Subtracting the constant term from the
approximating polynomials proves the assertion when $f(0)=0$.

If $h$ is positive, take $f(t)=\sqrt{\max(t,0)}$ on the full interval.
Then $f(h)^2=h$, since the difference vanishes on $\sigma(h)$.
The element $f(h)$ is itself positive: write it as $q(h)^2$ with
$q(t)=(\max(t,0))^{1/4}$, and use the real spectrum of the self-adjoint
$q(h)$ and the polynomial spectral identity.
Taking $f_\pm(t)=\max(\pm t,0)$ gives the final decomposition and norm
bounds by the same argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Positive squares and order bounds]
\label{lem:cstar-order}
The positive elements form a norm-closed cone whose intersection with its
negative is zero.  They are exactly the elements $c^*c$.
Write $a\leq b$ when $b-a$ is positive.  Conjugation by any element preserves
this order, and
\[
 0\leq a\leq b\ \Longrightarrow\ \|a\|\leq\|b\|,
 \qquad c^*c\leq\|c\|^2 1.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For self-adjoint $h$ and $R>0$, the condition
$\|h-R1\|\leq R$ says exactly $\sigma(h)\subset[0,2R]$.
This criterion and the triangle inequality show that positive elements
are closed under addition: use radii $\|a\|$ and $\|b\|$ for two nonzero
positive elements.  Scalar multiplication by nonnegative reals preserves
positivity.  A norm-convergent sequence of positive elements has a common
bounding radius, so the cone is closed.  If both $h$ and $-h$ are positive,
the spectrum is $\{0\}$ and the normal-element norm identity gives $h=0$.

For every $x$, the nonzero spectra of $x^*x$ and $xx^*$ coincide.
This follows by scaling the identity
$(1-uv)^{-1}=1+u(1-vu)^{-1}v$ whenever the latter inverse exists.
Suppose first that $-x^*x$ is positive.  Writing $x=u+iv$ with self-adjoint
$u,v$ gives
\[
 xx^*=2u^2+2v^2-x^*x\geq0.
\]
The common nonzero spectrum must then be both positive and negative, hence
empty.  Thus $\|x\|^2=\|x^*x\|=0$.
For a general $x$, put $h=x^*x$, take its negative part $h_-$ from
Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-calculus}, and set $y=xh_-^{1/2}$.
Commutation in the calculus gives $y^*y=hh_-=-h_-^2$.
The preceding argument forces $y=0$, hence $h_-=0$ and $x^*x\geq0$.
Conversely, a positive element is the square of its self-adjoint square
root, proving the characterization.

If $h=d^*d\geq0$, then $c^*hc=(dc)^*(dc)\geq0$.
The spectral bound for $c^*c$ is $[0,\|c\|^2]$, which proves the second
order estimate.  Finally $b\leq\|b\|1$ implies
$a\leq\|b\|1$ when $0\leq a\leq b$.
The spectra of $a$ and $\|b\|1-a$ are nonnegative, so
$\sigma(a)\subset[0,\|b\|]$ and $\|a\|\leq\|b\|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A positive approximate identity]
\label{lem:cstar-approximate}
Every $C^*$-algebra $A$ has a net $(e_i)$ of positive elements with
$\|e_i\|\leq1$ and $e_i a\to a$, $a e_i\to a$ for every $a\in A$.
Such a net is called a positive contractive two-sided approximate identity.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a unital algebra take the constant net $1$; the zero algebra is
immediate.  Suppose now that $A$ is nonunital.  For a finite set
$F\subset A$ and $\epsilon>0$, define in the unitization
\[
 t_F=\sum_{a\in F}(a^*a+aa^*),\qquad
 e_{F,\epsilon}=t_F(t_F+\epsilon1)^{-1}.
\]
The calculus shows $e_{F,\epsilon}\in A$ and
$0\leq e_{F,\epsilon}\leq1$, since $t/(t+\epsilon)$ lies in $[0,1]$
for $t\geq0$ and vanishes at zero.
For $a\in F$, both $a^*a$ and $aa^*$ are bounded above by $t_F$.
Writing $e=e_{F,\epsilon}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-order} gives
\[
 \|a(1-e)\|^2\leq\|(1-e)t_F(1-e)\|
 \leq\sup_{t\geq0}\frac{\epsilon^2 t}{(t+\epsilon)^2}
 =\frac\epsilon4.
\]
Apply the same bound to $a^*$ to obtain $\|(1-e)a\|^2\leq\epsilon/4$.
Direct finite sets by inclusion and positive $\epsilon$ by decreasing size.
Every fixed $a$ then satisfies both limits.
\end{proof}


\section{Dagger transfer and completion}

\begin{theorem}[Dagger transfer]
\label{thm:dagger}
Let $T:\mathfrak A\to\mathfrak B$ be a $*$-homomorphism of complex
$*$-algebras, let $\omega:\mathfrak B\to\C$ be positive, and put
$W=\omega\circ T$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \langle a,c\rangle_W
 =\langle T(a),T(c)\rangle_\omega,
 \qquad
 \cN_W=T^{-1}(\cN_\omega).
 \label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 U_0:D_W\longrightarrow D_\omega,
 \qquad [a]\longmapsto[T(a)],
 \label{eq:gns-map}
\end{equation}
is an isometric injection and extends uniquely to an isometry
\begin{equation}
 U:\mathcal H_W\longrightarrow\mathcal H_\omega
 \label{eq:completion-map}
\end{equation}
with closed range.  On the algebraic common domain,
\begin{equation}
 U_0\pi_W(a)=\pi_\omega(T(a))U_0.
 \label{eq:algebraic-intertwining}
\end{equation}

If $\mathfrak B$ is a $C^*$-algebra, both sides of
\eqref{eq:algebraic-intertwining} extend boundedly and
\begin{equation}
 \|\pi_\omega(b)\|\leq\|b\|,
 \qquad
 \|\pi_W(a)\|\leq\|T(a)\|,
 \label{eq:cstar-bounds}
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
 U\pi_W(a)=\pi_\omega(T(a))U
 \quad\text{on }\mathcal H_W.
 \label{eq:bounded-intertwining}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $a,c\in\mathfrak A$,
\[
 W(c^*a)=\omega(T(c)^*T(a)),
\]
which proves positivity, \eqref{eq:pullback}, and the isometry
\eqref{eq:gns-map}.  Every isometry of pre-Hilbert spaces extends
uniquely to their completions; its range is closed because its domain is
complete.  Multiplicativity of $T$ gives
\eqref{eq:algebraic-intertwining}.

Suppose now that $\mathfrak B$ is a $C^*$-algebra.  Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-order}, with conjugation by $d$, gives
\[
 \omega(d^*b^*bd)\leq\|b\|^2\omega(d^*d),
\]
which proves the first estimate in \eqref{eq:cstar-bounds}.  Taking
$b=T(a)$ and $d=T(c)$ proves the second.  Continuity then extends
\eqref{eq:algebraic-intertwining} to
\eqref{eq:bounded-intertwining}.  No equality between arbitrary
unbounded closure domains is used.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Transfer of closed graph domains]
\label{prop:graph-transfer}
Use the data of Theorem~\ref{thm:dagger}, and set
$K=U\mathcal H_W\subset\mathcal H_\omega$ and $E=U_0D_W$.
On the dense subspace $E\subset K$, define
\[
 \rho(a)=\pi_\omega(T(a))|_E.
\]
Then $\rho$ is a $*$-representation, and
\begin{equation}
 U D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}=D_\rho^{\mathrm{cl}},
 \qquad
 U\pi_W^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)\xi=\rho^{\mathrm{cl}}(a)U\xi.
 \label{eq:graph-transfer}
\end{equation}
Here every closure defining $D_\rho^{\mathrm{cl}}$ is taken in $K$.
For the ambient closures on $\mathcal H_\omega$ one has
\begin{equation}
 U\mathcal D(\overline{\pi_W(a)})
 \subseteq\mathcal D(\overline{\pi_\omega(T(a))}),
 \qquad
 \overline{\pi_\omega(T(a))}U\xi
 =U\overline{\pi_W(a)}\xi.
 \label{eq:ambient-transfer}
\end{equation}
If $T$ is surjective, $U$ is unitary and
$U D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}=D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}$.
Without surjectivity this last domain equality need not hold,
even when $U$ is unitary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The algebraic intertwining identifies $\rho(a)$ with
$U\pi_W(a)U^{-1}$ on $E$.  As a map onto $K$, $U$ is unitary.
It identifies the individual graph closures and all graph seminorms.
Theorem~\ref{thm:graph-domain} therefore gives \eqref{eq:graph-transfer}.
If $\xi_j\in D_W$ approaches $\xi$ in the graph norm of
$\pi_W(a)$, its images approach $U\xi$ and have multiplier images
approaching $U\overline{\pi_W(a)}\xi$.
This proves \eqref{eq:ambient-transfer}.
For surjective $T$, one has $U_0D_W=D_\omega$ and $T(\mathfrak A)=\mathfrak B$.
Hence the Hilbert spaces and their full graph topologies agree.
\end{proof}

A concrete condition identifies the full target domain when every target
operation is controlled by those coming from the source.  This condition
has two parts: approximation must reach the target domain, and the omitted
algebra elements must impose no stronger graph topology.

\begin{proposition}[When the full graph domains agree]
\label{prop:full-graph-transfer}
Suppose the isometry $U$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:dagger} is onto.
Let $E=U_0D_W$ and $B_b=\overline{\pi_\omega(b)}$ for
$b\in\mathfrak B$.  The map $U$ identifies $D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}$ with
$D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}$, including their graph topologies, if and only if
both of the following conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $E$ is dense in $D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}$ for the full target graph
      topology;
\item for every $b\in\mathfrak B$ there are a finite set
      $F\subset\mathfrak A$ and a finite constant $C_b$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \|\pi_\omega(b)x\|
 \leq C_b\left(\|x\|^2+
          \sum_{a\in F}\|\pi_\omega(T(a))x\|^2\right)^{1/2}
 \qquad(x\in E).
 \label{eq:full-graph-control}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
In particular, surjectivity of $T$ implies both conditions.

For equality as vector sets, there is a separate exact domain criterion.
Put $C_a=\overline{\pi_\omega(T(a))|_E}$, with closure in
$\mathcal H_\omega$.  Then equality holds exactly when
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}&\subseteq\mathcal D(C_a)
       &&\quad(a\in\mathfrak A),\\
 \bigcap_{a\in\mathfrak A}\mathcal D(C_a)
       &\subseteq\mathcal D(B_b)
       &&\quad(b\in\mathfrak B).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:full-graph-domain-test}
\end{equation}
If $E$ is a core for every $B_{T(a)}$, the first inclusions are automatic
and $C_a=B_{T(a)}$.  In that case only the second family of inclusions
has to be checked.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $E$, the graph seminorms for the source are the Hilbert norm and
$x\mapsto\|\pi_\omega(T(a))x\|$.  They form a subfamily of the target
seminorms.  A seminorm is continuous for a topology generated by seminorms
exactly when it is bounded by a constant times a finite sum of them:
a neighborhood of zero gives the bound by scaling, and a sum can be
replaced by the displayed Euclidean sum after changing its constant.
Thus \eqref{eq:full-graph-control} is exactly equality of the two
graph topologies on $E$.  Under the density condition their completions
are $UD_W^{\mathrm{cl}}$ and $D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}$, respectively.
The common Hilbert limit identifies the two completions.
Conversely, an identification of the completed graph topologies restricts
to their equality on $E$, and density of the source algebraic domain
implies the stated target density.
If $T$ is onto, $E=D_\omega$ and every target seminorm already occurs
among the source seminorms.

Proposition~\ref{prop:graph-transfer} gives
$UD_W^{\mathrm{cl}}=\bigcap_a\mathcal D(C_a)$.
The target domain is $\bigcap_b\mathcal D(B_b)$.
The two displayed families of inclusions are precisely the two inclusions
between these intersections.  A core gives equality of the corresponding
individual closures, which proves the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A Hilbert isometry can miss the full target domain]
\label{ex:transfer-domain}
Let $\mathfrak A=c_{00}(\mathbb N)$ and
$\mathfrak B=\C[n]+c_{00}(\mathbb N)$, with pointwise multiplication
and conjugation.  Here $\C[n]$ denotes polynomial sequences on the
positive integers.  Let $T$ be inclusion and set
\[
 \omega(b)=\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-n}b_n,
 \qquad W=\omega|_{\mathfrak A}.
\]
All sums converge, and both functionals are positive and faithful: their
square values are zero only on the zero element, since every weight is positive.
Both GNS completions identify with
$H=\ell^2(\mathbb N,e^{-n})$, because they contain the finite sequences.
Thus $U$ is the identity on $H$.
Every $\mathfrak A$-multiplier is bounded, so $D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}=H$.
The element $n\in\mathfrak B$ acts by an unbounded multiplier.
Finite truncations give cores for every polynomial multiplier.  Therefore
\[
 D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}
 =\left\{v:\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-n}n^{2k}|v_n|^2<\infty
               \text{ for all }k\geq0\right\}.
\]
The vector $v_n=e^{n/2}/n$ belongs to $H$, but fails the condition for
$k=1$.  Thus $U D_W^{\mathrm{cl}}\nsubseteq D_\omega^{\mathrm{cl}}$.
Here $E=c_{00}(\mathbb N)$ is even a full target graph core, by
coordinate truncation.  The failed condition is
\eqref{eq:full-graph-control} for $b=n$: finitely many source multipliers
have a common finite support, whereas coordinate vectors outside that
support have target norm ratio $n$.  Only the restricted algebra
$T(\mathfrak A)$ appears in \eqref{eq:graph-transfer}.
\end{example}

For a field interpretation, algebra products represent repeated operations
on a common domain.  Theorem~\ref{thm:graph-domain} supplies this domain
after positivity has supplied the Hilbert norm.
Proposition~\ref{prop:graph-transfer} preserves it for the represented
algebra.  A vacuum vector, self-adjoint generators, locality, and a positive
energy condition require their own constructions.

\section{Positive unitization for nonunital
\texorpdfstring{$C^*$}{C-star}-algebras}

The unit missing from a convolution algebra cannot be adjoined to an
arbitrary positive functional without an additional bound.  Let $\omega$
be a positive linear functional on a nonunital $*$-algebra $\mathfrak A$.
Write
$\mathfrak A^+=\mathfrak A\oplus\C1$ and
\begin{equation}
 \omega_m(a+\lambda1)=\omega(a)+m\lambda.
 \label{eq:unit-extension}
\end{equation}
Expanding a square in this algebra gives
\[
 \omega_m((a+\lambda1)^*(a+\lambda1))
 =\omega(a^*a)+\lambda\omega(a^*)+\bar\lambda\omega(a)+m|\lambda|^2.
\]
Requiring real nonnegative values first forces $m\geq0$ and Hermitian
symmetry.  For $m>0$, minimization in $\lambda$ gives the inequality below.
For $m=0$, varying $\lambda$ forces $\omega(a)=0$ for every $a$.
Thus the exact algebraic criterion is
\begin{equation}
 \omega_m\text{ is positive}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{cases}
 m\in\R,\quad m\geq0,\\
 \omega(a^*)=\overline{\omega(a)},\\
 |\omega(a)|^2\leq m\,\omega(a^*a)&(a\in\mathfrak A).
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:unit-criterion}
\end{equation}

The same bound is necessary for a vector implementation, even when the
operators are unbounded.  Let $\pi$ be a $*$-representation on a common
invariant dense domain $D\subset H$, and let $\Omega\in D$ have finite norm.
If $\omega(a)=\langle\pi(a)\Omega,\Omega\rangle$, then the domain and
adjoint identities give
\[
 \omega(a^*a)=\langle\pi(a^*)\pi(a)\Omega,\Omega\rangle
             =\|\pi(a)\Omega\|^2.
\]
Hilbert-space Cauchy--Schwarz therefore implies
\begin{equation}
 |\omega(a)|^2\leq\|\Omega\|^2\omega(a^*a)
 \qquad(a\in\mathfrak A).
 \label{eq:vector-unit-bound}
\end{equation}
Neither cyclicity nor boundedness of the operators enters this argument.

A bounded representation is nondegenerate when
$\overline{\operatorname{span}\{\pi(a)x:a\in\mathfrak A,\ x\in H\}}=H$.
It is cyclic with vector $\Omega$ when
$\overline{\pi(\mathfrak A)\Omega}=H$.

\begin{theorem}[The nonunital $C^*$ case]
\label{thm:cstar-unitization}
Let $\mathfrak A$ be a nonunital $C^*$-algebra and let
$\omega:\mathfrak A\to\C$ be positive.  Then $\omega$ is Hermitian and
bounded, and the functional \eqref{eq:unit-extension} on the unitization
constructed in Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-adjoin} is positive exactly when
\begin{equation}
 m\in\R,\qquad m\geq\|\omega\|.
 \label{eq:minimal-mass}
\end{equation}
Let $(e_i)$ be any positive contractive two-sided approximate identity.
Write $H_m$ for the GNS completion of $\omega_m$ and
$\Omega_m=[1]\in H_m$, so $\|\Omega_m\|^2=m$.
For the minimal extension $m=\|\omega\|$, the natural isometry from
$\mathcal H_\omega$ onto the closed span of the classes $[a]$ in $H_m$
is onto $H_m$.  Under this identification the vector
$\Omega=\Omega_m$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 [e_i]\longrightarrow\Omega,
 \label{eq:approx-vector}
\end{equation}
and the restricted representation of $\mathfrak A$ is bounded,
nondegenerate, and cyclic:
\begin{equation}
 \omega(a)=\langle\pi_\omega(a)\Omega,\Omega\rangle,
 \qquad
 \overline{\pi_\omega(\mathfrak A)\Omega}=\mathcal H_\omega.
 \label{eq:cyclic-vector}
\end{equation}
For $m>\|\omega\|$, the vector $[1]$ of that larger extension is not the
limit in \eqref{eq:approx-vector}.  More precisely,
$H_m$ is the orthogonal sum of $\mathcal H_\omega$ and a one-dimensional
space on which $\mathfrak A$ acts by zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every self-adjoint element is the difference of two positive elements,
so positivity makes $\omega$ Hermitian.  If $\omega$ were unbounded, its
values would be unbounded on the positive unit ball: otherwise the
decomposition of an arbitrary element into four positive parts would
bound it.  One could then choose $a_n\geq0$ with
\[
 \|a_n\|\leq2^{-n},\qquad \omega(a_n)=1.
\]
The norm-convergent positive sum $a=\sum_na_n$ would satisfy
$\omega(a)\geq\sum_{n=1}^N\omega(a_n)=N$ for every $N$, a
contradiction.  Hence $\omega$ is bounded.

Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-approximate} constructs a positive contractive
approximate identity.  Cauchy--Schwarz and
$e_i a\to a$ give
\begin{equation}
 |\omega(a)|^2
 =\lim_i|\omega(e_i a)|^2
 \leq\|\omega\|\,\omega(a^*a).
 \label{eq:hilbert-bound}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:unit-criterion} and \eqref{eq:hilbert-bound} prove
positivity for $m=\|\omega\|$; adding $(m-\|\omega\|)$ times the
positive quotient functional $a+\lambda1\mapsto\lambda$, whose value
on a square is $|\lambda|^2$, proves
sufficiency for every larger $m$.  For a positive functional $\eta$ on a
unital $C^*$-algebra, Cauchy--Schwarz and Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-order} give
\[
 |\eta(a)|^2\leq\eta(1)\eta(a^*a)
             \leq\eta(1)^2\|a\|^2.
\]
Evaluation at $1$ therefore gives $\|\eta\|=\eta(1)$.
Applying this to $\eta=\omega_m$ and restricting yields $\|\omega\|\leq m$.

It remains to identify the vector $[1]$.  If $\|\omega\|>0$, choose
$\|a\|\leq1$ with $|\omega(a)|$ arbitrarily close to $\|\omega\|$.
Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$|\omega(e_i a)|^2\leq\omega(e_i^2)\omega(a^*a)
\leq\omega(e_i^2)\|\omega\|$.
Since $e_i a\to a$, this forces the lower limit of $\omega(e_i^2)$ to be
at least $\|\omega\|$.  The upper bound is $\|\omega\|\|e_i^2\|\leq\|\omega\|$.
Therefore $\omega(e_i^2)\to\|\omega\|$.
Since $0\leq e_i^2\leq e_i\leq1$ in the unitization, also
$\omega(e_i)\to\|\omega\|$.  Therefore
\[
 \|[1]-[e_i]\|^2
 =\|\omega\|-2\omega(e_i)+\omega(e_i^2)\longrightarrow0,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:approx-vector}; the zero functional is immediate.
The classes $[a]+\lambda[1]$ are dense in $H_m$, so this convergence
also proves that the original GNS completion fills $H_m$ at minimal mass.
Moreover $\pi_\omega(a)\Omega=[a]$, proving cyclicity, and
$\pi_\omega(e_i)[a]=[e_i a]\to[a]$, proving nondegeneracy.  The
$C^*$-estimate in \eqref{eq:cstar-bounds} proves boundedness.  If
$m>\|\omega\|$, the same computation instead gives
\[
 \lim_i\|[1]-[e_i]\|^2=m-\|\omega\|>0.
\]
The classes $[e_i]$ still converge to a vector $\Omega_0$ in the closed
span of $[\mathfrak A]$, because their mutual inner products are those
of the original GNS space.  For $a\in\mathfrak A$, continuity gives
$\langle[a],[1]-\Omega_0\rangle
=\omega(a)-\lim_i\omega(e_i a)=0$.
Also $\pi_{\omega_m}(a)([1]-\Omega_0)
=[a]-\lim_i[ae_i]=0$.
The orthogonal difference has squared norm $m-\|\omega\|$, and the
classes $[a]+\lambda[1]$ are dense.  This proves the asserted orthogonal
sum and zero action.
\end{proof}

For $\mathfrak A=\{0\}$, the only functional is $\omega=0$, and
$\mathfrak A^+=\C$.  The extension $\omega_m(\lambda)=m\lambda$
is positive exactly when $m\geq0$.
At minimal mass $m=0$, its GNS Hilbert space and $\Omega$ are both zero,
and the constant approximate identity $e_i=0$ gives $[e_i]=\Omega$.
For $m>0$, the extended GNS space is one-dimensional with
$\|\Omega\|^2=m$; the original zero algebra acts as zero and
$[e_i]=0$ cannot converge to $\Omega$.

\begin{example}[Positive unitization can fail algebraically]
Let $\mathfrak A=c_{00}(\mathbb N)$ with pointwise multiplication and
conjugation, and define
\[
 \omega(a)=\sum_{n\geq1}n a_n.
\]
This is a Hermitian positive functional.  If a positive extension
$\omega_m$ existed, Cauchy--Schwarz in the unitization, applied to the
$n$th point mass $e_n$, would give
\[
 n^2=|\omega_m(e_n)|^2
 \leq\omega_m(1)\omega_m(e_n^*e_n)=mn
\]
for every $n$, which is impossible for finite $m$.
\end{example}


\section{Finite mass and positive Gaussian subspaces}
\label{sec:weil-mass}

The unit bound compares a squared functional value with its
convolution-square value.  For the full Weil functional, their ratio is
already unbounded on the compact convolution subalgebra
\[
 \mathscr D=C_c^\infty(\R)\subset\cA_\zeta.
\]
Here $C_c^\infty(\R)$ consists of smooth functions with compact support.
It inherits the convolution, involution and Fourier convention in
\eqref{eq:operations}--\eqref{eq:fourier}.
Small supports eliminate every prime-power evaluation in the full
functional.  The logarithmic gamma term then determines the divergence.
This argument requires no hypothesis about the zeros.

\subsection{Concentrating compact tests}

\begin{lemma}[An integrable gamma remainder]
\label{lem:gamma-integrable-remainder}
Put
\[
 k_\infty(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi,
 \qquad
 r(t)=k_\infty(t)-\log\frac{|t|}{2\pi}\quad(t\ne0).
\]
Then $r\in L^1(\R)$, with $r(t)=O(t^{-2})$ as $|t|\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For each fixed $0<\delta<\pi$, the digamma expansion
\cite[\S5.11(i), equation (5.11.2)]{DLMF} gives
\[
 \psi(z)=\log z-\frac1{2z}+O(|z|^{-2})
 \qquad(|z|\to\infty,\ |\arg z|\leq\pi-\delta),
\]
where $\log z$ is the principal logarithm.
The line $z=1/4+it/2$ lies in the sector with $\delta=\pi/2$.
Along this line,
\[
 \log|z|-\log(|t|/2)
   =\frac12\log\left(1+\frac1{4t^2}\right)=O(t^{-2}),
 \qquad \operatorname{Re}\frac1z=O(t^{-2}).
\]
Taking real parts proves the asserted tail estimate.
The function $k_\infty$ is smooth near zero.  Thus the only singularity
of $r$ there is an integrable logarithm, proving $r\in L^1(\R)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[No finite unit mass on compact tests]
\label{thm:weil-compact-mass}
Choose a real nonnegative $\eta\in C_c^\infty(-1,1)$ with
$\int_\R\eta(u)\,du=1$.  For $\epsilon>0$, put
\begin{equation}
 \eta_\epsilon(u)=\epsilon^{-1}\eta(u/\epsilon),\qquad
 a_\epsilon=\eta_\epsilon^*\star\eta_\epsilon,\qquad
 q(t)=|\widehat\eta(t)|^2.
 \label{eq:compact-concentrating-tests}
\end{equation}
For $j=1,2$, define the finite constants
\[
 Q_j=\int_\R q(t)^j\,dt>0,\qquad
 C_j=\int_\R q(t)^j\log\frac{|t|}{2\pi}\,dt.
\]
The full functional satisfies, as $\epsilon\downarrow0$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 L_\zeta(a_\epsilon)
   &=\frac{Q_1\log(1/\epsilon)+C_1}{2\pi\epsilon}+O(1),\\
 L_\zeta(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)
   &=\frac{Q_2\log(1/\epsilon)+C_2}{2\pi\epsilon}+O(1).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:weil-compact-asymptotics}
\end{equation}
Both remainders are bounded uniformly for sufficiently small $\epsilon$.
Both values are then positive, and
\begin{equation}
 \frac{|L_\zeta(a_\epsilon)|^2}
      {L_\zeta(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)}
 \sim\frac{Q_1^2}{2\pi Q_2}
                \frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}
 \longrightarrow\infty.
 \label{eq:weil-compact-divergence}
\end{equation}
Consequently $L_\zeta|_{\mathscr D}$ has no positive extension to
$\mathscr D^+$ with finite value at the identity.
The same exclusion holds for every larger $*$-algebra whose functional
restricts to $L_\zeta|_{\mathscr D}$, including $\cA_\zeta$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Smooth compact support makes $\widehat\eta$ rapidly decreasing on the
real line, with the same property for every derivative.
Thus $q$ is a Schwartz function.
Nonnegativity and unit integral of $\eta$ give
$0\leq q\leq1$ and $q(0)=1$.
These facts prove finiteness of $Q_j,C_j$ and strict positivity of $Q_j$.
The logarithm is integrable near zero, and rapid decay controls infinity.

Scaling and the convolution identities give
\[
 a_\epsilon=a_\epsilon^*,\qquad
 \widehat a_\epsilon(t)=q(\epsilon t),\qquad
 \widehat{a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon}(t)=q(\epsilon t)^2.
\]
The two supports lie in $[-2\epsilon,2\epsilon]$ and
$[-4\epsilon,4\epsilon]$, respectively.
If $4\epsilon<\log2$, both functions vanish at every $\pm\log n$,
$n\geq2$.  Every prime-power term of \eqref{eq:weil-functional}
is therefore zero for these tests.

For the polar terms, set
\[
 p_\epsilon^\pm=\int_\R\eta(v)e^{\pm\epsilon v/2}\,dv.
\]
Each satisfies $0<p_\epsilon^\pm\leq e^{\epsilon/2}$.
The polar contributions for the first and second tests are
$2p_\epsilon^+p_\epsilon^-$ and
$2(p_\epsilon^+p_\epsilon^-)^2$.
They are uniformly bounded for $0<\epsilon\leq1$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:gamma-integrable-remainder}, the remaining integrals
have the form
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_\R q(\epsilon t)^j k_\infty(t)\,dt
 &=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R q(\epsilon t)^j
                     \log\frac{|t|}{2\pi}\,dt+R_{j,\epsilon},\\
 |R_{j,\epsilon}|&\leq\frac{\|r\|_{L^1}}{2\pi}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The bound follows from $q^j\leq1$ and is independent of $\epsilon$.
Substitution $u=\epsilon t$ evaluates the first integral as
\[
 \frac{Q_j\log(1/\epsilon)+C_j}{2\pi\epsilon}.
\]
Adding the bounded polar terms proves
\eqref{eq:weil-compact-asymptotics} for the complete functional.
Since $Q_1,Q_2>0$, division gives \eqref{eq:weil-compact-divergence}.
This contradicts \eqref{eq:unit-criterion} for every finite unit value.
A positive finite-value extension on a larger algebra would restrict
to one on $\mathscr D^+$, proving the last assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A finite positive real spectrum]
\label{ex:weil-finite-spectrum}
Let $F\subset\R$ be nonempty and finite, and assign positive integer
multiplicities $m_\gamma$ to its points.  Put
\[
 L_F(f)=\sum_{\gamma\in F}m_\gamma\widehat f(\gamma),\qquad
 M_F=\sum_{\gamma\in F}m_\gamma.
\]
The extension $L_F^+(f+\lambda1)=L_F(f)+M_F\lambda$ is positive, since
its value on $(f+\lambda1)^*(f+\lambda1)$ is
$\sum_{\gamma\in F}m_\gamma|\widehat f(\gamma)+\lambda|^2$.
On $\ell^2(F,m)$, with inner product
$\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_\gamma m_\gamma x_\gamma\overline{y_\gamma}$,
Fourier multiplication represents convolution.
The constant vector implements $L_F$ and has squared norm $M_F$.
For the tests in \eqref{eq:compact-concentrating-tests},
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 L_F(a_\epsilon)\longrightarrow M_F,\qquad
 L_F(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)\longrightarrow M_F,\\
 \frac{|L_F(a_\epsilon)|^2}
      {L_F(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)}\longrightarrow M_F.
 \end{gathered}
\]
These limits follow term by term from $q(\epsilon\gamma)\to1$.
They concern the finite spectral functional $L_F$ and do not verify
the full arithmetic functional $L_\zeta$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Gaussian mass and positive subspaces}

Gaussians give a second quantitative description of the mass growth.
Their convolution law also determines positive finite-dimensional
subspaces.  The normalized family is
\begin{equation}
 g_a(u)=\frac{e^{-u^2/(4a)}}{\sqrt{4\pi a}},
 \qquad \widehat g_a(t)=e^{-at^2},
 \qquad g_a^*\star g_a=g_{2a},\quad a>0.
 \label{eq:gaussian-semigroup}
\end{equation}

For this calculation a weaker gamma estimate suffices.
It follows directly from the convergent series \eqref{eq:digamma}.

\begin{lemma}[The logarithmic size of the gamma term]
\label{lem:gamma-log-size}
For each fixed $\sigma>0$,
\[
 \operatorname{Re}\psi(\sigma+it)=\log|t|+O_\sigma(1)
 \qquad(|t|\geq1).
\]
Consequently $k_\infty(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi$
differs from $\log|t|$ by a bounded function outside $[-1,1]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $|t|\geq1$, put
$h_t(x)=(x+\sigma)/((x+\sigma)^2+t^2)$ on $x\geq0$.
Its derivative changes sign at most once, and its maximum is at most
$1/(2|t|)$.  Thus $\int_0^\infty|h_t'(x)|\,dx\leq1/|t|$.
On each unit interval the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\[
 \left|h_t(n)-\int_n^{n+1}h_t(x)\,dx\right|
 \leq\int_n^{n+1}|h_t'(x)|\,dx.
\]
The real part of the series in \eqref{eq:digamma} therefore equals
\[
 -\gamma+\lim_{N\to\infty}
 \left(\sum_{n=0}^N\frac1{n+1}
 -\frac12\log\frac{(N+1+\sigma)^2+t^2}{\sigma^2+t^2}\right)
 +O(|t|^{-1}).
\]
By the defining limit of $\gamma$, this is
$\tfrac12\log(\sigma^2+t^2)+O(|t|^{-1})$.
The stated bound follows, and replacing $t$ by $t/2$ changes the logarithm
only by $-\log2$.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}[No finite positive unitization of the Weil functional]
\label{thm:weil-mass}
As $a\downarrow0$, the functional in \eqref{eq:weil-functional} satisfies
\begin{equation}
 L_\zeta(g_a)
 =\frac{a^{-1/2}\log(1/a)}{4\sqrt\pi}+O(a^{-1/2}).
 \label{eq:weil-gaussian-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
In particular $L_\zeta(g_{2a})>0$ for all sufficiently small $a$, and
\begin{equation}
 \frac{|L_\zeta(g_a)|^2}{L_\zeta(g_a^*\star g_a)}
 \sim\frac{\sqrt2}{4\sqrt\pi}\,a^{-1/2}\log(1/a)
 \longrightarrow\infty.
 \label{eq:weil-unitization-divergence}
\end{equation}
There is no positive extension of $L_\zeta$ to $\cA_\zeta^+$ with a
finite value at the adjoined unit.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every derivative of $g_a$ is a polynomial times a Gaussian.
Thus $g_a\in\cA_\zeta$.  Completing the square proves
\eqref{eq:gaussian-semigroup}, with the Fourier normalization
\eqref{eq:fourier}.
The polar contribution is $2e^{a/4}=O(1)$.

For $0<a\leq1$, split the Gaussian exponent into two equal parts.
Since $\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)\leq\log n$, the absolute prime contribution
is bounded by
\begin{equation}
 \frac{e^{-(\log2)^2/(8a)}}{\sqrt{\pi a}}
 \sum_{n\geq2}\frac{\log n}{\sqrt n}
                       e^{-(\log n)^2/8}.
 \label{eq:prime-gaussian-bound}
\end{equation}
The series is finite: its terms are at most $n^{-2}$ for all sufficiently
large $n$.  Hence this contribution is exponentially small relative to
$a^{-1/2}$.

Put $k_\infty(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:gamma-log-size} shows that
$k_\infty(t)-\log|t|$ is bounded for $|t|\geq1$.
It is integrable on $[-1,1]$, since $k_\infty$ is smooth there.
Therefore
\[
 \int_{\R}e^{-at^2}\bigl(k_\infty(t)-\log|t|\bigr)\,dt
 =O(a^{-1/2}).
\]
Substitution $s=\sqrt a\,t$ gives
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}e^{-at^2}\log|t|\,dt
 =\frac{a^{-1/2}}{2\pi}
 \left(\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\log(1/a)
          +\int_{\R}e^{-s^2}\log|s|\,ds\right).
\]
The remaining integral is finite.  Adding all three local terms proves
\eqref{eq:weil-gaussian-asymptotic}.
Applying that formula at $a$ and $2a$ proves
\eqref{eq:weil-unitization-divergence}.
A positive unitization of mass $m$ would require
$|L_\zeta(g_a)|^2\leq mL_\zeta(g_{2a})$ by
\eqref{eq:unit-criterion}, which contradicts the divergence.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}

Put $q_t=g_t$ for $t>0$.  The following proposition records the
finite-mass consequence for the same normalized Gaussian family.
\begin{proposition}[Gaussian mass obstruction]
\label{prop:gaussian-mass}
Without any assumption on the zeros of $\zeta$, one has
\[
 L_\zeta(q_t)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt\pi}\,t^{-1/2}\log(1/t)
                  +O(t^{-1/2})\qquad(t\downarrow0).
\]
There is no finite $m$ for which
$L_\zeta^+(g+\lambda1)=L_\zeta(g)+m\lambda$ is positive on
$\cA_\zeta^+$.  In particular, $L_\zeta$ cannot factor through a
positive linear functional defined on an entire $C^*$-algebra.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first two assertions are Theorem~\ref{thm:weil-mass} for $q_t=g_t$.
If $L_\zeta=\omega\circ T$ with $T$ a $*$-homomorphism into a
$C^*$-algebra and $\omega$ positive on that entire algebra,
Theorem~\ref{thm:cstar-unitization} extends $\omega$ positively with
finite mass to its unitization.  If the target is already unital, its
unit has the finite value $\omega(1)$.  In either case composition
with $T^+(g+\lambda1)=T(g)+\lambda1$ contradicts the preceding assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Positive finite Gaussian subspaces]
\label{thm:gaussian-subspaces}
Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$ be distinct positive real numbers.
There exists $a_0>0$ such that the Weil form is positive definite on
\[
 E_a=\operatorname{span}_{\C}\{g_{a\lambda_1},\ldots,g_{a\lambda_r}\}
 \qquad(0<a<a_0).
\]
The dimension of $E_a$ is $r$.  This assertion is unconditional.
The threshold $a_0$ may depend on the entire finite family.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Gram matrix of the displayed functions is the real symmetric matrix
\[
 G(a)_{jk}=L_\zeta(g_{a(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)}).
\]
Apply \eqref{eq:weil-gaussian-asymptotic} at each of the finitely many
scales $a(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)$.  Entry by entry,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{4\sqrt\pi\,\sqrt a}{\log(1/a)}G(a)
 \longrightarrow M,
 \qquad M_{jk}=\frac1{\sqrt{\lambda_j+\lambda_k}}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-gram-limit}
\end{equation}
For a column $c\in\C^r$, let $c^*$ be its conjugate transpose.  Then
\begin{equation}
 c^*Mc=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\R}
       \left|\sum_{j=1}^r c_j e^{-\lambda_j t^2}\right|^2dt.
 \label{eq:limiting-gaussian-gram}
\end{equation}
If this integral vanishes, continuity makes its integrand zero everywhere.
Order the $\lambda_j$ increasingly, divide the resulting identity by
$e^{-\lambda_1t^2}$, and let $t\to+\infty$.
This gives $c_1=0$.  Repeating proves $c=0$.
Thus $M$ is positive definite.
Write $\|c\|_2^2=\sum_j|c_j|^2$ and put
\[
 \mu=\inf_{\|c\|_2=1}c^*Mc.
\]
This infimum is attained: from a minimizing sequence, successively
choose subsequences on which each of its finitely many real and imaginary
coordinates converges.  A bounded real sequence has such a subsequence
by bisecting a bounded interval and retaining at each step a half
containing infinitely many terms.  The nested intervals have lengths
tending to zero, and completeness supplies their common limit.
The resulting coordinate limit still has norm one, and continuity of
the finite quadratic expression gives its value $\mu$.
Positive definiteness at that nonzero limit therefore gives $\mu>0$.

Let $A(a)$ denote the matrix on the left of
\eqref{eq:gaussian-gram-limit}, and set
$\delta(a)=\max_{j,k}|A(a)_{jk}-M_{jk}|$.
The entrywise convergence gives $\delta(a)\to0$.
Finite-sum Cauchy--Schwarz gives, for every $c\in\C^r$,
\[
 |c^*(A(a)-M)c|
 \leq\delta(a)\left(\sum_j|c_j|\right)^2
 \leq r\delta(a)\|c\|_2^2.
\]
Choose $0<a<1$ small enough that $r\delta(a)<\mu/2$.
Then $c^*A(a)c\geq(\mu/2)\|c\|_2^2$.
The scalar relating $A(a)$ to $G(a)$ is positive, so $G(a)$ is positive
definite as well.
The same linear-independence argument applied to the Fourier transforms
$e^{-a\lambda_jt^2}$ proves $\dim E_a=r$.
\end{proof}

For $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2=4$, the limiting matrix is
\[
 M=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt5\\1/\sqrt5&1/\sqrt8\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad\det M=\frac1{20}>0.
\]
This gives a positive two-dimensional family directly from the local terms.
The quantifiers in Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussian-subspaces} fix the finite family
before choosing its scale.  They do not imply positivity on all of
$\cA_\zeta$ at once.

\subsection{Representing vectors and finite trace domains}

\begin{corollary}[Excluded positive carriers]
\label{cor:weil-carriers}
There is no representation
\[
 L_\zeta(g)=\langle\pi(g)\Omega,\Omega\rangle
\]
for every $g\in\mathscr D$ on a positive Hilbert space with a finite-norm
vector $\Omega$ in a common invariant $*$-representation domain.
There is also no $*$-homomorphism $T:\mathscr D\to\mathfrak B$ to a
$C^*$-algebra with a positive linear functional
$\omega:\mathfrak B\to\C$ such that $L_\zeta|_{\mathscr D}=\omega\circ T$.
Both exclusions consequently hold on $\cA_\zeta$ as well.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In the vector case, the adjoint identity on the invariant domain gives
\[
 |L_\zeta(g)|^2
 \leq\|\Omega\|^2\|\pi(g)\Omega\|^2
 =\|\Omega\|^2L_\zeta(g^*\star g).
\]
This contradicts \eqref{eq:weil-compact-divergence}.
In the $C^*$ case, positivity implies boundedness and
$|\omega(b)|^2\leq\|\omega\|\omega(b^*b)$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:cstar-unitization}, or its unital Cauchy--Schwarz proof.
Substitute $b=T(g)$ and use the same divergence.
\end{proof}

This obstruction concerns the functional's representing vector.
It does not prevent bounded GNS multipliers.  Indeed, under RH,
\eqref{eq:zero-norm} and $|\widehat f(t)|\leq\|f\|_{L^1}$ give
\[
 L_\zeta((f\star b)^*\star(f\star b))
 \leq\|f\|_{L^1}^2 L_\zeta(b^*\star b).
\]
Thus every convolution multiplier is bounded under that hypothesis,
and $D_{L_\zeta}^{\mathrm{cl}}=\mathcal H_{L_\zeta}$.
The functional itself has no finite representing vector.
A positive functional on a proper test algebra, or a positive weight
finite on a specified test ideal, remains compatible with this restriction.
A positive weight assigns values in $[0,\infty]$ to positive elements,
additively and homogeneously for nonnegative scalars.  Its finite test
ideal and all products must be specified before a GNS or field
interpretation can be asserted.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional scalar and trace representations]
\label{prop:weil-conditional-trace}
Assume RH.  Let $\Gamma\subset\R$ be the set of distinct zero ordinates,
and let $m_\gamma$ be the multiplicity of $1/2+i\gamma$.
The map $f\mapsto(\widehat f(\gamma))_{\gamma\in\Gamma}$ identifies
the nonunital GNS completion of $L_\zeta$ with
\[
 H_{\mathrm{val}}=\ell^2(\Gamma,m),\qquad
 \langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
                       m_\gamma x_\gamma\overline{y_\gamma}.
\]
Convolution acts by bounded multiplication with $\widehat f$.
On the diagonal ideal
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathfrak J_m&=
 \left\{d\in\ell^\infty(\Gamma):
                   \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}m_\gamma|d_\gamma|<\infty\right\},\\
 \tau_m(d)&=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}m_\gamma d_\gamma,
 \end{aligned}
\]
the positive functional $\tau_m$ satisfies
$L_\zeta(f)=\tau_m((\widehat f(\gamma))_\gamma)$.
It has infinite value at the identity when extended as a positive weight.

For ordinary operator trace, use instead
\begin{equation}
 H_{\mathrm{occ}}=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\C^{m_\gamma},
 \qquad
 \Pi(f)=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
                         \widehat f(\gamma)I_{m_\gamma}.
 \label{eq:weil-multiplicity-trace}
\end{equation}
Every $\Pi(f)$, $f\in\cA_\zeta$, is trace class, and
$L_\zeta(f)=\operatorname{Tr}_{H_{\mathrm{occ}}}\Pi(f)$.
The identity is outside this finite trace domain.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:zero-norm} makes the evaluation map isometric on
the GNS quotient.  To prove density, fix $\gamma_0\in\Gamma$ and use
$f_a(u)=e^{-i\gamma_0u}g_a(u)\in\cA_\zeta$.
Its transform is $e^{-a(t-\gamma_0)^2}$.
As $a\to\infty$, the evaluation vectors tend to the coordinate vector
at $\gamma_0$ in $\ell^2(\Gamma,m)$.
Indeed, for $a\geq1$ their squared errors are dominated by
$m_\gamma e^{-2(\gamma-\gamma_0)^2}$ away from $\gamma_0$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-count} makes this majorant summable.
Dominated convergence proves the claim, and finite coordinate vectors
are dense.  Fourier multiplication and
$|\widehat f(t)|\leq\|f\|_{L^1}$ give the bounded convolution action.

The space $\mathfrak J_m$ is a $*$-ideal of the bounded diagonal algebra:
multiplication by a bounded sequence preserves its defining sum.
Coordinatewise conjugation is its involution, and
$\tau_m(d^*d)=\sum_\gamma m_\gamma|d_\gamma|^2\geq0$.
Rapid Fourier decay and Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-count} give
\[
 \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}m_\gamma|\widehat f(\gamma)|<\infty.
\]
The explicit formula \eqref{eq:target} therefore proves the asserted
factorization through $\tau_m$.

An orthonormal basis of $H_{\mathrm{occ}}$ has $m_\gamma$ vectors for
each $\gamma$.  Thus the trace norm of the diagonal operator $\Pi(f)$
is the displayed absolute sum, and its trace is
$\sum_\gamma m_\gamma\widehat f(\gamma)=L_\zeta(f)$.
This also proves positivity on convolution squares.
If $\sum_\gamma m_\gamma$ were finite, the bounds
$0\leq\widehat a_\epsilon(\gamma)\leq1$ would give
$L_\zeta(a_\epsilon)\leq\sum_\gamma m_\gamma$.
That contradicts \eqref{eq:weil-compact-asymptotics}.
Hence the total multiplicity is infinite, and the identity has infinite
weight in both realizations.
\end{proof}

The scalar and multiplicity spaces encode different information.
The scalar space has one dimension per distinct ordinate.
Its ordinary diagonal trace is $\sum_\gamma\widehat f(\gamma)$,
whereas $\tau_m$ counts the multiplicities.
Repeating each scalar coordinate $m_\gamma$ times embeds
$H_{\mathrm{val}}$ isometrically into $H_{\mathrm{occ}}$.
The image has one dimension in each multiplicity summand, so it is
onto exactly when every zero is simple.
Both constructions use the zero ordinates and assume RH.
They supply no independent arithmetic proof of the positivity hypothesis.

\begin{remark}[A separately specified two-point form]
\label{rem:weil-two-point}
Multiplicativity is unnecessary for the finite-vector obstruction if
both the first moment and the squared norm are prescribed.
Suppose a linear assignment $f\mapsto\mathcal O(f)$ and one finite-norm
vector $\Omega$ satisfy
\[
 \langle\mathcal O(f)\Omega,\Omega\rangle=L_\zeta(f),
 \qquad
 \|\mathcal O(f)\Omega\|^2=L_\zeta(f^*\star f)
 \quad(f\in\mathscr D).
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz alone then gives the impossible bound
\eqref{eq:vector-unit-bound}.

A two-point prescription alone asks instead for
\begin{equation}
 \langle\mathcal O(f)\Omega,\mathcal O(g)\Omega\rangle
       =L_\zeta(g^*\star f).
 \label{eq:weil-two-point-only}
\end{equation}
The mass obstruction does not exclude this separate requirement.
For example, assume Weil positivity and write $j(f)=[f]\in\mathcal H_{L_\zeta}$.
On $K=\C\oplus\mathcal H_{L_\zeta}$ put
\[
 \Omega=(1,0),\qquad \mathcal O(f)(c,h)=(0,cj(f)).
\]
These bounded operators depend linearly on $f$ and satisfy
\eqref{eq:weil-two-point-only}.
Their first moments vanish, and they do not represent convolution or
the test involution.
They give a conditional two-point realization in the larger operator
algebra $\mathcal B(K)$.
Further field products, adjoint relations, and dynamics require their
own constructions.
\end{remark}

\section{The full Weil form on compact support windows}
\label{sec:compact-weil}

The three local terms in the Weil functional define a closed form on
each bounded support interval.  A finite matrix can control this whole
form only when its omitted modes are bounded on the same domain.  We
first identify that domain and a diagonal comparison, and then retain
the complete residual Gram matrix in a weighted Schur inequality.

Let $\mathscr S(\R)$ be the space of smooth functions $b$ such that
\[
 \sup_{t\in\R}(1+|t|)^j|b^{(k)}(t)|<\infty
 \qquad(j,k\geq0).
\]
These seminorms specify its topology.

\begin{lemma}[Fourier--Plancherel with the fixed measure]
\label{lem:prime-plancherel}
The Fourier transform is a continuous bijection of $\mathscr S(\R)$
onto itself and extends uniquely to a unitary map
\[
 \mathcal F:L^2(\R,du)\longrightarrow L^2(\R,dt/(2\pi)).
\]
For $f,h\in L^2(\R)$ and $v\in\R$,
\[
 \langle f,h\rangle_2
   =\int_{\R}\widehat f(t)\overline{\widehat h(t)}\,\frac{dt}{2\pi},
 \qquad \mathcal F(\tau_v f)(t)=e^{itv}\widehat f(t).
\]
Here the hat on an $L^2$ function denotes this extension.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Differentiation under the integral and integration by parts bound each
Schwartz seminorm of $\widehat f$ by finitely many Schwartz seminorms
of $f$.  The Gaussian regularization used in Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian}
uses only continuity, integrability, and the integrability of the Fourier
transform; it therefore gives the same inversion formula for every
Schwartz function.  The inverse integral also maps Schwartz functions
continuously to Schwartz functions, by the same estimates.  This proves
bijectivity on $\mathscr S(\R)$.
For Schwartz $f,h$, insert the inverse transform of $h$ into
$\int f\overline h$.  Fubini applies because
$\|f\|_1\|\widehat h\|_1<\infty$, and yields the displayed pairing.
In particular, the transform is an isometry in the stated measures.

Smooth compactly supported functions are dense in ordinary $L^2$:
truncate support and size, approximate by step functions, and replace
each interval endpoint by a smooth transition on a set of arbitrarily
small measure.  Thus Schwartz functions are dense in both Hilbert spaces.
The isometry extends to their completions; its range is closed by
completeness and contains the dense Schwartz space, so it is onto.
Finally, substitution proves the translation identity for Schwartz
functions.  Translation is an isometry of $L^2(du)$, and multiplication
by $e^{itv}$ is an isometry of $L^2(dt/(2\pi))$; density extends the identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The logarithmic form domain}

For $T>0$, write $I_T=(-T,T)$ and $\mathcal H_T=L^2(I_T,dx)$.
Every element of $\mathcal H_T$ is extended by zero to $\R$ before
taking its Fourier transform or translating it.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Q_T=\left\{g\in\mathcal H_T:
  \int_\R\log(2+|t|)|\widehat g(t)|^2\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}<\infty\right\}.
 \label{eq:window-domain}
\end{equation}
There is no prescribed endpoint value.  In particular, this domain
will contain polynomials restricted to $I_T$.
Put
\[
 m(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi,
 \qquad \ell_\pm(g)=\int_{-T}^Tg(x)e^{\pm x/2}\,dx,
 \qquad c_n=\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}.
\]
Let $S_b g$ be the restriction of $\tau_b g$ to $I_T$; thus
$(S_b g)(x)=g(x-b)$ with the zero extension understood.  The full form is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 Q_T(g)={}&q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}(g)
       +2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\overline{\ell_+(g)}\ell_-(g)\bigr)\\
 &-\sum_{\substack{n\ge2\\\log n<2T}}
       c_n\langle(S_{\log n}+S_{-\log n})g,g\rangle_2,\\
 q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}(g)={}&\int_\R m(t)|\widehat g(t)|^2\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:window-form}
\end{equation}
A term with $\log n=2T$ vanishes almost everywhere.  The sum includes
every prime power in its range, with $c_4=\log2/2$ in particular.
The polar term is a signed cross term, not a positive square.
For $g\in C_c^\infty(I_T)$, Fourier multiplication in
\eqref{eq:weil-functional} gives
$Q_T(g)=L_\zeta(g^*\star g)$.

\begin{proposition}[Closed form and its compact core]
\label{prop:window-closed}
The form $Q_T$ is densely defined, closed and bounded below on
$\mathcal Q_T$.  The space $C_c^\infty(I_T)$ is a form core: it is
dense for the norm of $Q_T+c\|\cdot\|_2^2$ for any sufficiently
large $c$.  There is a unique self-adjoint operator $W_T$ bounded
below whose associated closed form is $Q_T$.  Its resolvent is compact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The digamma series and its large-argument estimate give
\[
 m(t)\ge m(0)=m_0,
 \qquad m(t)=\log|t|-\log(2\pi)+O(|t|^{-1})\quad(|t|\to\infty),
\]
where
\begin{equation}
 m_0=-\gamma-\frac\pi2-3\log2-\log\pi.
 \label{eq:window-mzero}
\end{equation}
For the lower bound, take real parts term by term in
$\psi(z)=-\gamma+\sum_{j\ge0}((j+1)^{-1}-(j+z)^{-1})$.
The value at $1/4$ is the special value in \cite[\S5.4(ii)]{DLMF}.
After adding a constant, $m$ is bounded above and below by positive
multiples of $\log(2+|t|)$.  The weighted Fourier space is complete,
and the condition that the inverse transform vanish outside
$[-T,T]$ is closed in $L^2$.  Thus $q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}$ is closed on
\eqref{eq:window-domain}.  The translations are bounded, and the
two polar vectors $e^{\pm x/2}$ belong to $\mathcal H_T$.
They add a bounded Hermitian form and preserve closedness and the domain.

For the core assertion first compress support by replacing $g$ with
$g_s(x)=s^{-1/2}g(x/s)$, $0<s<1$.  Its transform is
$s^{1/2}\widehat g(st)$.  Dilation tends strongly to the identity
in $L^2(\log(2+|t|)dt)$ as $s\uparrow1$: this follows first for
compactly supported continuous functions and then by density and the
uniform comparison of the weights for $1/2\le s\le1$.
For fixed $s$, convolve $g_s$ with a smooth nonnegative function of
integral one supported in a sufficiently small interval.  The support
stays in $I_T$, and the Fourier multipliers are bounded by one and
tend pointwise to one.  Dominated convergence gives convergence in
the form norm.  This proves the core assertion and density.

For completeness, the associated operator can be obtained directly
from the form.  Choose $c$ so that the polarized form $q_c$ of
$Q_T+c\|\cdot\|_2^2$ dominates $\|\cdot\|_2^2$.  Riesz
representation in the Hilbert space $(\mathcal Q_T,q_c)$ gives a
bounded operator $B$ on $\mathcal H_T$ with
\[
 q_c(Bf,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_2\qquad(v\in\mathcal Q_T).
\]
It is positive, self-adjoint and injective; its range is dense because
its kernel is zero.  The inverse on that range is self-adjoint:
the defining identity for an adjoint shows that
$h\in\operatorname{Dom}((B^{-1})^*)$ precisely when $h=Bk$ for
some $k$, and then $(B^{-1})^*h=k$.  Hence $W_T=B^{-1}-cI$
has the asserted form and is uniquely determined by it.
Indeed the range of $B$ is also dense in the form norm: a vector
$q_c$-orthogonal to every $Bf$ is orthogonal to every $f$ in
$\mathcal H_T$, and is therefore zero.

Finally, a bounded set in the form norm has uniformly small Fourier
$L^2$ tails, bounded by a constant divided by $\log(2+R)$ outside
$[-R,R]$.  On the rectangle $[-T,T]\times[-R,R]$, the Fourier
kernel is a uniform limit of finite sums of separated functions.
The transform from supported $L^2$ functions into $L^2(-R,R)$
is therefore compact.  Combining this with the tail bound proves
compactness of the form-domain inclusion, and hence of $B$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A diagonal comparison in Legendre polynomials}

The logarithmic Fourier weight has a useful spatial expression.  For
$r>0$, define
\begin{equation}
 J(r)=\frac{e^{r/2}}{2\sinh r},\quad
 R(r)=J(r)-\frac1{2r},\quad
 K(r)=\int_r^\infty J(v)\,dv
     =\operatorname{artanh}(e^{-r/2})+\arctan(e^{-r/2}).
 \label{eq:window-kernels}
\end{equation}
The singularity of $R$ is removable, with $R(0)=1/4$.
Expanding $J(r)=\sum_{j\ge0}e^{-(2j+1/2)r}$ and integrating
each nonnegative difference term yields
\begin{equation}
 q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}(g)=m_0\|g\|_2^2+
 \frac12\int_{\R^2}J(|x-y|)|g(x)-g(y)|^2\,dx\,dy.
 \label{eq:window-jump-form}
\end{equation}
Indeed the contribution of $e^{-ar}$ has multiplier
$2t^2/(a(a^2+t^2))$, which is the corresponding digamma-series
difference from its value at zero.  Monotone convergence proves
the identity on the full form domain.  Separating the exterior of
$I_T$ gives the potential $K(T-x)+K(T+x)$ in addition to the
interior double integral.

Let $D_T$ be the nonnegative operator associated with
\[
 d_T(g)=\frac14\int_{I_T^2}
             \frac{|g(x)-g(y)|^2}{|x-y|}\,dx\,dy.
\]
Its form is closed by Fatou's lemma.  Write $P_n$ for the Legendre
polynomial normalized by $P_n(1)=1$, and put
\begin{equation}
 p_n(x)=P_n(x/T),\quad
 g_n=\|p_n\|_2^2=\frac{2T}{2n+1},\quad
 e_n=p_n/\sqrt{g_n},\quad
 H_0=0,\quad H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k.
 \label{eq:window-legendre}
\end{equation}
Then $D_Te_n=H_ne_n$.  To verify this, its polynomial action is
\[
 (D_Tp)(x)=\frac12\int_{-T}^T\frac{p(x)-p(y)}{|x-y|}\,dy.
\]
It preserves polynomials of degree at most $n$ and has leading
coefficient $H_n$ on $x^n$.  Symmetry and Legendre orthogonality
give the stated eigenvectors.  Polynomial density makes them a
complete orthonormal basis, so
$d_T(g)=\sum_{n\ge0}H_n|\langle g,e_n\rangle_2|^2$ on its
entire form domain.  Denote by $P_N^T$ the orthogonal projection
onto $p_0,\ldots,p_{N-1}$.

The polynomial density used here follows from the uniform approximation
proved in Lemma~\ref{lem:cstar-calculus} and the density of continuous
functions in $L^2(I_T)$.

The regular kernel gives a bounded integral operator
$(\mathcal R_Tg)(x)=\int_{-T}^T R(|x-y|)g(y)\,dy$.
Since
\[
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}\left(K(r)+\tfrac12\log r\right)
   =\log2+\frac\pi4,
 \qquad \left(K(r)+\tfrac12\log r\right)'=-R(r),
\]
the interior multiplication term and the exterior potential combine
to give the exact closed-form identity
\begin{equation}
 A_T=D_T-\gamma-\log(2\pi T)+V_T-\mathcal R_T,
 \qquad V_T(x)=-\frac12\log(1-x^2/T^2).
 \label{eq:window-arch-identity}
\end{equation}
Here $A_T$ is the operator associated with $q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}$, and
the right side is interpreted as a sum of forms.  In particular,
$\mathcal Q_T$ is exactly the domain of
$d_T+\int V_T|g|^2$.  Every $p_n$ belongs to
$\operatorname{Dom}W_T$, because $V_Tp_n\in L^2(I_T)$ and all
other terms in its operator image are in $L^2$.  Thus $P_N^T$
preserves $\mathcal Q_T$ without imposing endpoint conditions.

\begin{proposition}[A positive diagonal tail]
\label{prop:window-legendre-lower}
For $0<T\le3/2$, define
\begin{align}
 \nu(T)&=\sum_{\substack{n\ge2\\\log n<2T}}
        2c_n\cos\frac{\pi}{\lceil2T/\log n\rceil+1},\nonumber\\
 a(T)&=-m_0-2K(T)+\nu(T)+2(\sinh T-T).
 \label{eq:window-lower-constant}
\end{align}
Then $W_T\ge D_T-a(T)I$ in form order.  In particular, the
restriction to $(1-P_N^T)\mathcal Q_T$ is bounded below by
$(H_N-a(T))\|\cdot\|_2^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $0<r\le3$,
\[
 \frac{\sinh r}{r}
  \le\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(r^2/6)^k}{k!}
  =e^{r^2/6}\le e^{r/2},
\]
where $(2k+1)!\ge6^kk!$ follows by induction.  Thus $R(r)\ge0$
there.  Also $J$ is decreasing, so $K$ is convex and
$K(T-x)+K(T+x)\ge2K(T)$.  The jump identity now gives
$q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}\ge d_T+(m_0+2K(T))\|\cdot\|_2^2$.

For a translation distance $b>0$, decompose the interval into
chains modulo $b$.  Almost every chain has at most
$q=\lceil2T/b\rceil$ vertices.  On a chain with $k$ vertices,
$S_b+S_{-b}$ is the path adjacency matrix; its sine vectors have
eigenvalues $2\cos(j\pi/(k+1))$, $1\le j\le k$.
The operator norm is consequently at most $2\cos(\pi/(q+1))$.
Summing with the positive coefficients $c_n$ gives $\nu(T)$.
The polar operator has its two possibly nonzero eigenvalues
$2T+2\sinh T$ and $2T-2\sinh T$, on the sum and difference
of $e^{x/2}$ and $e^{-x/2}$.  Its lower bound completes the proof.
\end{proof}

For example, the arithmetic list at $T=3/4$ is $2,3,4$, and
\[
 \nu(3/4)=\frac32\log2+\frac{\log3}{\sqrt3}.
\]
At $T=81/100$ the list is $2,3,4,5$, and the additional term in
$\nu$ is $\log5/\sqrt5$.  Elementary constants in
\eqref{eq:window-lower-constant} can be enclosed using the positive
series for $K$.  Its tail satisfies, for every integer $q\ge1$,
\[
 0\le2K(T)-2\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}
       \frac{e^{-(2j+1/2)T}}{2j+1/2}
 \le\frac{2e^{-(2q+1/2)T}}
          {(2q+1/2)(1-e^{-2T})}.
\]

\section{Finite matrices that bound the whole form}
\label{sec:window-schur}

A diagonal lower bound on the tail allows one to eliminate it by
completion of the square.  Keeping its successive diagonal weights
improves on replacing all of them by the first.  The last infinite
part is measured by its full residual Gram matrix.

Fix $T$ and abbreviate $P_N^T$ to $P_N$.  Let $\widetilde W_T$
be a self-adjoint operator with the same domain as $W_T$ and a
bounded difference, and suppose
\[
 W_T\ge D_T-aI,\qquad \|W_T-\widetilde W_T\|\le e,
 \qquad e\ge0.
\]
Every diagonal compression of an unbounded operator in this section
means the operator associated with its restricted closed form.
For a real target $\lambda$, set
\begin{equation}
 z=\lambda+e,\qquad d_n=H_n-a-\lambda-2e,
 \qquad G_N=\operatorname{diag}(g_0,\ldots,g_{N-1}).
 \label{eq:window-shifts}
\end{equation}
For integers $1\le N<K$, form the matrices
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 M_{ij}&=\langle\widetilde W_Tp_j,p_i\rangle_2,&
 C_{nj}&=\langle\widetilde W_Tp_j,p_n\rangle_2,\\
 J_{ij}&=\langle\widetilde W_Tp_j,\widetilde W_Tp_i\rangle_2,&
 \Gamma_K&=J-C^*G_K^{-1}C.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:window-moments}
\end{equation}
Here $i,j<N$; $C_n$ denotes the indicated row for every $n\ge0$,
and the finite matrix $C$ consists of its first $K$ rows.  These index
orders respect the convention that inner products are linear in
their first argument.  For $E_Nb=\sum_{j<N}b_jp_j$, one has
$\|E_Nb\|_2^2=b^*G_Nb$ and
\[
 \Gamma_K=\bigl((1-P_K)\widetilde W_TE_N\bigr)^*
                  \bigl((1-P_K)\widetilde W_TE_N\bigr)\ge0.
\]
This Gram matrix includes every omitted mode and every mixed product
between the archimedean, prime-power and polar terms.

\begin{theorem}[Complete weighted Schur bound]
\label{thm:window-weighted}
Suppose $d_N>0$.  If
\begin{equation}
 F_{N,K}(\lambda)=M-zG_N
   -\sum_{n=N}^{K-1}\frac{C_n^*C_n}{g_nd_n}
   -\frac{\Gamma_K}{d_K}\ge0,
 \label{eq:window-weighted-matrix}
\end{equation}
where $C_n$ is row $n$ of $C$, then
$Q_T(g)\ge\lambda\|g\|_2^2$ for every $g\in\mathcal Q_T$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $A=\widetilde W_T-zI$ and $Q=1-P_N$.  Its restriction to
the closed tail form domain satisfies
\[
 QAQ\ge\sum_{n\ge N}d_n\langle\,\cdot\,,e_n\rangle e_n>0.
\]
The inverse is bounded above by the inverse of the displayed diagonal
operator.  One way to see inverse order directly is the variational
identity
\[
 \langle K^{-1}x,x\rangle
   =\sup_v\{2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,v\rangle-k(v,v)\}
\]
for any strictly positive closed form $k$ with operator $K$.
The cross operator from $P_N\mathcal H_T$ to the tail is bounded,
since all its columns are operator-domain polynomials.  Its exact
Schur correction is therefore at most
\[
 \sum_{n\ge N}\frac{C_n^*C_n}{g_nd_n}
 \le\sum_{n=N}^{K-1}\frac{C_n^*C_n}{g_nd_n}
          +\frac{\Gamma_K}{d_K}.
\]
Completion of the square in the tail variable, on the original
form domain, gives $A\ge0$.  Finally
$W_T\ge\widetilde W_T-eI=A+\lambda I$.
\end{proof}

Both error shifts in \eqref{eq:window-shifts} are necessary.  The
low block loses $eG_N$ at target zero, and the tail comparison loses
$2e$.  Positivity of $\widetilde W_T$ without these shifts would
give only $W_T\ge-eI$.  Moreover, a generalized eigenvalue of the
finite sufficient matrix relative to $G_N$ is not itself a spectral
lower bound for $W_T$; a proposed target must enter all the
denominators in \eqref{eq:window-weighted-matrix}.

\subsection{Scalar weights and the original inverse correction}

At target zero, let $B_{\mathrm{wt}}$ denote the correction subtracted
from $M-eG_N$ in \eqref{eq:window-weighted-matrix}, and define
\[
 \Gamma_N=J-MG_N^{-1}M,\qquad
 B_{\mathrm{sc}}=\Gamma_N/d_N,\qquad
 S_{\mathrm{wt}}=M-eG_N-B_{\mathrm{wt}},\quad
 S_{\mathrm{sc}}=M-eG_N-B_{\mathrm{sc}}.
\]
The weighted improvement is exactly
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 S_{\mathrm{wt}}-S_{\mathrm{sc}}
  ={}&\sum_{n=N}^{K-1}
        \left(\frac1{d_N}-\frac1{d_n}\right)\frac{C_n^*C_n}{g_n}\\
   &+\left(\frac1{d_N}-\frac1{d_K}\right)\Gamma_K\ge0.
\end{split}
\label{eq:window-weighted-gain}
\end{equation}
A negative direction of $S_{\mathrm{sc}}$ can thus measure loss in
the scalar estimate while the full form remains nonnegative.

This comparison also controls the exact correction for $W_T$.
Put $A=\widetilde W_T-eI$, so $0\le W_T-A\le2eI$.
For either operator, its Schur form on the low space is the infimum
of its full form over the tail variable.  Hence the exact Schur
forms $S_W,S_A$, expressed in the $p_j$ coordinates, satisfy
$S_W\ge S_A$.  If $B_W$ is the exact correction for $W_T$, then
\begin{equation}
 B_W\le B_{\mathrm{wt}}+2eG_N,
 \qquad B_{\mathrm{sc}}-B_W\ge B_{\mathrm{sc}}-B_{\mathrm{wt}}-2eG_N.
 \label{eq:window-original-correction}
\end{equation}
Indeed $S_A\ge M-eG_N-B_{\mathrm{wt}}$ and the low matrix of $W_T$
is at most $M+eG_N$.  Evaluation at the minimizing tail for $A$
also gives the two-sided estimate
\begin{equation}
 0\le S_W-S_A\le2e\left(G_N+\frac{\Gamma_N}{d_N^2}\right).
 \label{eq:window-schur-perturbation}
\end{equation}
Here the tail minimizer has squared norm at most
$b^*\Gamma_Nb/d_N^2$, since the inverse tail operator has norm
at most $d_N^{-1}$.  Thus an overestimate of the inverse correction,
an error in replacing $W_T$, and a negative value of the full form
are three different quantities.

\subsection{A finite solve for the true tail inverse}

The diagonal inverse estimate can itself be compared with the true
inverse.  This requires solving only a finite part of the tail and
bounding the residual, including its interaction with the far modes.
Use the orthonormal basis $e_n$ in this subsection, let $P=P_N$,
$Q=1-P$, and suppose the tail operator satisfies
\[
 \mathcal K=QW_TQ\ge\mathcal L
    :=\sum_{n\ge N}(H_n-a)\langle\,\cdot\,,e_n\rangle e_n>0.
\]
For $M>N$, split the tail into the projections
$R_0=P_M-P_N$ and $F_0=1-P_M$.  In that decomposition write
\[
 \mathcal K=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal D&\mathcal E\\
                      \mathcal E^*&\mathcal G\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathcal L=\begin{pmatrix}L_R&0\\0&L_F\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad B=R_0W_TP,\quad C=F_0W_TP.
\]
The finite spaces lie in the operator domain, so these cross
operators are bounded.  For any linear map
$V:P\mathcal H_T\to R_0\mathcal H_T$, define
\begin{align}
 T_V&=B^*V+V^*B-V^*\mathcal D V,\nonumber\\
 E_V&=(B-\mathcal D V)^*L_R^{-1}(B-\mathcal D V)
      +(C-\mathcal E^*V)^*L_F^{-1}(C-\mathcal E^*V).
 \label{eq:window-tail-solve}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Inverse correction with a residual interval]
\label{prop:window-inverse-residual}
The exact omitted-mode correction obeys
\begin{equation}
 T_V\le (QW_TP)^*\mathcal K^{-1}(QW_TP)\le T_V+E_V.
 \label{eq:window-inverse-interval}
\end{equation}
For $u\in P\mathcal H_T$, put $f=u-Vu$ and
$z_n=\langle W_Tf,e_n\rangle_2$.  For every $J_0\ge M$, the
far residual in $\langle E_Vu,u\rangle$ is at most
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n=M}^{J_0-1}\frac{|z_n|^2}{H_n-a}
  +\frac{\|W_Tf\|_2^2-\sum_{n=0}^{J_0-1}|z_n|^2}{H_{J_0}-a}.
 \label{eq:window-far-residual}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $x=QW_Tu$ and $y=(Vu,0)$.  Completing the square gives
\[
 \langle\mathcal K^{-1}x,x\rangle
  =2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,y\rangle
       -\langle\mathcal Ky,y\rangle
       +\langle\mathcal K^{-1}(x-\mathcal Ky),x-\mathcal Ky\rangle.
\]
The last term is nonnegative and is at most the same expression
with $\mathcal L^{-1}$, by inverse order.  Its two components are
$(B-\mathcal D V)u$ and $(C-\mathcal E^*V)u$, which proves
\eqref{eq:window-inverse-interval} on every vector and hence in
operator order.  The latter component is $F_0W_Tf$.  Expanding in
$e_n$ and bounding all denominators beyond $J_0$ by
$(H_{J_0}-a)^{-1}$ proves \eqref{eq:window-far-residual}.
\end{proof}

In particular, the far residual contains the mixed products of
$C$ with $\mathcal E^*V$.  Replacing it by $C^*L_F^{-1}C$
would not give the stated correction.  The final numerator in
\eqref{eq:window-far-residual} is the full squared norm of the
omitted residual; no decay hypothesis replaces it.

For one low vector $u$, let
\[
 A_u=Q_T(u),\quad t_u=\langle T_Vu,u\rangle,\quad
 \eta_u=\langle E_Vu,u\rangle,\quad
 m_u=\langle\mathcal L^{-1}QW_Tu,QW_Tu\rangle.
\]
The true Schur value lies in
$[A_u-t_u-\eta_u,A_u-t_u]$.  The excess of the diagonal
correction over the true correction lies in
\begin{equation}
 [m_u-t_u-\eta_u,m_u-t_u]\cap[0,\infty).
 \label{eq:window-inverse-excess}
\end{equation}
If the true-Schur interval has a positive lower endpoint while
$A_u-m_u<0$, the negative sufficient test is entirely explained
by the inverse comparison.  An interval meeting zero leaves an
uncertainty measured by the two residuals in
\eqref{eq:window-tail-solve}.

There is a further, separable excess when the diagonal sum uses a
constant denominator beyond $J_0$.  If
$x_n=\langle QW_Tu,e_n\rangle$ and $d_n=H_n-a$, then
\[
 \widehat m_{J_0}-m_u
   =\sum_{n\ge J_0}|x_n|^2
                \left(\frac1{d_{J_0}}-\frac1{d_n}\right)\ge0,
 \quad
 \widehat m_{J_0}=
  \sum_{n=N}^{J_0-1}\frac{|x_n|^2}{d_n}
       +\frac{\sum_{n\ge J_0}|x_n|^2}{d_{J_0}}.
\]
Any further finite block bounds part of this excess from below;
the remaining squared norm divided by $d_{J_0}$ bounds the rest
from above.  Thus this loss is distinct from the inverse loss in
\eqref{eq:window-inverse-excess} and from a bounded operator error.
For $W_T-\lambda I$, all low and tail diagonal blocks and all
denominators must include $-\lambda$.  For a bounded approximation
one must also include its operator-error allowance as in
\eqref{eq:window-shifts}.  A negative sufficient matrix alone
never supplies a negative value of $Q_T$.

\section{Exact moments and finite-window positivity}
\label{sec:window-finite}

The finite data in Theorem~\ref{thm:window-weighted} are determined
by polynomial and logarithmic integrals.  We first derive a finite
criterion with explicit error bounds, then evaluate it on two support
windows.

\subsection{The logarithmic matrix}

In the coordinate $s=x/T$, the normalized Legendre vectors are
$\sqrt{(2n+1)/2}P_n(s)$.  Multiplication by
$V(s)=-\tfrac12\log(1-s^2)$ has matrix
\begin{equation}
 V_{mn}=\begin{cases}
 0,&m+n\text{ odd},\\[2pt]
 \displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(2m+1)(2n+1)}}{|n-m|(n+m+1)},
      &m\ne n,\ m+n\text{ even},\\[6pt]
 \displaystyle 2(H_{2n}-H_n)+\frac1{2n+1}-\log2,&m=n.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:window-log-matrix}
\end{equation}
For the off-diagonal formula, subtract the Legendre equations
$((1-s^2)P_n')'+n(n+1)P_n=0$, multiply by the opposite
polynomial and integrate against $\log(1-s^2)$.  For $n>m$ the
remaining numerator is
\[
 -2\int_{-1}^1s(P_n'P_m-P_nP_m')\,ds
   =\begin{cases}-4,&n+m\text{ even},\\0,&n+m\text{ odd}.
     \end{cases}
\]
Integration by parts leaves the endpoint value $1+(-1)^{n+m}$;
orthogonality removes the lower-degree terms.  Division by
$n(n+1)-m(m+1)$ gives the formula with the normalizing factors.

For the diagonal, let $P_n^{(\alpha,\alpha)}$ be the Jacobi
polynomial orthogonal for $(1-s^2)^\alpha$, with $|\alpha|<1/4$
and normalization
$P_n^{(\alpha,\alpha)}(1)=\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)/(n!\Gamma(\alpha+1))$.
Rodrigues' formula and $n$ integrations by parts give its squared
norm and leading coefficient
\[
 h_n(\alpha)=
 \frac{2^{2\alpha+1}\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)^2}
 {(2n+2\alpha+1)n!\Gamma(n+2\alpha+1)},\qquad
 k_n(\alpha)=
 \frac{\Gamma(2n+2\alpha+1)}{2^nn!\Gamma(n+2\alpha+1)}.
\]
Differentiate the squared norm $h_n/k_n^2$ of its monic version
at zero.  The derivative of the polynomial has degree below $n$
and contributes zero by orthogonality.  The logarithmic derivative
of the displayed norm is
$2\log2-2/(2n+1)-4(H_{2n}-H_n)$, proving the diagonal formula.

The same entries give a useful remote bound.  For $M\ge N\ge1$,
put $c_{N,M}=1-N(N-1)/(M(M+1))>0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\sum_{n=M}^\infty |V_{nm}|^2
       \le\frac{N^2}{c_{N,M}^2M^2}.
 \label{eq:window-log-remote}
\end{equation}
Indeed $n(n+1)-m(m+1)\ge c_{N,M}n(n+1)$, and
$(2n+1)/(n^2(n+1)^2)=n^{-2}-(n+1)^{-2}$ telescopes after
dropping the parity restriction.  This bounds the logarithmic
cross operator alone.  In a residual containing other terms,
their mixed products remain part of the full Gram matrix.

\subsection{A bounded polynomial replacement}

In \eqref{eq:window-arch-identity}, replace $R$ by its Taylor
polynomial $R_{64}$ through degree $64$, $\gamma$ by a rational
number $\gamma_*$, and the two vectors $e^{\pm x/2}$ in the
polar cross operator by their Taylor polynomials through degree
$32$.  Denote the resulting operator by $\widetilde W_T$.
The potential $V_T$, all coefficients $c_n$ and all translation
endpoints remain exact.  Only bounded operators have been
replaced, so the operator and form domains are unchanged.

Here are explicit allowances for these replacements.  Define the
Bernoulli polynomials and numbers by
\[
 \frac{ze^{xz}}{e^z-1}=\sum_{j\ge0}B_j(x)\frac{z^j}{j!},
 \qquad B_j=B_j(0).
\]
Thus $B_1=-1/2$.  For integers $q,m\ge1$, put
\[
 C_{q,m}=H_q-\frac1{2q}
       +\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{B_{2j}}{2jq^{2j}},\qquad
 E_{q,m}=\frac{|B_{2m}|}{2mq^{2m}}.
\]
Euler--Maclaurin for $x^{-1}$ on $[q,M]$, followed by
$M\to\infty$, gives
\begin{equation}
 |\gamma-(C_{q,m}-\log q)|\le E_{q,m}.
 \label{eq:window-gamma-error}
\end{equation}
The remainder is the negative of
$\int_q^\infty B_{2m}(\{x\})x^{-2m-1}dx$.
The absolutely convergent Fourier series of the periodic Bernoulli
polynomial, obtained by repeated integration by parts, bounds its
absolute value by $|B_{2m}|$ and proves the inequality.  Thus a
rational enclosure of $\log q$ gives a fully specified bound for
$|\gamma-\gamma_*|$ without a sign assumption on the remainder.

Writing $R(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}r_jz^j$, multiplication by
$2\sinh z$ gives the rational recurrence
\begin{equation}
 r_n=\frac1{2^{n+2}(n+1)!}
    -\frac{\mathbf1_{\{n\text{ odd}\}}}{2(n+2)!}
    -\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{r_{n-2j}}{(2j+1)!}.
 \label{eq:window-r-recurrence}
\end{equation}
In particular $r_0=1/4$.  On $|z|=3$,
$|\sinh z|\ge\sin3$ and
$|R(z)|\le e^{3/2}/(2\sin3)+1/6<17$.
For the first bound use $|\sinh z|^2=
\sinh^2(\operatorname{Re}z)+\sin^2(\operatorname{Im}z)$.
If $|\operatorname{Re}z|\ge1$ the hyperbolic term suffices;
otherwise $\sqrt8<|\operatorname{Im}z|\le3<\pi$.
For $L=2T<3$, Cauchy's coefficient estimate beyond degree $128$
and the integral-kernel norm bound therefore give
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathcal R_T-\mathcal R_{T,64}\|
 \le L\left\{\sum_{j=65}^{128}|r_j|L^j
            +\frac{17(L/3)^{129}}{1-L/3}\right\}.
 \label{eq:window-r-error}
\end{equation}
Retaining the intervening exact coefficients only sharpens the
error bound; the approximating kernel still has degree $64$.

Each polar vector has norm $M_T=\sqrt{2\sinh T}$.  Its Taylor
remainder has norm at most
$b_T^{\mathrm{pol}}=\sqrt{2T}e^{T/2}(T/2)^{33}/33!$.
The rank-one operator inequality
$\|\langle\,\cdot\,,v\rangle u\|=\|u\|\|v\|$
therefore bounds the polar cross error by
$4M_Tb_T^{\mathrm{pol}}+2(b_T^{\mathrm{pol}})^2$.
An admissible total operator allowance is any $e$ at least
\begin{equation}
 |\gamma-\gamma_*|
 +L\left\{\sum_{j=65}^{128}|r_j|L^j
            +\frac{17(L/3)^{129}}{1-L/3}\right\}
 +4M_Tb_T^{\mathrm{pol}}+2(b_T^{\mathrm{pol}})^2.
 \label{eq:window-total-error}
\end{equation}
Finite matrix entry errors are additional to this operator allowance.

\subsection{Polynomial and logarithmic moments}

Set $t=(x+T)/L$, so $0<t<1$.  With $p_j(t)=P_j(2t-1)$,
each column has the form
\begin{equation}
 (\widetilde W_Tp_j)(x)=V(t)p_j(t)+q_j(t),\qquad
 V(t)=-\log2-\tfrac12\log t-\tfrac12\log(1-t).
 \label{eq:window-piecewise-column}
\end{equation}
The function $q_j$ is polynomial on the intervals cut out by
$\log n/L$ and $1-\log n/L$ for the retained prime powers.
The regular-kernel integral splits at $y=x$, where each part is
polynomial; the compressed translations give precisely the stated
breakpoints.  At $T=3/4$ there are seven intervals, and for
$j<64$ the degree of $q_j$ is at most $128$.
At $T=81/100$ there are nine intervals, and for $j<128$ the
degree is at most $192$.  With $K=256$, the ordinary moments
needed for $C$ have degrees at most $383$ and $447$, respectively.

For $b=k+1$ and $H_b^{(2)}=\sum_{j=1}^b j^{-2}$, the
full-interval logarithmic moments are
\begin{align}
 \int_0^1t^k\log t\,dt&=-b^{-2},&
 \int_0^1t^k\log^2t\,dt&=2b^{-3},\nonumber\\
 \int_0^1t^k\log(1-t)\,dt&=-H_b/b,&
 \int_0^1t^k\log^2(1-t)\,dt&=(H_b^2+H_b^{(2)})/b,\nonumber\\
 \int_0^1t^k\log t\log(1-t)\,dt
   &=H_b/b^2-(\pi^2/6-H_b^{(2)})/b.
 \label{eq:window-log-moments}
\end{align}
The first two follow by differentiating $\int_0^1t^{b-1}dt$.
The others follow by differentiating the beta integral
$\int_0^1t^{b-1}(1-t)^{c-1}dt=
\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c)/\Gamma(b+c)$ at $c=1$, and also in $b$
for the mixed moment.  Differentiation is justified by integrable
logarithmic majorants in a compact subset of $b,c>0$.
The gamma recurrence yields the harmonic numbers in the formulas.
On subintervals, the primitive
\[
 t^{k+1}\left(\frac{\log t}{k+1}-\frac1{(k+1)^2}\right)
\]
and the substitution $t\mapsto1-t$ give the single-logarithm
moments.  In $J$, the terms $V^2p_ip_j$ always have limits
$0,1$; only the mixed terms with $q_j$ require subdivision.
Both $Vp_iq_j$ terms and the full $q_iq_j$ product are retained.
The resulting moment description involves no quadrature remainder.

\subsection{A rational finite lower matrix}

Here is one explicit way of turning entry enclosures into the
matrix inequality of Theorem~\ref{thm:window-weighted}.
Suppose $L,a,e,\lambda$ are rational and $d_N>0$.
Let real symmetric centers for $M,J$ and real centers for $C$
enclose every corresponding entry with radius less than $10^{-80}$.
Reflection symmetry makes the opposite-parity entries zero.
Round each center downward to a multiple of $S^{-1}$, $S=10^{80}$,
using the same value for symmetric entries, and enlarge every entry
radius to $r=3/S$.  Write the resulting centers as
$\widehat M,\widehat C,\widehat J$.  Assume their entries have
absolute value at most $100$.  Then, with
\[
 r_M=r_J=Nr,\quad r_C=Kr,\quad B=100K,\quad
 g_*=(2K-1)/L,\quad h=2Br_C+r_C^2,
\]
the spectral-norm errors are bounded by $r_M,r_C,r_J$ and
$\|\widehat C\|\le B$, $\|\widehat J\|\le100N$.
The difference between a true and centered $C$ Gram matrix is
bounded by $h$, and its $G_K^{-1}$-weighted version by $g_*h$.

Round each weight $1/(g_nd_n)$, $N\le n<K$, and $1/d_K$
downward to a multiple of $V^{-1}$, $V=10^{100}$.
Using these weights and the rounded centers in
\eqref{eq:window-weighted-matrix} defines a rational matrix
$\widehat F$.  Direct norm estimates give
\begin{equation}
 \|F_{N,K}(\lambda)-\widehat F\|\le E_F,
 \quad
 E_F=r_M+\frac{g_*}{d_N}h+\frac{B^2}{V}
       +\frac{r_J+g_*h}{d_K}
       +\frac{100N+g_*B^2}{V}.
 \label{eq:window-rational-error}
\end{equation}
The two terms divided by $V$ bound inverse-weight rounding;
all other terms bound the moment errors.  Consequently, if
$E_F<\kappa$ and $\widehat F-\kappa I\ge0$, then
$F_{N,K}(\lambda)\ge0$.  A finite sufficient hypothesis for
the latter inequality is that every leading principal minor of
each parity block of $\widehat F-\kappa I$ be positive.
These are exact rational inequalities involving the complete
remote Gram expression, not its trace or its diagonal alone.

\begin{corollary}[Two finite-window criteria]
\label{cor:window-conditional}
For either row of the following table, assume that
$a(T)\le a$, that \eqref{eq:window-total-error} is at most $e$,
and that $d_N>0$.  Assume the entry enclosures and magnitude bounds
specified above, $E_F<10^{-60}$, and positivity of every leading
principal minor in both parity blocks of
$\widehat F-10^{-60}I$.
\[
 \begin{array}{c|r|r|c|c|c}
 T&N&K&a&e&\lambda\\\hline
 3/4&64&256&4.30094&4\cdot10^{-22}&0\\
 81/100&128&256&5.16201132&7.3\cdot10^{-20}&10^{-18}
 \end{array}
\]
Then the corresponding complete form satisfies
$Q_T(g)\ge\lambda\|g\|_2^2$ on $\mathcal Q_T$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The first two hypotheses give the operator comparison and error
bound in \eqref{eq:window-shifts}.  The moment and finite-matrix
hypotheses imply $F_{N,K}(\lambda)\ge0$ by
\eqref{eq:window-rational-error}.  Apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:window-weighted}.
\end{proof}

In the second row the low shift is
$\lambda+e=1.073\cdot10^{-18}$, and the tail shift is
$\lambda+2e$; both belong to the same full-form estimate.

A coarser allowance has a separate conditional implication.  If a
bounded approximation on $I_{3/4}$ has operator error at most
$8\cdot10^{-9}$, and a complete Schur bound for that approximation
gives a lower endpoint $-2\cdot10^{-9}$, then
\begin{equation}
 Q_{3/4}(g)\ge-10^{-8}\|g\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:window-coarse-allowance}
\end{equation}
This follows by adding the operator error.  It does not require
the stronger finite positivity hypotheses in
Corollary~\ref{cor:window-conditional}.

\section{Changing the support window}
\label{sec:window-cofinal}

\subsection{The new-prime collar and exact zero extension}

A new prime term appears on a thin pair of endpoint intervals when
the support passes its threshold.  Its operator norm need not tend
to zero with their width.  The exterior logarithmic potential does,
however, give a relative form bound.

For the threshold $b=\log5$, let $L=2T$ satisfy $b<L<2b$ and
put $\delta=L-b$.  In coordinates $0<x<L$, set
$w(x)=K(x)+K(L-x)$ and $c_5=\log5/\sqrt5$.
The positive exponential expansion of $K$ makes $w$ log-convex:
Cauchy--Schwarz for that expansion gives
$w(x)w(\delta-x)\ge w(\delta/2)^2$.
Since $w(x+b)=w(\delta-x)$, weighted arithmetic--geometric mean
implies, for $0<x<\delta$,
\[
 2c_5|g(x)g(x+b)|
 \le\alpha\bigl(w(x)|g(x)|^2+w(x+b)|g(x+b)|^2\bigr),
 \qquad
 \alpha=\frac{c_5}{K(\delta/2)+K(b+\delta/2)}.
\]
The two intervals are disjoint.  Integrating and using
\eqref{eq:window-jump-form} gives
\begin{equation}
 \bigl|c_5\langle(S_b+S_{-b})g,g\rangle_2\bigr|
  \le\alpha\bigl(q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}(g)-m_0\|g\|_2^2\bigr).
 \label{eq:window-collar-relative}
\end{equation}
Let $W^-$ be the full operator with just the $n=5$ term deleted,
on this same interval, and let $\nu_{\mathrm{old}}(T)$ be the sum in
\eqref{eq:window-lower-constant} with just that term omitted.
Set
$C^-=-m_0+2(\sinh T-T)+\nu_{\mathrm{old}}(T)$.
The other polar and arithmetic terms give
$q_{\mathrm{ar,T}}-m_0I\le W^-+C^-I$.
Thus, if $W^-\ge\lambda_-I$ and $0\le\alpha\le1$, the
operator $W^+$ including the new prime satisfies
\begin{equation}
 W^+\ge\bigl((1-\alpha)\lambda_- -\alpha C^-\bigr)I.
 \label{eq:window-collar-affine}
\end{equation}
This formula allows either sign of $\lambda_-$.  It requires a
lower bound on the same interval; a bound on a smaller support
does not provide that hypothesis.

\begin{proposition}[Coherence under zero extension]
\label{prop:window-extension}
If $0<T\le U$, zero extension embeds $\mathcal Q_T$ isometrically
for the physical $L^2$ norm into $\mathcal Q_U$, and
$Q_U(g)=Q_T(g)$ there.  Consequently any full lower bound
$Q_U\ge\lambda\|\cdot\|_2^2$ holds on every smaller window.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The extended function, its Fourier transform, and its two polar
integrals are unchanged.  A translation by distance at least $2T$
has zero inner product with a function supported in $[-T,T]$.
Thus every term of the complete form agrees.  The Fourier domain
condition \eqref{eq:window-domain} is unchanged as well.
\end{proof}

This is a statement about form domains and forms; it does not assert
that an operator-domain restriction commutes with the extension.
Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:window-certified}, it gives the
common bound
\begin{equation}
 Q_T(g)\ge10^{-18}\|g\|_2^2
 \qquad(0<T\le81/100, g\in\mathcal Q_T).
 \label{eq:window-common-bound}
\end{equation}
At and below $T=(\log5)/2$ the new term vanishes.  Just above
that threshold, while $2T<2\log5$, its norm is $c_5$: on each
nontrivial chain it is $c_5$ times a two-vertex adjacency matrix.
The common bound therefore follows from exact agreement of forms,
without an operator-norm continuity claim at the threshold.

\subsection{Fixed-window convergence}

For every finite $T$ there is a positive Legendre tail.  The
regular kernel satisfies the uniform estimate $|R(r)|\le1/4$.
To see the upper bound, compare power-series coefficients in
\[
 e^{r/2}\le(1+r/2)\frac{\sinh r}{r}\qquad(r\ge0).
\]
The even coefficients require $4^k\ge2k+1$ and the odd
coefficients require $4^k\ge1$.  Division by $2\sinh r$ gives
$R(r)\le1/4$.  The proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:window-legendre-lower} gives $R\ge0$ on
$[0,3]$.  On $[3,\infty)$, positivity of $J$ gives
$R(r)\ge-1/(2r)\ge-1/6$.  The value $R(0)=1/4$ completes the bound.
The row and column integrals therefore give
$\|\mathcal R_T\|\le T/2$, and
\begin{equation}
 W_T\ge D_T-\mathcal A(T)I,
 \quad
 \mathcal A(T)=\gamma+\log(2\pi T)+\frac12T
                   +\nu(T)+2(\sinh T-T).
 \label{eq:window-global-comparison}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu(T)$ retains its finite path-sum definition for all $T$.

The logarithmic cross operator also admits an $N$-independent bound:
\begin{equation}
 \|(1-P_N)V_TP_N\|\le\pi.
 \label{eq:window-uniform-log-cross}
\end{equation}
For $n\ge N>m$, \eqref{eq:window-log-matrix} implies
$|V_{nm}|\le1/(n-m)$, because
$\sqrt{(2m+1)(2n+1)}\le m+n+1$.
Reversing the low index reduces this majorant to a rectangular
part of the matrix $1/(k+l+1)$, $k,l\ge0$.
For $a=k+1/2$, the function $t^{-1/2}/(a+t)$ is convex.
Midpoint convexity on the intervals $[l,l+1]$ gives
\[
 \sum_{l\ge0}\frac{(l+1/2)^{-1/2}}{k+l+1}
  \le\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{(a+t)\sqrt t}
  =\frac\pi{\sqrt a}.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz with these positive row and column weights proves
that the majorant has norm at most $\pi$, establishing
\eqref{eq:window-uniform-log-cross}.
The exact polar norm is $2T+2\sinh T$, so
\begin{equation}
 \|(1-P_N)W_TP_N\|\le b(T),
 \qquad b(T)=\pi+\frac12T+2T+2\sinh T+\nu(T).
 \label{eq:window-uniform-cross}
\end{equation}

Polynomials are a core for the original form.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:window-closed}, it suffices to approximate
a compactly supported smooth function.  Approximate its derivative
uniformly by polynomials, integrate, and adjust the constant.
For the resulting difference $q$,
\[
 d_T(q)\le\frac{(2T)^3}{12}\|q'\|_\infty^2,
 \qquad
 \int_{I_T}V_T|q|^2\le2T(1-\log2)\|q\|_\infty^2.
\]
The bounded terms tend to zero with the $L^2$ norm.
Thus, if $\mu_N$ is the smallest eigenvalue of the exact low
operator $P_NW_TP_N$, then $\mu_N$ decreases to
$\inf\operatorname{spec}(W_T)$.
With $\delta_N=H_N-\mathcal A(T)>0$, the two-dimensional
comparison on $\|P_Ng\|_2$ and $\|(1-P_N)g\|_2$ gives
\begin{equation}
 W_T\ge r_N I,\qquad
 r_N=\frac{\mu_N+\delta_N-
       \sqrt{(\mu_N-\delta_N)^2+4b(T)^2}}2.
 \label{eq:window-galerkin-convergence}
\end{equation}
Since $\delta_N\to\infty$ and $b(T)$ does not depend on $N$,
$r_N\to\inf\operatorname{spec}(W_T)$ for fixed $T$.
This gives a convergent lower-enclosure construction without a
signed estimate as $T$ grows.  A simpler sufficient allowance,
when $P_NW_TP_N\ge-\alpha P_N$ with $\alpha\ge0$, is
\[
 W_T\ge-\left(\alpha+\frac{b(T)^2}{\delta_N}\right)I.
\]
The full residual Gram gives a sharper matrix correction than
the scalar numerator $b(T)^2$.

\subsection{The cofinal signed estimate}

\begin{theorem}[Cofinal lower allowances]
\label{thm:window-cofinal}
Suppose $T_j\to\infty$ and each window has a complete matrix
$F_j(0)$ as in \eqref{eq:window-weighted-matrix}, including its
operator error and a positive zero-target tail $d_{N_j}(0)>0$.
Let $\widehat F_j$ be a finite center, let $G_j$ be its physical
Gram matrix, and suppose
\begin{align}
 \sigma_j&=\lambda_{\min}
       (G_j^{-1/2}\widehat F_jG_j^{-1/2}),\nonumber\\
 \rho_j&\ge\|G_j^{-1/2}(F_j(0)-\widehat F_j)G_j^{-1/2}\|,
 \qquad \sigma_j\ge\rho_j-\varepsilon_j,
 \quad \varepsilon_j\ge0,\quad\varepsilon_j\longrightarrow0.
 \label{eq:window-cofinal-estimate}
\end{align}
Then every $Q_T$ is nonnegative on $\mathcal Q_T$, and
$L_\zeta(f^*\star f)\ge0$ for every $f\in\cA_\zeta$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The assumptions give $F_j(0)\ge-\varepsilon_jG_j$.
Decreasing the target from zero to $-\varepsilon_j$ increases
each positive tail denominator and adds $\varepsilon_jG_j$ to
the low block.  Since every subtracted Gram matrix is nonnegative,
\[
 F_j(-\varepsilon_j)\ge F_j(0)+\varepsilon_jG_j\ge0.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:window-weighted} yields
$Q_{T_j}\ge-\varepsilon_j\|\cdot\|_2^2$.
For a fixed $g\in\mathcal Q_T$, zero extension eventually puts
$g$ in every $\mathcal Q_{T_j}$ with its form value and norm
unchanged.  Letting $j\to\infty$ gives $Q_T(g)\ge0$.

For $f\in\A_a$, $a>1/2$, take smooth cutoffs equal to one on
larger and larger intervals.  Their products with $f$ converge
to $f$ in every seminorm of $\A_{a'}$, where
$1/2<a'<a$: the product rule reduces each difference to weighted
tails of derivatives of $f$, with uniformly bounded cutoff
derivatives.  Proposition~\ref{prop:algebra} and continuity of
$L_\zeta$ then pass positivity from the compactly supported
convolution squares to $f^*\star f$.
\end{proof}

An ordinary Euclidean matrix error $E_jI$ gives the admissible
physical error
\begin{equation}
 \rho_j=\frac{E_j}{g_{\min,j}},\qquad
 g_{\min,j}=\frac{2T_j}{2N_j-1}.
 \label{eq:window-physical-error}
\end{equation}
The Gram normalization is necessary: an entry or Euclidean norm
error does not already measure error per unit physical $L^2$ norm.
The constants in \eqref{eq:window-global-comparison} and
\eqref{eq:window-uniform-cross} prove that each fixed window has
a positive tail and a convergent lower enclosure.  They contain
exponentially growing polar norms, whereas $H_N$ grows only
logarithmically.  They do not establish
\eqref{eq:window-cofinal-estimate}.  That estimate depends on the
signed low matrix and its full mixed residual, where the three
local terms can cancel.  The next construction makes the approximation
errors explicit while preserving this signed question.

\begin{proposition}[No eventual positive power-law margin]
\label{prop:window-near-null}
There are no constants $c>0$, $M\in\R$ and a cofinal sequence
$T_j\to\infty$ such that
$Q_{T_j}(g)\ge cT_j^{-M}\|g\|_2^2$ for every $j$ and every
$g\in\mathcal Q_{T_j}$.  In particular, no such bound holds on
all sufficiently large windows.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Such a bound would make every compact-window form nonnegative,
by zero extension, and hence imply the full Weil criterion and RH.
Under that consequence, choose a nonzero
$\phi\in C_c^\infty(-1,1)$ with $\|\phi\|_2=1$ and set
$v_T(x)=T^{-1/2}\phi(x/T)$.  The explicit formula gives
\begin{equation}
 Q_T(v_T)=T\sum_\gamma m_\gamma
                    |\widehat\phi(T\gamma)|^2,
 \qquad \|v_T\|_2=1.
 \label{eq:window-near-null}
\end{equation}
There is a nonzero gap around ordinate zero.  Indeed, on
$0<s<1$ the alternating Dirichlet series is positive when grouped
in consecutive pairs, and equals $(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$ by
partial summation and continuation.  Thus $\zeta(1/2)<0$.
Discreteness of the zeros gives the gap under RH.
Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-count} then implies
$\sum_\gamma m_\gamma|\gamma|^{-2k}<\infty$ for every integer
$k\ge1$.  Repeated integration by parts in $\widehat\phi$
gives $Q_T(v_T)=O_k(T^{1-2k})$.  Taking $2k>M+1$ contradicts
the asserted lower bound along the same sequence $T_j$.
\end{proof}

This obstruction concerns a positive margin.  It excludes neither
nonnegativity nor negative lower allowances tending to zero.

\section{Why an indefinite quotient does not give positivity}

The Hermitian part of the construction exists without positivity.
For a complex $*$-algebra $\mathfrak A$ and a Hermitian functional $W$,
meaning $W(a^*)=\overline{W(a)}$, put
\[
 B_W(f,g)=W(g^*f),\qquad
 R_W=\{f:B_W(f,g)=0\text{ for every }g\in\mathfrak A\}.
\]
Then $R_W$ is a left ideal, since
\[
 B_W(af,g)=W(g^*af)=B_W(f,a^*g)=0\quad(f\in R_W).
\]
The quotient $\mathfrak A/R_W$ consequently has a nondegenerate Hermitian
pairing and an algebraic left action satisfying
$B_W(af,g)=B_W(f,a^*g)$.
This applies unconditionally to $(\cA_\zeta,L_\zeta)$ by
\eqref{eq:hermitian}.  It supplies neither a positive norm nor a class of
the missing unit.  Under positivity, Cauchy--Schwarz identifies $R_W$
with $\cN_W$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:gns} applies.

Let $V=\C^2$, $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, and
\[
 [x,y]=y^*Jx.
\]
The vectors $e_1+e_2$ and $e_1-e_2$ are isotropic, while their sum is
not.  Thus the isotropic cone $\{x:[x,x]=0\}$ is not a linear subspace
and cannot be divided out.  The radical
$\{x:[x,y]=0\ \forall y\}$ is zero here.  In general, quotienting by the
radical produces only a nondegenerate Hermitian space; it supplies
neither a fundamental symmetry nor a complete Hilbert majorant.
A fundamental symmetry is a linear involution $J$ satisfying
$[Jx,y]=[x,Jy]$ and $[Jx,x]>0$ for $x\ne0$.
A complete Hilbert majorant is a complete Hilbert norm on the same space
for which the original Hermitian pairing is continuous.
In this finite-dimensional example the displayed $J$ gives both choices,
but the radical quotient itself selects neither choice.

Nor is a vector functional for an indefinite adjoint positive.  On
$M_2(\C)$ put $A^\sharp=JA^*J$, let $\Omega=e_1$, and set
$\omega(A)=[A\Omega,\Omega]$.  For
\[
 X=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
one has
\begin{equation}
 \omega(X^\sharp X)=[X\Omega,X\Omega]=[e_2,e_2]=-1.
 \label{eq:indefinite-negative}
\end{equation}
Hence an indefinite pairing does not provide the positivity required in
\eqref{eq:weil-criterion}.

\section{Positive arithmetic forms from prime translations}
\label{sec:wp-prime-translations}

The negative prime coefficients admit a positive realization after an
explicit diagonal compensation.
The compensation is forced by positivity, even for a single prime.
This gives a nonunital arithmetic carrier with infinite mass and determines
the obstruction to summing its positive forms over all primes.
The comparison uses the convolution algebra $\cA_\zeta$ and the Fourier
normalization of \eqref{eq:fourier} throughout.
All spaces and algebras in this section are complex and have degree zero.
There is no formal parameter or change of coefficient field.

For $v\in\R$, let $(\tau_v f)(u)=f(u-v)$.
Lebesgue measure makes $\tau_v$ unitary on $L^2(\R,du)$.
If $f\in\cA_\zeta$ and $g=f^*\star f$, direct substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 g(0)=\|f\|_2^2,\qquad
 g(v)=\langle f,\tau_v f\rangle_2,\qquad
 \|f-\tau_v f\|_2^2=2g(0)-g(v)-g(-v).
 \label{eq:wp-translation-square}
\end{equation}
The inner product is linear in its first argument.
The reflected value satisfies $g(-v)=\overline{g(v)}$.
These identities follow from absolutely convergent integrals, since
$\cA_\zeta\subset L^2(\R)$.

\begin{example}[One prime and one Gaussian]
\label{ex:wp-prime-two}
Retain only $n=2$, with coefficient
$c=\log 2/\sqrt 2$ and translation length $\ell=\log 2$.
Define
\[
 W_{\{2\}}(g)=c\bigl(2g(0)-g(\ell)-g(-\ell)\bigr).
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:wp-translation-square} gives
$W_{\{2\}}(f^*\star f)=c\|f-\tau_\ell f\|_2^2\geq0$.
For the normalized Gaussian $f=g_a$ with $a=\ell^2/8$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 W_{\{2\}}(g_{2a})&=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}(1-e^{-1}),\\
 -c\bigl(g_{2a}(\ell)+g_{2a}(-\ell)\bigr)
 &=-\sqrt{\frac2\pi}e^{-1},\\
 2c\,g_{2a}(0)&=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wp-prime-two-values}
\end{equation}
Indeed, $g_{2a}(0)=1/(\sqrt\pi\ell)$ and
$g_{2a}(\ell)=e^{-1}g_{2a}(0)$.
Thus the raw prime term is already negative on this convolution square.
Its positive realization includes the displayed nonzero diagonal term.
\end{example}

The same prime gives a two-dimensional calculation with an exact Gram
matrix.  Keep $a=\ell^2/8$, put $x=e^{-1}$, and use the ordered vectors
$f_0=g_a$, $f_1=\tau_\ell g_a$.
The Hermitian form $B(f,h)=W_{\{2\}}(h^*\star f)$ has matrix
\begin{equation}
 G=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 2(1-x)&2x-1-x^4\\
 2x-1-x^4&2(1-x)
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:wp-two-vector-gram}
\end{equation}
To compute its off-diagonal entry, expand
$c\langle f_0-\tau_\ell f_0,
\tau_\ell f_0-\tau_{2\ell}f_0\rangle_2$.
The four correlations give
$c(2g_{2a}(\ell)-g_{2a}(0)-g_{2a}(2\ell))$.
The eigenvalues of $\sqrt{2\pi}G$ are
\[
 1-x^4,\qquad 3-4x+x^4=(1-x)^2(x^2+2x+3).
\]
Both are positive because $0<x<1$.
This calculation retains the actual prime weight, convolution involution,
and off-diagonal pairing on the two displayed test functions.

Let $(c_n)_{n\geq2}$ be nonnegative real numbers satisfying
\[
 0<C:=\sum_{n\geq2}c_n<\infty.
\]
Only indices with $c_n>0$ contribute.
The cases of interest retain the Euler weights
$\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)/\sqrt n$ on finitely many prime powers,
or on every power of finitely many primes.
Define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 W_c(g)&=\sum_{n\geq2}c_n
       \bigl(2g(0)-g(\log n)-g(-\log n)\bigr),\\
 \lambda_c(t)&=\sum_{n\geq2}c_n|1-e^{it\log n}|^2
       =2\sum_{n\geq2}c_n(1-\cos(t\log n)),\\
 d\mu_c(t)&=\lambda_c(t)\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wp-positive-density}
\end{equation}
The coefficient sum makes the first series absolutely convergent for
bounded $g$, and the second uniformly convergent on $\R$.
Consequently $\lambda_c$ is continuous and $0\leq\lambda_c\leq4C$.
Choosing any $n_0$ with $c_{n_0}>0$ shows that its zeros belong to
$\{2\pi k/\log n_0:k\in\Z\}$.
It is therefore positive almost everywhere.
When all coefficients vanish, $W_c$ and $\mu_c$ are zero.
That degenerate case has a zero Hilbert space and admits finite positive
unitizations.  The hypothesis $C>0$ excludes it in the following statements.

\begin{theorem}[The arithmetic positive form and its least compensation]
\label{thm:wp-positive-form}
For every $g\in\cA_\zeta$ and $f\in\cA_\zeta$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 W_c(g)&=\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)\,d\mu_c(t),\\
 W_c(f^*\star f)
 &=\sum_{n\geq2}c_n\|f-\tau_{\log n}f\|_2^2
   =\int_{\R}|\widehat f(t)|^2\,d\mu_c(t).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wp-positive-realization}
\end{equation}
The second value is strictly positive for $f\ne0$.
For real $d$, define
\[
 V_d(g)=d\,g(0)-\sum_{n\geq2}c_n
                         \bigl(g(\log n)+g(-\log n)\bigr).
\]
Then $V_d(f^*\star f)\geq0$ for every $f\in\cA_\zeta$
if and only if $d\geq2C$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fourier inversion gives
\[
 2g(0)-g(v)-g(-v)
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)(2-e^{-itv}-e^{itv})\,dt.
\]
Since $\widehat g$ is integrable and $\sum c_n=C$, an integrable
bound $4C|\widehat g(t)|$ permits summation inside the integral.
This proves the first identity in \eqref{eq:wp-positive-realization}.
Apply it to $g=f^*\star f$ and use
$\widehat{f^*\star f}=|\widehat f|^2$ on the real axis.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wp-translation-square} gives the other expression.
If its value is zero, $\widehat f$ vanishes almost everywhere because
$\lambda_c>0$ almost everywhere.
Continuity and Fourier inversion then give $f=0$.

For $d\geq2C$, the identity
$V_d(f^*\star f)=W_c(f^*\star f)+(d-2C)\|f\|_2^2$
proves positivity.
Conversely, use $f=g_A$ and divide by the positive number $g_{2A}(0)$.
The Gaussian formula gives
\[
 \frac{V_d(g_{2A})}{g_{2A}(0)}
 =d-2\sum_{n\geq2}c_n e^{-(\log n)^2/(8A)}
 \longrightarrow d-2C\qquad(A\to\infty).
\]
The summable coefficients justify the limit.
If $d<2C$, the value is negative for sufficiently large $A$.
\end{proof}

Every power of one prime can be retained without a cutoff.
For a prime $p$, put $r=p^{-1/2}$, $\theta=t\log p$, and
$c_{p^m}=\log p\,r^m$ for $m\geq1$, with all other coefficients zero.
Then
\begin{equation}
 C_p=\frac{\log p\,r}{1-r},\qquad
 \lambda_p(t)=
 \frac{2\log p\,r(1+r)(1-\cos\theta)}
 {(1-r)(1-2r\cos\theta+r^2)}.
 \label{eq:wp-euler-density}
\end{equation}
To verify the expression, sum $\sum_{m\geq1}r^m=r/(1-r)$ and
take the real part of $\sum_{m\geq1}(re^{i\theta})^m$.
The latter real part is
$(r\cos\theta-r^2)/(1-2r\cos\theta+r^2)$.
Subtracting gives \eqref{eq:wp-euler-density}.
Its denominator is positive because $0<r<1$.
This constructs the positive form of one complete local Euler factor,
with exactly its prime-power coefficients and least diagonal compensation.

\subsection{A nonunital algebra and its Hilbert completion}

On the Schwartz space $\mathscr S(\R)$ defined in
Section~\ref{sec:compact-weil}, take pointwise multiplication and the involution
$b^*(t)=\overline{b(t)}$.
The product rule makes $\mathscr S(\R)$ a complex $*$-algebra.
It has no identity: a pointwise identity must equal one wherever
$e^{-t^2}$ is nonzero, whereas Schwartz functions tend to zero at infinity.



Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak B_c=\mathscr S(\R),\qquad
 \omega_c(b)=\int_{\R}b(t)\,d\mu_c(t),\qquad
 T_c:\cA_\zeta\longrightarrow\mathfrak B_c,
 \quad T_c(g)=\widehat g|_{\R}.
 \label{eq:wp-carrier}
\end{equation}
The bound $\lambda_c\leq4C$ makes this integral finite and continuous
in the Schwartz topology.
It is not asserted to be continuous in the supremum norm.

\begin{theorem}[Arithmetic GNS representation]
\label{thm:wp-carrier}
The map $T_c$ is an injective $*$-homomorphism, and
$W_c=\omega_c\circ T_c$.
The functional $\omega_c$ is positive with zero GNS null space.
Its GNS Hilbert space and algebraic invariant domain are
\[
 H_c=L^2(\R,\mu_c),\qquad D_c=\mathscr S(\R)\subset H_c,
\]
with action $(\pi_c(b)h)(t)=b(t)h(t)$.
Every $\pi_c(b)$ extends boundedly, with
$\|\pi_c(b)\|=\sup_{t\in\R}|b(t)|$.
The image $T_c(\cA_\zeta)$ is dense in $H_c$.
Thus the GNS isometry $[f]\mapsto\widehat f$ for $W_c$ is onto
after Hilbert completion.
Neither $W_c$ nor $\omega_c$ admits a positive unitization of finite mass.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $g\in\cA_\zeta$, every derivative of $u^j g(u)$ is integrable.
Fourier differentiation and repeated integration by parts therefore show
$\widehat g\in\mathscr S(\R)$.
The product and involution identities in \eqref{eq:transform} prove
that $T_c$ is a $*$-homomorphism.
Fourier inversion proves its injectivity, and
\eqref{eq:wp-positive-realization} proves the equality of functionals.
The identity
\[
 \omega_c(b^*b)=\int_{\R}|b(t)|^2\,d\mu_c(t)
\]
proves positivity and identifies its null space as zero.

Smooth compactly supported functions are dense in $H_c$.
Indeed, truncate a vector's support and size first.
The resulting bounded functions of compact support belong to ordinary
$L^2(dt)$ and approximate the vector in $H_c$ by dominated convergence.
Approximate them in $L^2(dt)$ by smooth compactly supported functions.
The bound $d\mu_c\leq(2C/\pi)dt$ transfers this approximation to $H_c$.
For this ordinary approximation, step functions are dense in $L^2(dt)$,
and smooth ramps replace their interval endpoints on sets of vanishing measure.
It follows that $D_c$ has Hilbert completion $H_c$.
Multiplication preserves $D_c$ and satisfies the adjoint identity there.
Its norm is at most $\sup|b|$.
Every interval has positive $\mu_c$ measure.
A bump function in an interval where $|b|>\sup|b|-\epsilon$
gives the reverse norm bound, after normalization and then
$\epsilon\downarrow0$.

To prove density of the image, take $b\in C_c^\infty(\R)$ and
let $f$ be its inverse Fourier transform.
Integration by parts shows that $f\in\mathscr S(\R)$ and $\widehat f=b$.
Choose a smooth cutoff $\chi$ equal to one on $[-1,1]$ and supported
in $[-2,2]$.
Then $f_j(u)=\chi(u/j)f(u)$ belongs to $C_c^\infty(\R)\subset\cA_\zeta$
and converges to $f$ in every Schwartz seminorm.
Fourier differentiation and integration by parts show that
$\widehat f_j\to b$ in every Schwartz seminorm.
In particular, this convergence holds in $H_c$.
The preceding density proves the assertion about the GNS isometry.

Finally, evaluate $W_c$ on the Gaussians $g_a$ as $a\downarrow0$.
Since every contributing $n$ is at least two,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 W_c(g_a)&=\frac1{\sqrt{\pi a}}
       \sum_{n\geq2}c_n\bigl(1-e^{-(\log n)^2/(4a)}\bigr)\\
 &=\frac{C}{\sqrt{\pi a}}
       \left(1+O\left(e^{-(\log2)^2/(4a)}\right)\right),\\
 \frac{|W_c(g_a)|^2}{W_c(g_{2a})}
 &\sim\frac{\sqrt2\,C}{\sqrt\pi}\,a^{-1/2}\longrightarrow\infty.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wp-infinite-mass}
\end{equation}
The unitization inequality \eqref{eq:unit-criterion} excludes any finite mass.
A positive unitization of $\omega_c$ would pull back through $T_c$
to one of $W_c$, so it is excluded as well.
\end{proof}

The weight can also be defined on the positive cone of $L^\infty(\mu_c)$
by $\Phi_c(b)=\int b\,d\mu_c\in[0,\infty]$.
Its finite linear ideal is
$\mathfrak m_c=L^1(\mu_c)\cap L^\infty(\mu_c)$.
This ideal is closed under conjugation and multiplication by bounded functions.
Its square-integrable ideal is
$\mathfrak n_c=L^2(\mu_c)\cap L^\infty(\mu_c)$.
For $b\in\mathfrak n_c$, one has $b^*b\in\mathfrak m_c$ and
$\Phi_c(b^*b)=\|b\|_{H_c}^2$.
Bounded functions supported on finite intervals approximate any vector
in $H_c$.
Moreover, restricting a nonnegative bounded function to these intervals
gives increasing finite-weight functions with that function as their limit.
These statements specify the finite ideals without assigning a finite
value to the identity.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wp-infinite-mass} and monotone convergence for
$e^{-at^2}\uparrow1$ imply $\Phi_c(1)=\infty$.

Although every Schwartz multiplier is bounded, the functional has no
finite-norm representing vector in any invariant $*$-representation domain.
Such a vector would give
$|W_c(g)|^2\leq\|\Omega\|^2W_c(g^*\star g)$ by Cauchy--Schwarz,
contrary to \eqref{eq:wp-infinite-mass}.
The positive functional is defined on the proper algebra $\mathfrak B_c$.
Its supremum-norm completion is $C_0(\R)$, the continuous functions
vanishing at infinity: truncate their tails and approximate the compactly
supported functions uniformly by convolution with smooth approximate identities.
It does not extend positively to this entire $C^*$-algebra.
Such an extension would admit a finite positive unitization by
Theorem~\ref{thm:cstar-unitization}, contrary to the proved divergence.

\subsection{Frequency multipliers on a common invariant domain}

There is a natural unbounded multiplier compatible with this arithmetic
representation: the real Fourier coordinate $t$.
For clarity, it is a multiplier of $\mathfrak B_c$, not an element of it.
Its powers cannot be inserted as finite-weight observables.
Indeed, choose $n_0$ with $c_{n_0}>0$.
The lower bound
$\lambda_c(t)\geq2c_{n_0}(1-\cos(t\log n_0))$
is uniformly positive on a fixed positive-length interval in every
period.  Hence $\int_\R |t|^k\,d\mu_c(t)=\infty$
for every integer $k\geq0$.
Put
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 (Nh)(t)&=t h(t),&
 \mathcal D(N)&=\{h\in H_c:t h\in H_c\},\\
 D_c^\infty&=\bigcap_{k\geq0}\{h\in H_c:t^k h\in H_c\}.&&
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wp-moment-domain}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Self-adjoint frequency and all products]
\label{prop:wp-domain}
The operator $N$ is unbounded and self-adjoint.
Schwartz functions form a core for every power $N^k$.
The space $D_c^\infty$ is dense and complete for the seminorms
$\|t^k h\|_{H_c}$, $k\geq0$.
It is invariant under $N$, polynomial multipliers, Schwartz multipliers,
and $U_s h(t)=e^{ist}h(t)$ for every real $s$.
All products and adjoint identities hold on this common domain.
The image $T_c(\cA_\zeta)$ is dense in $D_c^\infty$ for these seminorms.
On that image, $N T_c(f)=T_c(i\partial_u f)$ and
$U_s T_c(f)=T_c(\tau_s f)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication by a measurable real function on its maximal domain is
closed in this case: take an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence
of a convergent pair $h_j\to h$, $t h_j\to v$ in $H_c$.
Its limits obey $v=th$.
To construct such a subsequence, choose successive errors with summable
squared norms and apply the integral bound to their squared sum.

The operator is symmetric.
If $v$ belongs to its adjoint domain, the adjoint identity tested on all
bounded functions of compact support forces $N^*v=tv$ almost everywhere.
Those tests belong to $\mathcal D(N)$, and on each compact interval
both sides are integrable against them by Cauchy--Schwarz.
Thus $tv\in H_c$ and $v\in\mathcal D(N)$.
This proves self-adjointness.
The same argument applies to multiplication by $t^k$.
Support and size truncation followed by smooth approximation gives density
of $C_c^\infty$ in its graph norm, because
$(1+t^{2k})\lambda_c(t)$ is bounded on each compact interval.
It follows that Schwartz functions form a core.
Normalized bumps supported in $(R,R+1)$ have $\|Nh\|\geq R\|h\|$,
so $N$ is unbounded.

A Cauchy sequence in all the displayed seminorms has a Hilbert limit
$h$ and limits for every $t^k h_j$.
Closedness of each multiplication operator identifies these limits as
$t^k h$ and proves completeness.
Support truncation and smooth approximation give density of Schwartz
functions simultaneously in any finite collection of the seminorms.
Polynomial multiplication only increases their indices.
A Schwartz multiplier $b$ obeys
$\|t^k bh\|\leq\|b\|_\infty\|t^k h\|$, and $U_s$ preserves each seminorm.
These estimates prove invariance and continuity.
Pointwise multiplication and conjugation prove all product and adjoint
identities on $D_c^\infty$.

For $b\in\mathscr S$, the cutoff construction in the preceding theorem
approximates $b$ by transforms of compactly supported smooth functions
in the Schwartz topology.
That topology controls every seminorm in \eqref{eq:wp-moment-domain},
since $\lambda_c$ is bounded.
This proves the image density.
Integration by parts gives $\widehat{i\partial_u f}(t)=t\widehat f(t)$,
and a change of variable gives $\widehat{\tau_s f}(t)=e^{ist}\widehat f(t)$.
Both $i\partial_u f$ and $\tau_s f$ remain in $\cA_\zeta$.
\end{proof}

Dominated convergence makes $(U_s)_{s\in\R}$ a strongly continuous
unitary group on $H_c$.
For $h\in\mathcal D(N)$, the estimate
$|(e^{ist}-1)/s|\leq|t|$ gives
$(U_s h-h)/s\to iNh$ in $H_c$.
Conversely, a convergent difference quotient has almost-everywhere limit
$ith$ along a subsequence, so its Hilbert limit requires $th\in H_c$.
Thus its generator domain is exactly $\mathcal D(N)$.
The smaller domain $D_c^\infty$ permits every polynomial in this generator.
For the Schwartz algebra alone, its common GNS graph completion is all
of $H_c$, since each of its multipliers is bounded.
These two domain statements refer to different multiplier algebras.

\subsection{The convergence boundary of the positive prime sum}

For $X\geq2$ and a real exponent $\sigma$, set
\[
 C_{X,\sigma}=\sum_{2\leq n\leq X}
               \Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)n^{-\sigma},\qquad
 q_{X,\sigma}(f)=\sum_{2\leq n\leq X}
    \Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)n^{-\sigma}
                        \|f-\tau_{\log n}f\|_2^2.
\]
These forms are defined for every $f\in L^2(\R,du)$.
Increasing $X$ increases their values.
The Weil exponent is $\sigma=1/2$.

\begin{theorem}[The exact convergence boundary for the positive prime sum]
\label{thm:wp-prime-limit}
For $\sigma>1$, the coefficient sum
$C_\sigma=\sum_{n\geq2}\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)n^{-\sigma}$ is finite.
It therefore defines the positive carrier above with all primes present.
Moreover,
\[
 0\leq q_\sigma(f):=\lim_{X\to\infty}q_{X,\sigma}(f)
 \leq4C_\sigma\|f\|_2^2.
\]
For every real $\sigma\leq1$ and every nonzero $f\in L^2(\R)$,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{X\to\infty}q_{X,\sigma}(f)=+\infty.
 \label{eq:wp-prime-divergence}
\end{equation}
Thus the finite domain of the increasing limit form is $\{0\}$
when $\sigma\leq1$.
It is not a densely defined quadratic form on $L^2(\R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $\sigma>1$, use
$0\leq\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)\leq\log n$ and the integral comparison
for $\sum(\log n)n^{-\sigma}$.
The bound $\|f-\tau_v f\|_2\leq2\|f\|_2$ proves the stated estimate.

For divergence of the coefficients, prime factorization gives
$\sum_{d\mid m}\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(d)=\log m$.
For an integer $M\geq4$, sum this identity over $m\leq M$ to obtain
\[
 \log(M!)=\sum_{n=2}^M\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)
                          \left\lfloor\frac Mn\right\rfloor
 \leq M\sum_{n=2}^M\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}n.
\]
The last $\lceil M/2\rceil$ factorial factors are at least $M/2$.
Hence the last sum is at least $\frac12\log(M/2)$ and tends to infinity.
For $\sigma\leq1$, $n^{-\sigma}\geq n^{-1}$, so
$C_{X,\sigma}\to\infty$ as well.

Finally, $\langle f,\tau_v f\rangle_2\to0$ as $v\to+\infty$.
To prove this, approximate $f$ in $L^2$ by a function $h$ supported
in a fixed finite interval.
For sufficiently large $v$, $h$ and $\tau_vh$ have disjoint supports.
Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the difference between their correlation and
that of $f$ by $\|f-h\|_2(\|f\|_2+\|h\|_2)$, uniformly in $v$.
The approximation error can tend to zero.
It follows that
$\|f-\tau_{\log n}f\|_2^2\to2\|f\|_2^2$ as $n\to\infty$.
For $f\ne0$, these squared norms are at least $\|f\|_2^2$
for every sufficiently large $n$.
The divergent coefficient tail proves \eqref{eq:wp-prime-divergence}.
\end{proof}

The damping exponent changes the coefficients and therefore changes the
functional.
For $\sigma>1$, the Euler product in \eqref{eq:euler} also writes its
positive Fourier density as
\[
 \lambda_\sigma(t)=2\left(-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\sigma)
       +\operatorname{Re}\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\sigma-it)\right).
\]
This equality uses only absolutely convergent Dirichlet series.
Continuation of its right side beyond that half-plane does not continue
the positive sum in \eqref{eq:wp-positive-density}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-prime-limit} proves the failure of that sum already
at $\sigma=1$.

The positive form is finite on every ordinary $L^2$ vector only in the
summable case.  Individual primes always satisfy that condition.  To keep
those local constructions simultaneously, one may place their Hilbert
spaces in orthogonal coordinates, where different prime functions need
not be equal.  This leads to a representation in which multiplication,
creation, and their common domains can be compared directly.

\section{Arithmetic currents and prime inclusions}
\label{sec:wc-currents}

The prime forms define creation and annihilation currents with the convolution involution.  Orthogonal prime coordinates give compatible
inclusions for all finite sets of primes.  Their Hilbert sum also supports
infinite families of currents with square-summable prime coordinates.
The unweighted arithmetic diagonal has no nonzero vector in this Hilbert
sum.  The multiplication representation nevertheless survives on all primes.

All vector spaces are complex.  Inner products are linear in their first
argument.  Algebra elements have degree zero.  Particle number gives a
separate nonnegative grading, with ordinary commutators throughout.
No coefficient change, deformation parameter, or zero spectrum enters
the construction.

For a prime $p$, retain every power $p^m$ and set
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 c_{p,m}&=(\log p)p^{-m/2},&
 C_p&=\sum_{m\geq1}c_{p,m}=\frac{\log p}{\sqrt p-1},\\
 \lambda_p(t)&=\sum_{m\geq1}c_{p,m}|1-e^{itm\log p}|^2,&
 d\mu_p(t)&=\lambda_p(t)\frac{dt}{2\pi},\\
 W_p(g)&=\sum_{m\geq1}c_{p,m}
 \bigl(2g(0)-g(m\log p)-g(-m\log p)\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-local-data}
\end{equation}
The density is given explicitly by \eqref{eq:wp-euler-density}.
In particular, $0\leq\lambda_p\leq4C_p$ and $\lambda_p>0$ almost
everywhere.  The measure is locally finite and has infinite total mass.
The Fourier map of \eqref{eq:fourier} gives
\[
 H_p=L^2(\R,\mu_p),\qquad
 T_p f=\widehat f,\qquad
 \langle T_pf,T_ph\rangle=W_p(h^*\star f).
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-carrier} proves that $T_p(\cA_\zeta)$ is dense in $H_p$.

\subsection{The nonunital arithmetic algebra}

Let $\mathcal P$ denote the set of primes.  On the disjoint union
$X=\mathcal P\times\R$, put
$\nu(\{p\}\times E)=\mu_p(E)$ for Borel sets $E$.
For a finite nonempty $P\subset\mathcal P$, define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 B_P&=\bigoplus_{p\in P}\mathscr S(\R),&
 B_{\mathrm{loc}}&=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathcal P}^{\mathrm{alg}}\mathscr S(\R),\\
 H_P&=\bigoplus_{p\in P}H_p,&
 H&=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathcal P}H_p=L^2(X,\nu),\\
 \omega(b)&=\sum_p\int_\R b_p(t)\,d\mu_p(t),&
 (\pi(b)h)_p(t)&=b_p(t)h_p(t).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-local-algebra}
\end{equation}
The superscript $\mathrm{alg}$ means finite prime support.
Products and involution are pointwise:
$(bd)_p=b_pd_p$ and $(b^*)_p=\overline{b_p}$.
Give $B_{\mathrm{loc}}$ the locally convex direct sum topology of its
Schwartz summands.  All defining integrals are finite in this algebra.

\begin{proposition}[Prime algebra, weight, and arithmetic map]
\label{prop:wc-weight}
The algebra $B_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is nonunital, and $\omega$ is a continuous
positive functional with zero GNS null space.
Its GNS completion is $H$, with bounded multiplication representation $\pi$.
For $b\in B_{\mathrm{loc}}$,
\[
 \|\pi(b)\|=\max_p\|b_p\|_\infty.
\]
The extended positive weight on $L^\infty(X,\nu)$ is
$\Phi(b)=\int_X b\,d\nu\in[0,\infty]$ for $b\geq0$.
Its finite ideals are
\[
 \mathfrak m_\Phi=L^1(\nu)\cap L^\infty(\nu),\qquad
 \mathfrak n_\Phi=L^2(\nu)\cap L^\infty(\nu).
\]
One has $\Phi(1)=\infty$.
The functional $\omega$ has no positive unitization of finite mass.

For finite nonempty $P$, the map
\begin{equation}
 T_P:\cA_\zeta\longrightarrow B_P,\qquad
 (T_Pf)_p=\widehat f\quad(p\in P)
 \label{eq:wc-arithmetic-map}
\end{equation}
is an injective $*$-homomorphism.  With $W_P=\sum_{p\in P}W_p$,
\[
 \omega(T_Pg)=W_P(g),\qquad
 \|T_Pf\|_{H_P}^2=W_P(f^*\star f).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
An identity would restrict to the constant one in each Schwartz summand,
which is impossible.  The density bound for $\lambda_p$ proves continuity
of each integral in the Schwartz topology.  The direct sum topology gives
continuity of their sum.  Positivity and the null-space assertion follow
from
\[
 \omega(b^*b)=\sum_p\|b_p\|_{H_p}^2
\]
and $\lambda_p>0$ almost everywhere.
Truncation in primes and density of Schwartz functions in each $H_p$
prove density in $H$.  The multiplication norm follows by choosing a
bump in one prime coordinate near the supremum of its multiplier.

For bounded measurable functions, integration gives the stated ideals.
The sets consisting of finitely many prime coordinates and bounded real
intervals have finite measure and exhaust $X$.
Their characteristic functions give finite-weight truncations of every
nonnegative bounded function.  A single prime coordinate already has
infinite total measure, so $\Phi(1)=\infty$.
A finite positive unitization of $\omega$ would restrict to one for its
$p=2$ summand, contrary to Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-carrier}.
Finally, the Fourier product and involution identities apply in every
coordinate.  Equation~\eqref{eq:wp-positive-realization} gives both
functional identities and Fourier inversion gives injectivity.
\end{proof}

The minimal Hilbert space for $W_P$ alone is
$\widetilde H_P=L^2(\R,(\sum_{p\in P}\lambda_p)dt/(2\pi))$.
The isometry
\[
 \Delta_P:\widetilde H_P\longrightarrow H_P,\qquad
 \Delta_Ph=(h)_{p\in P}
\]
has closed image equal to the closure of $T_P(\cA_\zeta)$.
Density follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-carrier} applied to the summable
family for $P$.
When $P$ contains two primes, this image is proper.
A vector supported in just one prime coordinate cannot be diagonal
unless it vanishes, since all $\mu_p$ are equivalent to Lebesgue measure.
The larger space $H_P$ retains the independently addressable prime currents.

\subsection{Operators and their common domain}

For $K=H_P$ or $K=H$, give the algebraic tensor power the
sesquilinear form determined by
\[
 \langle h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n,
 k_1\otimes\cdots\otimes k_n\rangle
 =\prod_{r=1}^n\langle h_r,k_r\rangle.
\]
Every finite tensor sum lies in tensor powers of finite-dimensional
subspaces; orthonormal bases there identify this form with a positive
Euclidean inner product.  Its Hilbert completion is $K^{\otimes n}$;
for $n=0$ it is $\C$.
For a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, the unitary
$U_\sigma$ permutes the tensor factors, sending an elementary tensor
to $h_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}$.
The average
$S_n=(n!)^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_n}U_\sigma$
is self-adjoint and idempotent, by inversion and multiplication in the
finite permutation group.  It is the orthogonal projection onto the
symmetric tensors.
Set $K^{\odot n}=S_nK^{\otimes n}$, $K^{\odot0}=\C$, and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal F(K)=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}K^{\odot n},\qquad
 \mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)
 =\bigoplus_{n\geq0}^{\mathrm{alg}}K^{\odot n}.
 \label{eq:wc-fock-domain}
\end{equation}
Each sector here is a completed Hilbert space.
Finite particle number does not require finite prime support.
The vacuum is $\Omega=(1,0,0,\ldots)$.

For $v\in K$ and $\psi_n\in K^{\odot n}$, define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 a^*(v)\psi_n&=\sqrt{n+1}\,S_{n+1}(v\otimes\psi_n),\\
 a(v)\psi_n&=\sqrt n\,C_v\psi_n\quad(n\geq1),&
 a(v)\Omega&=0.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-creation-definition}
\end{equation}
The bounded contraction $C_v$ on $K^{\otimes n}$ sends
$h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n$ to
$\langle h_1,v\rangle h_2\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n$.
It maps symmetric tensors to symmetric tensors.
Thus $a^*(v)$ is linear in $v$, while $a(v)$ is conjugate-linear in $v$.
These definitions agree with the standard bosonic normalization
\cite[Sections 5.5--5.7, pp.~21--23]{wc-Derezinski}, after reversing
the inner-product convention there.

Regard $b\in B_P$ or $B_{\mathrm{loc}}$ as its vector $j(b)\in K$.
Define three complex-linear families on $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)$:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 J^+(b)&=a^*(j(b)),&
 J^-(b)&=a(j(b^*)),\\
 \mathsf N(b)|_{K^{\odot n}}
 &=\sum_{r=1}^n
 I^{\otimes(r-1)}\otimes\pi(b)\otimes I^{\otimes(n-r)},&
 \mathsf N(b)\Omega&=0.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-current-definition}
\end{equation}
The sum commutes with $S_n$ and therefore restricts to the displayed sector.
The symbol $\mathsf N(b)$ denotes a number current, distinct from the
frequency multiplier in \eqref{eq:wp-moment-domain}.

\begin{theorem}[Arithmetic current relations]
\label{thm:wc-current}
Every operator in \eqref{eq:wc-current-definition} preserves the dense
domain $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)$ and is closable.
On this common domain, the adjoint pairing relations are
\begin{equation}
 J^+(b)^\dagger=J^-(b^*),\qquad
 J^-(b)^\dagger=J^+(b^*),\qquad
 \mathsf N(b)^\dagger=\mathsf N(b^*).
 \label{eq:wc-current-star}
\end{equation}
Here $A^\dagger=B$ means
$\langle A\psi,\eta\rangle=\langle\psi,B\eta\rangle$ on this domain.
It does not identify the full Hilbert adjoint domain with this domain.
All finite products are defined there, and
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 [J^-(b),J^+(d)]&=\omega(bd)I,&
 [J^+(b),J^+(d)]&=[J^-(b),J^-(d)]=0,\\
 [\mathsf N(b),J^+(d)]&=J^+(bd),&
 [\mathsf N(b),J^-(d)]&=-J^-(bd),\\
 [\mathsf N(b),\mathsf N(d)]&=0.&&
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-current-relations}
\end{equation}
In particular, these operators represent the associative $*$-algebra
of finite noncommutative polynomials subject to the displayed linearity,
involution, and commutator relations.  The generator $I$ acts as the identity.
Creation and annihilation are unbounded for nonzero labels.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Orthogonal symmetrization and contraction give the sector bounds
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \|a^*(v)\psi_n\|&\leq\sqrt{n+1}\|v\|\|\psi_n\|,\\
 \|a(v)\psi_n\|&\leq\sqrt n\|v\|\|\psi_n\|,\\
 \|\mathsf N(b)\psi_n\|&\leq n\|\pi(b)\|\|\psi_n\|.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-sector-bounds}
\end{equation}
They prove invariance and finite values on every sector.
The defining tensor inner product gives
$\langle a^*(v)\psi,\eta\rangle=\langle\psi,a(v)\eta\rangle$.
Likewise, the adjoint pairing for multiplication gives the one for
$\mathsf N$.  Replacing $v$ by $j(b)$ or $j(b^*)$ proves
\eqref{eq:wc-current-star}.
Each densely defined operator has a densely defined operator in its
Hilbert adjoint, and is therefore closable.

On $h_1\odot\cdots\odot h_n=S_n(h_1\otimes\cdots\otimes h_n)$,
the contraction formula is
\[
 a(v)(h_1\odot\cdots\odot h_n)
 =\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{r=1}^n\langle h_r,v\rangle
 h_1\odot\cdots\odot\widehat h_r\odot\cdots\odot h_n.
\]
Contracting a newly inserted vector gives $\langle w,v\rangle$.
The other contractions equal those in the reverse composition.
Hence $[a(v),a^*(w)]=\langle w,v\rangle I$.
The creation operators commute by symmetrization, and the contraction
operators commute by their displayed formula.
Applying the sum defining $\mathsf N(b)$ gives
\[
 [\mathsf N(b),a^*(v)]=a^*(\pi(b)v),\qquad
 [\mathsf N(b),a(v)]=-a(\pi(b^*)v).
\]
These equalities hold first on elementary symmetric tensors.
The sector bounds extend each fixed product to the whole input sector,
so they hold on $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(K)$.
Now
$\langle j(d),j(b^*)\rangle=\omega(bd)$ and
$\pi(b^*)j(d^*)=j((bd)^*)$.
This gives every mixed relation in \eqref{eq:wc-current-relations}.
The number currents commute because their one-particle multipliers do.
The adjoint pairing reverses products on the invariant domain, which
gives the stated $*$-representation.
Finally, for $e=v/\|v\|$, the unit vector $e^{\otimes n}$ has creation
norm $\sqrt{n+1}\|v\|$ and annihilation norm $\sqrt n\|v\|$.
These values prove unboundedness.
\end{proof}

The currents $Q(b)=J^+(b)+J^-(b)$ and
$P(b)=i(J^+(b)-J^-(b))$ satisfy
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 Q(b)^\dagger=Q(b^*),\qquad P(b)^\dagger=P(b^*),\\
 [Q(b),Q(d)]=[P(b),P(d)]=0,\qquad
 [Q(b),P(d)]=2i\omega(bd)I.
 \end{gathered}
\]
For a finite prime set, put
$A_P^\pm(f)=J^\pm(T_Pf)$ and $D_P(f)=\mathsf N(T_Pf)$.
The relations become
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 [A_P^-(f),A_P^+(h)]&=W_P(f\star h)I,\\
 [D_P(f),A_P^\pm(h)]&=\pm A_P^\pm(f\star h),\\
 A_P^\pm(f)^\dagger&=A_P^\mp(f^*),&
 D_P(f)^\dagger&=D_P(f^*).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-native-current-relations}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \langle Q(T_Pf)\Omega,Q(T_Ph)\Omega\rangle
 =W_P(h^*\star f).
 \label{eq:wc-vacuum-covariance}
\end{equation}
This establishes the current action and its arithmetic covariance
without changing the test algebra.
The map $f\mapsto Q(T_Pf)$ is linear and respects the involution.
It is not a convolution homomorphism.
For a nonzero self-adjoint $f$, its square on $\Omega$ has nonzero
vacuum and two-particle components, while $Q(T_P(f\star f))\Omega$
has only a one-particle component.

The central identity in the current algebra is distinct from a unit in
$B_{\mathrm{loc}}$.  The latter still has none.
In particular, $\langle\mathsf N(b)\Omega,\Omega\rangle=0$, even when
$\omega(b)>0$.  Thus the vacuum is not a representing vector for
$\omega$ under $b\mapsto\mathsf N(b)$.
That map is also not an associative homomorphism: its two-particle
product has cross terms absent from $\mathsf N(bd)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wc-vacuum-covariance} represents the quadratic form
through current vectors.  It supplies no finite positive unitization
of the arithmetic weight.

\subsection{The two-prime calculation}

Begin with $H_{\{2,3\}}=H_2\oplus H_3$ and
$v_2(f)=(T_2f,0)$, $v_3(f)=(0,T_3f)$.
Their inner products are
\begin{equation}
 \langle v_p(f),v_q(h)\rangle
 =\delta_{pq}W_p(h^*\star f),\qquad p,q\in\{2,3\}.
 \label{eq:wc-two-prime-covariance}
\end{equation}
Thus $v_2(f)+v_3(f)$ realizes the sum of both full local Euler factors.
For $f=g_a$, where
$g_a(u)=(4\pi a)^{-1/2}e^{-u^2/(4a)}$ and $a>0$, its squared norm is
\begin{equation}
 q_2(a)+q_3(a),\qquad
 q_p(a)=\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{2\pi a}}
 \sum_{m\geq1}p^{-m/2}
 \left(1-e^{-m^2(\log p)^2/(8a)}\right)>0.
 \label{eq:wc-gaussian-norm}
\end{equation}
This follows from $g_a^*\star g_a=g_{2a}$ and the displayed value of
$W_p(g_{2a})$.  Truncation after $M$ powers has error at most
\[
 \frac{\log p}{\sqrt{2\pi a}}
 \frac{p^{-(M+1)/2}}{1-p^{-1/2}}.
\]

Let $\Omega$ have norm one in particle number zero.
For $p=2,3$, let $\iota_p(T_pf)$ be the Schwartz label in $B_{\{2,3\}}$
whose $p$ coordinate is $\widehat f$ and whose other coordinate is zero.
Define $A_p^\pm(f)=J^\pm(\iota_p(T_pf))$ on
$\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H_{\{2,3\}})$.
The current definitions give, for $p,q\in\{2,3\}$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_p^+(f)\Omega&=v_p(f),& A_p^-(f)\Omega&=0,\\
 A_p^-(f)A_q^+(h)\Omega
 &=\delta_{pq}W_p(f\star h)\Omega,&
 A_p^+(f)A_q^+(h)\Omega
 &=\sqrt2\,S_2(v_p(f)\otimes v_q(h)).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-two-prime-action}
\end{equation}
Here $S_2(x\otimes y)=(x\otimes y+y\otimes x)/2$.
For distinct primes the squared norm of the last vector is
$W_p(f^*\star f)W_q(h^*\star h)$.
For $p=q$ and $f=h$, it is $2W_p(f^*\star f)^2$.
These are the actual two-particle products, including their normalization.
The factor $\sqrt2$ above comes from the particle-sector normalization
in \eqref{eq:wc-creation-definition}; the orthogonality of distinct prime
coordinates removes the cross contractions.
For the Gaussian, the vectors
$e_p=v_p(g_a)/\sqrt{q_p(a)}$ are orthonormal.
The normalized occupation vectors
\[
 |r,s\rangle=
 \frac{a^*(e_2)^r a^*(e_3)^s}{\sqrt{r!s!}}\Omega
 \qquad(r,s\geq0)
\]
therefore obey
\[
 A_2^+(g_a)|r,s\rangle
 =\sqrt{q_2(a)}\sqrt{r+1}|r+1,s\rangle,\qquad
 A_3^-(g_a)|r,s\rangle
 =\sqrt{q_3(a)}\sqrt{s}|r,s-1\rangle.
\]
The second expression is zero when $s=0$.

\subsection{Coherent prime inclusions and the infinite domain}

For finite $P\subset Q$, let $e_{PQ}:B_P\to B_Q$ and
$i_{PQ}:H_P\to H_Q$ insert zero in the new prime coordinates.
The first is a $*$-homomorphism and preserves $\omega$.
The second is an isometry and obeys
$i_{PQ}\pi_P(b)=\pi_Q(e_{PQ}b)i_{PQ}$.
Both maps compose exactly along $P\subset Q\subset R$.

\begin{theorem}[The prime current system]
\label{thm:wc-system}
The isometries
\[
 \Gamma(i_{PQ})=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}i_{PQ}^{\otimes n}:
 \mathcal F(H_P)\longrightarrow\mathcal F(H_Q)
\]
preserve the vacuum and intertwine $J^\pm(b)$ and $\mathsf N(b)$
with the currents labeled by $e_{PQ}b$.
They compose exactly.  Their Hilbert completion is $\mathcal F(H)$.
The same operator formulas give all local currents on its common
domain $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)$.

For an arbitrary vector $v=(v_p)\in H$, let $v^{(P)}$ be its finite
prime truncation.  Then, for every $\psi\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)$,
\begin{equation}
 a^*(v^{(P)})\psi\longrightarrow a^*(v)\psi,\qquad
 a(v^{(P)})\psi\longrightarrow a(v)\psi.
 \label{eq:wc-infinite-currents}
\end{equation}
Conversely, convergence of $a^*(v^{(P)})\Omega$ for a prescribed family
$(v_p)$ requires $\sum_p\|v_p\|^2<\infty$.
Thus square summability is the exact domain for creation limits
whose common domain contains the vacuum.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Tensor powers of an isometry commute with symmetrization and preserve
tensor inner products.  The creation and contraction formulas therefore
give both intertwiners.  The one-particle multiplication intertwiner
gives the number-current identity after summing over tensor positions.
Composition follows on each tensor power, including particle number zero.

Finite prime truncations converge strongly to the identity on $H$.
Their tensor powers converge on elementary tensors by a telescoping
expansion, and hence on each completed tensor power by their norm bound.
They also commute with $S_n$.
Truncation in particle number proves density of the union of the embedded
Fock spaces in $\mathcal F(H)$.

If $\psi$ has particle number at most $N$, the sector bounds give
\[
 \|(a^*(v^{(P)})-a^*(v))\psi\|
 \leq\sqrt{N+1}\|v^{(P)}-v\|\|\psi\|.
\]
The annihilation estimate has $\sqrt N$ in place of $\sqrt{N+1}$.
This proves both limits.  Conversely,
$a^*(v^{(P)})\Omega=v^{(P)}$.
The squared distances of two such vectors are sums of squared norms
of their differing prime coordinates.  The Cauchy criterion gives the
necessary and sufficient square summability.
\end{proof}

The theorem yields currents with infinite prime support.
For example, choose a unit vector $u_p\in H_p$ for every prime.
Then $v_p=p^{-1}u_p$ has infinite support and
$\sum_p\|v_p\|^2\leq\sum_{n\geq2}n^{-2}<\infty$.
Its current is defined by \eqref{eq:wc-infinite-currents}.
For labels $b,d\in H$, pointwise conjugation is still an isometry and
$\int_X|bd|\,d\nu\leq\|b\|_H\|d\|_H$.
Thus $J^+(b)=a^*(b)$ and $J^-(b)=a(b^*)$ extend the Heisenberg
relations to all Hilbert labels with central pairing $\int_Xbd\,d\nu$.
Number currents also extend to each bounded measurable multiplier $b$,
using \eqref{eq:wc-current-definition} and
$\|\mathsf N(b)\psi_n\|\leq n\|b\|_\infty\|\psi_n\|$.
The two extensions have different label spaces.

\begin{proposition}[The unweighted arithmetic diagonal]
\label{prop:wc-diagonal-barrier}
For $f\in L^2(\R,du)$, define the formal prime family
$\delta f=(\widehat f)_p$, using the $L^2$ Fourier transform.
Every coordinate belongs to $H_p$.
Its finite norm domain is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \{f\in L^2(\R,du):\delta f\in H\}=\{0\}.
 \label{eq:wc-diagonal-domain}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for each nonzero $f\in\cA_\zeta$, neither
$A_P^+(f)\Omega$ nor $Q(T_Pf)\Omega$ converges as $P$ increases to
all primes.  No operators agreeing with these limits can have a common
domain containing $\Omega$.
For $P\subsetneq Q$, there is no isometry between the minimal
arithmetic Hilbert spaces that sends every $T_Pf$ to $T_Qf$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The bound on $\lambda_p$ and Plancherel's identity put every coordinate
in $H_p$.  Monotone summation gives
\[
 \sum_p\|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2
 =\sum_p\sum_{m\geq1}\frac{\log p}{p^{m/2}}
 \|f-\tau_{m\log p}f\|_2^2.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-prime-limit} proves that this equals infinity for
every nonzero $f\in L^2$.
All terms are nonnegative, so exhaustion by finite sets of whole prime
factors has the same supremum as exhaustion by finite prime powers.
This proves \eqref{eq:wc-diagonal-domain}.
Both current vectors on the vacuum equal the finite truncation of
$\delta f$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:wc-system} excludes their convergence.
Finally, for nonzero $f\in\cA_\zeta$,
\[
 \|T_Qf\|^2-\|T_Pf\|^2
 =\sum_{p\in Q\setminus P}W_p(f^*\star f)>0.
\]
An isometry preserving those vectors would require equality of the norms.
\end{proof}

For $P\subsetneq Q$, the zero-insertion maps obey
\[
 T_Qf=e_{PQ}T_Pf+e_{Q\setminus P,Q}T_{Q\setminus P}f.
\]
The two terms have orthogonal prime support, and their currents add.

\begin{proposition}[Infinite arithmetic multiplication and number currents]
\label{prop:wc-infinite-multiplication}
There is an injective $*$-homomorphism
\[
 \rho:\cA_\zeta\longrightarrow\mathcal B(H),\qquad
 (\rho(f)h)_p(t)=\widehat f(t)h_p(t),\qquad
 \|\rho(f)\|=\|\widehat f\|_\infty.
\]
More generally, every $b\in L^\infty(X,\nu)$ acts by a bounded multiplier
$M_b$ on all of $H$, with $M_b^*=M_{\overline b}$ and
$\|M_b\|=\|b\|_{L^\infty(X,\nu)}$.
For particle number $n$, put
\[
 N_n(b)=\sum_{r=1}^n
 I^{\otimes(r-1)}\otimes M_b\otimes I^{\otimes(n-r)}
 \quad\hbox{on }H^{\odot n},\qquad N_0(b)=0.
\]
Its direct sum $\overline{\mathsf N}(b)$ is closed on the maximal domain
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D(\overline{\mathsf N}(b))=
 \left\{\psi\in\mathcal F(H):
             \sum_{n\geq0}\|N_n(b)\psi_n\|^2<\infty\right\}.
 \label{eq:wc-number-maximal-domain}
\end{equation}
Its Hilbert adjoint is $\overline{\mathsf N}(\overline b)$, and
$\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)$ is an invariant core.
For real $b$, the operator is self-adjoint.
In particular, for $b_p(t)=\widehat f(t)$ define
$D_{\infty,n}(f)=N_n(b)$ and $D_\infty(f)=\overline{\mathsf N}(b)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D(D_\infty(f))=
 \left\{\psi\in\mathcal F(H):
       \sum_{n\geq0}\|D_{\infty,n}(f)\psi_n\|^2<\infty\right\},
 \qquad D_\infty(f)^*=D_\infty(f^*).
 \label{eq:wc-infinite-number-domain}
\end{equation}
For $f=f^*$ this is a self-adjoint operator.
On the common finite-particle domain, the prime cutoffs satisfy
$D_P(f)\psi\to D_\infty(f)\psi$.
Here $D_P(f)$ acts on $\mathcal F(H)$ through its prime-supported
bounded multiplier.
For every $v\in H$, the same domain supports
\begin{equation}
 [D_\infty(f),a^*(v)]=a^*(\rho(f)v),\qquad
 [D_\infty(f),a(v)]=-a(\rho(f^*)v).
 \label{eq:wc-infinite-mixed}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a bounded measurable $b$, integration gives
$\|M_bh\|\leq\|b\|_\infty\|h\|$ and
$\langle M_bh,k\rangle=\langle h,M_{\overline b}k\rangle$.
Since both multipliers act on all of $H$, this identifies their full
Hilbert adjoints.
To obtain the reverse norm bound, take a nonzero vector supported in a
finite-measure subset of
$\{|b|>\|b\|_\infty-\epsilon\}$ and normalize it.
The measure is sigma-finite, so such a subset exists whenever the
essential supremum exceeds $\epsilon$.
This proves the asserted norm formula.
For $b_p=\widehat f$, continuity and positivity of $\lambda_p$ almost
everywhere identify the essential supremum with
$\|\widehat f\|_\infty$.
The Fourier product and involution identities prove that $\rho$ is a
$*$-homomorphism; Fourier inversion proves injectivity.

The sum defining $N_n(b)$ commutes with symmetrization, has norm at most
$n\|b\|_\infty$, and satisfies
$N_n(b)^*=N_n(\overline b)$ on the completed sector.
If $\psi^{(j)}\to\psi$ and
$\overline{\mathsf N}(b)\psi^{(j)}\to\eta$ in Fock space, convergence
in each sector gives $\eta_n=N_n(b)\psi_n$.
The vector $\eta$ is square summable, proving both membership in
\eqref{eq:wc-number-maximal-domain} and closedness.
If $\eta$ is in the Hilbert adjoint domain, testing against a vector
supported in its $n$th sector forces the $n$th coordinate of its adjoint
image to be $N_n(\overline b)\eta_n$.
Square summability of that image gives precisely
$\eta\in\mathcal D(\overline{\mathsf N}(\overline b))$.
Conversely, that summability and Cauchy--Schwarz justify summing the
sector adjoint identities against every vector in the original domain.
This proves the full adjoint formula in both directions.
When $b$ is real, the two maximal domains and actions coincide, proving
self-adjointness.

Particle truncation approximates both a vector in
\eqref{eq:wc-number-maximal-domain} and its image, so the finite-particle
domain is a graph core.  Each operator preserves it because it preserves
every sector.
The bound $n\|b\|_\infty$ supplies a sufficient number-moment condition,
but the maximal domain is the displayed sum of actual sector norms.
For example, if $M_b$ has a nonzero kernel, every particle sector
formed from that kernel lies in the kernel of its number current; arbitrary
square-summable sums of those sectors belong to the maximal domain
without any particle-number moment assumption.

Prime truncations of $M_b$ converge strongly on $H$, with common bound
$\|b\|_\infty$, because their squared error on $h$ is bounded by
$\|b\|_\infty^2\sum_{p\notin P}\|h_p\|^2$.
The tensor-position sums converge strongly on each sector, first on
elementary tensors and then on its completion by the uniform norm bound.
Only finitely many sectors occur in a finite-particle vector.
This proves the asserted number-current limit.
Finally, applying $N_n(b)$ to a symmetrized tensor and comparing the
terms before and after one creation or contraction gives
\[
 [\mathsf N(b),a^*(v)]=a^*(M_bv),\qquad
 [\mathsf N(b),a(v)]=-a(M_{\overline b}v).
\]
The sector bounds extend these identities to completed sectors.
Specializing $b_p=\widehat f$ gives \eqref{eq:wc-infinite-mixed}.
\end{proof}

The operator $\overline{\mathsf N}(1)$ is particle number: its $n$th
sector is multiplication by $n$.
It is distinct from the frequency multiplier $Nh(t)=th(t)$ of
\eqref{eq:wp-moment-domain}.
The constant one is a bounded multiplier, although it is not an element
of the nonunital arithmetic algebra or its finite-weight ideal.

Thus the unweighted diagonal defines a number current for every arithmetic
test.  Its creation vector exists only for the zero test, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:wc-diagonal-barrier}.


\section{Weighted arithmetic diagonals}
\label{sec:wc-weighted-diagonal}

The multiplication representation $\rho$ acts on every prime coordinate
without requiring its multiplier to be a vector of $H$.
A creation operator has a different requirement: its label must have
finite Hilbert norm.  We determine exactly which scalar weights make a
common arithmetic function into such a label, while keeping every measure
$\mu_p$ unchanged.

For complex numbers $\alpha=(\alpha_p)_{p\in\mathcal P}$ and
$f\in L^2(\R,du)$, consider the coordinate family
\begin{equation}
 V_\alpha(f)=(\alpha_p\widehat f)_p,\qquad
 \mathcal D(V_\alpha)=
 \left\{f\in L^2(\R):
       \sum_p|\alpha_p|^2\|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2<\infty\right\}.
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-diagonal}
\end{equation}
The Fourier transform here is its $L^2$ extension, with
$\|f\|_2^2=\int_\R|\widehat f(t)|^2dt/(2\pi)$.
Every coordinate is well defined because $\lambda_p\leq4C_p$.
The map is complex-linear on its stated domain.

\begin{example}[Complex coefficients on two prime factors]
\label{ex:wc-complex-diagonal}
Let $f=g_{1/2}$, $h=if$, and put
\[
 (\alpha_2,\alpha_3)=(1+i,2-i),\qquad
 (\beta_2,\beta_3)=(2-i,-1+2i),
\]
with all other coefficients zero.  Set
$q_p=\|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2>0$, whose complete Euler series is
\eqref{eq:wc-gaussian-norm} with $a=1/2$.
Since the inner product is linear in its first argument,
\begin{equation}
 \langle V_\alpha(f),V_\beta(h)\rangle
 =-i\sum_{p=2,3}\alpha_p\overline{\beta_p}q_p
 =(3-i)q_2+(-3+4i)q_3.
 \label{eq:wc-complex-covariance}
\end{equation}
The expression with $\beta_p$ in place of $\overline{\beta_p}$ would be
$(1-3i)q_2+5q_3$.  The displayed covariance minus this expression has
imaginary part $2q_2+4q_3>0$, so conjugation cannot be omitted.

Multiplication of $V_\alpha(h)$ by $\rho(f)$ gives the coordinates
$i\alpha_p e^{-t^2}$, which are exactly those of
$V_\alpha(f\star h)$.
To compare products of two labels, use the actual pointwise algebra
$\mathscr S(\R)\oplus\mathscr S(\R)$.
In that algebra,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 (V_\alpha(f)V_\alpha(h)-V_\alpha(f\star h))_2
   &=(-1-i)e^{-t^2},\\
 (V_\alpha(f)V_\alpha(h)-V_\alpha(f\star h))_3
   &=(3+i)e^{-t^2},\\
 (V_\alpha(f)^*-V_\alpha(f^*))_{2,3}
   &=(-2i,2i)e^{-t^2/2}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-complex-defects}
\end{equation}
These values follow from
$i(\alpha_p^2-\alpha_p)$ and
$\overline{\alpha_p}-\alpha_p$, respectively.
The module identity therefore holds although multiplication and the
involution are not preserved by this weighted diagonal.
In contrast, the coefficients $(1,0)$ preserve both operations and retain
exactly the complete factor at $2$.  They omit every power of $3$.
\end{example}

The two-prime computation identifies the operations, but convergence over
all primes requires information uniform in the prime-power index.
Large translations provide it without an estimate for the number of primes.

\begin{theorem}[Exact domain of a weighted diagonal]
\label{thm:wc-weighted-domain}
For each fixed $f\in L^2(\R)$,
\begin{equation}
 \left|\frac{\|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2}{2C_p}-\|f\|_2^2\right|
 \leq\sup_{v\geq\log p}|\langle f,\tau_vf\rangle_2|
 \longrightarrow0\qquad(p\to\infty).
 \label{eq:wc-local-asymptotic}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if
$A_\alpha=\sum_p|\alpha_p|^2C_p$, then
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D(V_\alpha)=
 \begin{cases}
 L^2(\R),& A_\alpha<\infty,\\
 \{0\},& A_\alpha=\infty.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:wc-domain-dichotomy}
\end{equation}
In the first case $V_\alpha:L^2(\R)\to H$ is bounded and
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \|V_\alpha f\|_H^2
 &=\int_\R|\widehat f(t)|^2\lambda_\alpha(t)\,\frac{dt}{2\pi},\\
 \lambda_\alpha(t)&=\sum_p|\alpha_p|^2\lambda_p(t),\qquad
 \|V_\alpha\|^2=\|\lambda_\alpha\|_\infty\leq4A_\alpha.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-norm}
\end{equation}
The density is continuous, and $V_\alpha$ is injective unless every
$\alpha_p$ is zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The translation identity and Plancherel give, for ordinary $L^2$ functions,
\[
 \|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2
 =\sum_{m\geq1}c_{p,m}
    \bigl(2\|f\|_2^2-2\operatorname{Re}
                   \langle f,\tau_{m\log p}f\rangle_2\bigr).
\]
The series converges because its summands are bounded in absolute value
by $4c_{p,m}\|f\|_2^2$.
Divide by $2C_p$.
The coefficients $c_{p,m}/C_p$ are nonnegative and sum to one,
while $m\log p\geq\log p$ for every $m\geq1$.
This proves the inequality in \eqref{eq:wc-local-asymptotic}.
The correlation decay proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-prime-limit} makes
its right side tend to zero.  The bound is uniform over all powers of
the given sufficiently large prime; the required lower bound on the
prime may depend on $f$.  No uniformity over the unit sphere of $L^2$
or pointwise convergence of the densities $\lambda_p/C_p$ is asserted.

If $f\ne0$, choose $p_0(f)$ such that the right side is at most
$\|f\|_2^2/2$ for $p\geq p_0(f)$.
It follows that
\[
 C_p\|f\|_2^2\leq\|\widehat f\|_{H_p}^2
                 \leq4C_p\|f\|_2^2\qquad(p\geq p_0(f)).
\]
The upper bound in fact holds for every prime.
After multiplication by $|\alpha_p|^2$, the upper bound proves that
$A_\alpha<\infty$ suffices for every $f$.
The lower bound proves that membership of a single nonzero $f$
forces the weighted coefficient tail to be summable.
There are only finitely many smaller primes, and each $\alpha_p$ is a
finite complex number; their contribution is finite.
This proves \eqref{eq:wc-domain-dichotomy}.

When $A_\alpha<\infty$, the bound
$|\alpha_p|^2\lambda_p\leq4|\alpha_p|^2C_p$
gives uniform convergence of the density series and its continuity.
Tonelli's theorem proves the integral identity.
Plancherel makes the Fourier transform onto $L^2(dt/(2\pi))$,
so the norm squared is the essential supremum of $\lambda_\alpha$:
for the lower bound take a nonzero function supported in a finite-measure
part of a set where the density is within any prescribed positive error
of that supremum.
If $\alpha_{p_1}\ne0$, then
$\lambda_\alpha\geq|\alpha_{p_1}|^2\lambda_{p_1}>0$
almost everywhere, proving injectivity.  If every coefficient is zero,
the map and density are zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Prime powers as diagonal weights]
\label{cor:wc-weighted-exponent}
For $s\in\C$, define $\alpha_p=p^{-s}=e^{-s\log p}$.
Then
\[
 \mathcal D(V_\alpha)=L^2(\R)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Re}s>\frac14.
\]
At the boundary $\operatorname{Re}s=1/4$, and throughout the complementary
closed half-plane, the domain is $\{0\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Put $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s$ and $\beta=2\sigma+1/2$.
The coefficient sum is exactly
\[
 A_\alpha=\sum_p\frac{(\log p)p^{-\beta}}{1-p^{-1/2}}.
\]
If $\beta>1$, its terms are bounded by
$(1-2^{-1/2})^{-1}(\log p)p^{-\beta}$.
Comparison with $\sum_{n\geq2}(\log n)n^{-\beta}$ proves convergence.
For the reverse implication, the factorial identity in
Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-prime-limit} proves divergence of
$\sum_{n\geq2}\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)/n$.
The contribution of powers with exponent at least two is finite, since
\[
 \sum_p\sum_{m\geq2}\frac{\log p}{p^m}
 =\sum_p\frac{\log p}{p(p-1)}
 \leq2\sum_{n\geq2}\frac{\log n}{n^2}<\infty.
\]
Hence $\sum_p(\log p)/p$ diverges.
For $\beta\leq1$, each summand of $A_\alpha$ is at least
$(\log p)/p$, proving divergence, including equality at the boundary.
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:wc-weighted-domain}.
\end{proof}

The local Euler coefficients have remained
$c_{p,m}=(\log p)p^{-m/2}$ throughout.
The squared diagonal weight multiplies a complete factor by
$p^{-2\operatorname{Re}s}$, so its quadratic form has coefficients
$(\log p)p^{-m/2-2\operatorname{Re}s}$.
This is a different modification from replacing $n^{-1/2}$ by
$n^{-\sigma}$ for every prime power $n$.
The two convergence boundaries concern these two specified operations.

\subsection{Module maps and algebra maps}

The weighted diagonal has a natural module property.
For $a\in\cA_\zeta$ and $f\in L^2(\R)$, convolution $a\star f$
belongs to $L^2$ and
$\widehat{a\star f}=\widehat a\widehat f$ there.
Indeed, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 |(a\star f)(u)|^2\leq\|a\|_1
                  \int_\R|a(v)||f(u-v)|^2\,dv.
\]
Tonelli and integration in $u$ give
$\|a\star f\|_2\leq\|a\|_1\|f\|_2$.
The Fourier identity follows first for Schwartz functions by Fubini
and then by density.
For $A_\alpha<\infty$, coordinatewise multiplication therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \rho(a)V_\alpha(f)=V_\alpha(a\star f).
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-module}
\end{equation}
Thus $V_\alpha$ intertwines the ordinary $L^2$ convolution action and
the bounded arithmetic multiplication action on $H$.
This identity contains only one weighted label.
A product of two weighted labels introduces two factors of $\alpha_p$.

To discuss that product, let
$\mathfrak B^\Pi=\prod_{p\in\mathcal P}\mathscr S(\R)$,
with componentwise multiplication, componentwise conjugation, and the
product locally convex topology.
An identity would have the constant one in each Schwartz coordinate,
which is impossible; the algebra is nonunital.
Completeness holds coordinatewise: a Cauchy sequence in the Schwartz
seminorms has uniform limits for every weighted derivative; the
fundamental theorem of calculus on compact intervals identifies them
as the derivatives of one smooth function, and the seminorm bounds
pass to that limit.  The finite prime families are dense in this
complete product for the topology inherited from the product.  Thus the
same finite-support algebra completes to $\mathfrak B^\Pi$ when equipped
with that weaker topology, rather than the locally convex direct-sum
topology used for $B_{\mathrm{loc}}$ above.  Its Hilbert completion $H$
uses yet another topology, given by the quadratic form.
For any complex family $\alpha$, the formula
\[
 \mathcal T_\alpha:\cA_\zeta\longrightarrow\mathfrak B^\Pi,
 \qquad (\mathcal T_\alpha f)_p=\alpha_p\widehat f
\]
defines a linear map.
It agrees with $V_\alpha$ wherever the latter belongs to $H$;
no product on all of $H$ is being defined.
A useful algebra inside $H$ is the square-integrable ideal
$\mathfrak n_\Phi=H\cap L^\infty(X,\nu)$ from
Proposition~\ref{prop:wc-weight}.
It is closed under multiplication and involution because
$\|bd\|_H\leq\|b\|_\infty\|d\|_H$.

\begin{proposition}[The coefficient criterion for algebra maps]
\label{prop:wc-weighted-algebra}
The map $\mathcal T_\alpha$ is multiplicative if and only if
$\alpha_p^2=\alpha_p$ for every prime.
It preserves the involution if and only if every $\alpha_p$ is real.
It is therefore a $*$-homomorphism into $\mathfrak B^\Pi$ precisely when
$\alpha_p\in\{0,1\}$ for every prime.

The formula maps every test in $\cA_\zeta$ into the actual algebra
$\mathfrak n_\Phi$ if and only if
\[
 \sup_p|\alpha_p|<\infty,
 \qquad \sum_p|\alpha_p|^2C_p<\infty.
\]
It is a $*$-homomorphism with that target precisely when
$\alpha=1_E$ for a set of primes $E$ with
$\sum_{p\in E}C_p<\infty$.
Such a map is injective when $E$ is nonempty.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $f,h\in\cA_\zeta$ the product difference in prime coordinate $p$ is
$(\alpha_p^2-\alpha_p)\widehat f\widehat h$.
The difference between conjugation and the image of the involution is
$(\overline{\alpha_p}-\alpha_p)\overline{\widehat f}$.
Taking $f=h=g_a$ gives a transform that is nowhere zero on $\R$.
Thus the displayed scalar conditions are necessary, and the same
coordinate formulas prove their sufficiency.
The only complex roots of $z^2=z$ are $0$ and $1$.

If both summability and boundedness hold, Theorem~\ref{thm:wc-weighted-domain}
gives the Hilbert condition, and
\[
 \|\mathcal T_\alpha f\|_{L^\infty(X,\nu)}
 =\left(\sup_p|\alpha_p|\right)\|\widehat f\|_\infty
\]
gives the bounded-function condition.
The equality uses equivalence of each $\mu_p$ with Lebesgue measure.
Conversely, apply both requirements to a single nonzero Gaussian:
its Hilbert membership forces the summability condition, and its nonzero
Fourier supremum forces boundedness of $\alpha$.
Combining with the scalar product criterion proves the assertion for
$\mathfrak n_\Phi$.  One retained prime coordinate and Fourier inversion
prove injectivity when $E\ne\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

Infinite sets $E$ satisfying this criterion exist.
There are arbitrarily large primes, and
$C_p=(\log p)/(\sqrt p-1)\to0$.
Choose inductively distinct increasing primes $p_j$ with $C_{p_j}\leq2^{-j}$;
then $E=\{p_j:j\geq1\}$ is infinite and $\sum_{p\in E}C_p\leq1$.
For this idempotent restriction, $\mathcal T_{1_E}g$ also belongs to
$\mathfrak m_\Phi$, since
\[
 \int_X|\mathcal T_{1_E}g|\,d\nu
 \leq4\left(\sum_{p\in E}C_p\right)
          \int_\R|\widehat g(t)|\,\frac{dt}{2\pi}<\infty.
\]
Its positive functional is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(\mathcal T_{1_E}g)=
 \sum_{p\in E}\sum_{m\geq1}\frac{\log p}{p^{m/2}}
 \bigl(2g(0)-g(m\log p)-g(-m\log p)\bigr).
 \label{eq:wc-sparse-functional}
\end{equation}
Here $\Phi$ denotes the linear integral on $\mathfrak m_\Phi$.
The restriction keeps all powers of every selected prime, with their
original coefficients and diagonal compensations.
It omits all powers of every prime outside $E$, and contains neither
polar nor archimedean terms.
The failure of the full diagonal $E=\mathcal P$ is thus compatible with
infinite, faithful $*$-homomorphisms of this sparse kind.
For $\alpha_p=p^{-s}$ in the summable half-plane, every coefficient is
nonzero and has modulus less than one, so multiplicativity fails.
Preservation of involution for all primes holds precisely for real $s$:
if $\operatorname{Im}s\ne0$, real values at both $2$ and $3$ would force
$\log2/\log3$ to be rational, contrary to unique prime factorization.

\subsection{Weighted current relations}

Let
$\mathcal E=\{\alpha:\sum_p|\alpha_p|^2C_p<\infty\}$.
For $\alpha\in\mathcal E$ and $f\in\cA_\zeta$, define operators on
$\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)$ by
\begin{equation}
 A_\alpha^+(f)=a^*(V_\alpha(f)),\qquad
 A_\alpha^-(f)=a(V_{\overline\alpha}(f^*)).
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-current}
\end{equation}
Both families are complex-linear in $f$ and in $\alpha$.
The second linearity uses the conjugate-linearity of annihilation twice:
$V_{\overline\alpha}(f^*)$ is conjugate-linear in each of these labels.
The operators preserve the displayed common domain and are closable by
the sector estimates and adjoint pairing already proved.
For a family $z$ with $\sum_p|z_p|C_p<\infty$, write
$W_z(g)=\sum_p z_pW_p(g)$.
This defines a finite linear functional on $\cA_\zeta$ because
$|W_p(g)|\leq4C_p\int|\widehat g(t)|dt/(2\pi)$.
It need not be positive for complex $z$.

\begin{proposition}[Module action and adjoint pairing for weighted currents]
\label{prop:wc-weighted-current}
For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal E$ and $a,f,h\in\cA_\zeta$, the
following identities hold on $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{fin}}(H)$:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_\alpha^\pm(f)^\dagger&=A_{\overline\alpha}^{\mp}(f^*),\\
 [A_\alpha^-(f),A_\beta^+(h)]&=W_{\alpha\beta}(f\star h)I,\\
 [D_\infty(a),A_\alpha^\pm(f)]&=\pm A_\alpha^\pm(a\star f).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-current-relations}
\end{equation}
Creation operators commute with each other, as do annihilation operators.
If $Q_\alpha(f)=A_\alpha^+(f)+A_\alpha^-(f)$, then
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_\alpha(f)^\dagger&=Q_{\overline\alpha}(f^*),\\
 \langle Q_\alpha(f)\Omega,Q_\beta(h)\Omega\rangle
 &=W_{\alpha\overline\beta}(h^*\star f).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:wc-weighted-vacuum}
\end{equation}
For fixed $\alpha$, pairing $A_\alpha^+(f)$ instead with
$A_{\overline\alpha}^-(f)=a(V_\alpha(f^*))$ gives a field family
that respects the involution in $f$; its annihilation part is
conjugate-linear in the weight $\alpha$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Weighted Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$\sum_p|\alpha_p\beta_p|C_p\leq
(A_\alpha A_\beta)^{1/2}$, so every functional in the formulas is defined.
The adjoint pairing of $a^*(v)$ and $a(v)$ gives
\[
 A_\alpha^+(f)^\dagger=a(V_\alpha(f))
 =A_{\overline\alpha}^-(f^*),
\]
and the other equality follows by interchanging creation and annihilation.
The basic commutator is
\[
 [a(v),a^*(w)]=\langle w,v\rangle I.
\]
With $v=V_{\overline\alpha}(f^*)$ and $w=V_\beta(h)$, its scalar is
\[
 \sum_p\beta_p\alpha_p
     \int_\R\widehat h(t)\widehat f(t)\,d\mu_p(t)
 =W_{\alpha\beta}(f\star h).
\]
For the mixed annihilation relation, the already proved number-current
identity gives
\[
 [D_\infty(a),a(V_{\overline\alpha}(f^*))]
 =-a(\rho(a^*)V_{\overline\alpha}(f^*))
 =-a(V_{\overline\alpha}((a\star f)^*)),
\]
where \eqref{eq:wc-weighted-module} and the convolution involution were used.
The creation relation uses the same module identity without conjugation.
Each equality is first a sector identity between bounded maps with fixed
input particle number; the sector estimates extend it to every vector
in that completed sector and hence to the finite-particle domain.
Finally, annihilation kills $\Omega$, so
$Q_\alpha(f)\Omega=V_\alpha(f)$.
The first-linear inner product gives
$\sum_p\alpha_p\overline{\beta_p}W_p(h^*\star f)$,
which proves the covariance and its conjugations.
The final assertion is the same adjoint pairing with $\alpha$ held fixed.
\end{proof}

The weighted currents and the bounded arithmetic number currents therefore
act together on one invariant dense domain.
Their covariance uses the modified prime coefficients
$|\alpha_p|^2c_{p,m}$.
This supplies positive infinite families of operators with a precise
module action, while the equality with the full Weil functional remains
a separate question about all of its local terms.


\section{Finite matrix kernels and positive trace}
\label{sec:weil-formal-kernels}

A scalar obtained by closing an operator kernel need not define a
positive pairing.  This distinction can already be seen on finite
matrices inside the formal operator algebra.
For this comparison, use the underlying field $\C$ with the discrete
topology and put
\[
 H_{\mathrm{for}}=\C[[x,y]]/\C.
\]
Enumerate the nonconstant monomials as $e_0,e_1,\ldots$ in increasing
total degree.  The formal topology makes $H_{\mathrm{for}}$ the product
$\C^{\mathbb N}$ of discrete coefficient spaces.  Write
$\epsilon^j(h)=h_j$ for its coefficient functionals.
A continuous endomorphism has a matrix with finitely many nonzero entries
in each row.  Indeed, every output coefficient must depend on finitely
many input coefficients.  Conversely, this condition makes every finite
output projection continuous.  Denote this algebra by $\mathscr E$.
Its operator topology requires agreement of each entire row in a fixed
finite set of output rows.

Put $E_{ij}=e_i\otimes\epsilon^j$ and let $\mathscr F_0$ be their
finite complex span.  These are the matrices with finitely many nonzero
entries in total.  They satisfy
\[
 E_{ij}E_{k\ell}=\delta_{jk}E_{i\ell},\qquad
 E_{ij}^*=E_{ji}.
\]
Conjugating coefficients defines the displayed involution on
$\mathscr F_0$.  This algebra has no unit, since each of its elements
annihilates all but finitely many basis vectors.
It is dense in $\mathscr E$: retaining finitely many whole rows of a
row-finite matrix gives a matrix in $\mathscr F_0$ with those rows unchanged.

The same matrix units act on $D_0=\C^{(\mathbb N)}$, the finite sequences,
inside the Hilbert space $\ell^2(\mathbb N)$ over the usual complex field.
Its inner product is $\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_jv_j\overline{w_j}$.
Every finite matrix preserves $D_0$, and conjugate transpose is its
Hilbert adjoint restricted to $D_0$.  Thus this comparison preserves the
algebra and involution on $\mathscr F_0$.  It does not identify the two
topologies or their completions.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[The positive finite-matrix trace]
\label{prop:weil-matrix-trace}
Define $\tau(B)=\sum_i B_{ii}$ on $\mathscr F_0$.
It is a positive linear functional with zero GNS null space.
Its GNS completion is $\ell^2(\mathbb N\times\mathbb N)$, with $[B]$
identified with its coefficient array $(B_{ij})$.
The GNS action is left matrix multiplication, and
\[
 \|\pi_\tau(A)\|=\|A\|_{\ell^2\to\ell^2}
 \qquad(A\in\mathscr F_0).
\]
Nevertheless, $\tau$ has no positive extension of finite mass to
$\mathscr F_0^+=\C I+\mathscr F_0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
All sums in the algebraic calculation are finite, and
\[
 \tau(C^*B)=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}\overline{C_{ij}},\qquad
 \tau(B^*B)=\sum_{i,j}|B_{ij}|^2.
\]
The second expression vanishes exactly when $B=0$.
For a norm-Cauchy sequence of finite arrays, every coefficient has a
limit $b_{ij}$.  A common norm bound bounds every finite sum of
$|b_{ij}|^2$, so the limit array is square-summable.
Pass to the coefficientwise limit in a finite sum of
$|(B_n)_{ij}-(B_m)_{ij}|^2$, then take the supremum over the finite sums.
The Cauchy estimate gives $\|B_n-b\|\to0$.
Conversely, finite rectangular truncations of a square-summable array
converge in this norm.  This identifies the quotient and completion.
Multiplication by $A$ acts separately on each column, so
\[
 \|AB\|^2=\sum_j\|A B_{\bullet j}\|_{\ell^2}^2
       \leq\|A\|_{\ell^2\to\ell^2}^2\sum_j\|B_{\bullet j}\|_{\ell^2}^2.
\]
The operator norm on the right is finite.  The entrywise
Cauchy--Schwarz inequality bounds it by
$(\sum_{i,j}|A_{ij}|^2)^{1/2}$.
Conversely, take a matrix $B$ whose only nonzero column is a finite
vector $v$.  The ratio $\|AB\|/\|B\|$ is $\|Av\|/\|v\|$.
Since $A$ uses only finitely many input coordinates, such vectors
compute its full operator norm.  This proves equality.

For $n\geq1$, let $Q_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}E_{ii}$.
It is a self-adjoint projection with $\tau(Q_n)=n$.
If a positive extension had value $c$ on $I$, then $c$ would be real
and nonnegative, while
\[
 0\leq\tau^+((I-Q_n)^*(I-Q_n))=c-n
\]
for every $n$.  No finite $c$ satisfies these inequalities.
Equivalently,
$|\tau(Q_n)|^2/\tau(Q_n^*Q_n)=n$ is unbounded.
\end{proof}

The map $(\lambda,B)\mapsto\lambda I+B$ identifies the algebraic
unitization with the displayed subalgebra of $\mathscr E$.
It is injective because a scalar multiple of the infinite identity is
a finite matrix only when that scalar is zero.
On this subalgebra, any linear extension of finite-matrix trace has the
form
\[
 \tau_c(\lambda I+B)=c\lambda+\operatorname{tr}(B).
\]
For $A,B\in\mathscr F_0$, its value on a product is
\[
 \tau_c((\lambda I+A)(\mu I+B))
 =c\lambda\mu+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(B)
       +\mu\operatorname{tr}(A)+\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}.
\]
The sum is finite and is unchanged when the two factors are exchanged.
Thus $\tau_c$ is cyclic, but the proposition excludes positivity for every $c$.  In particular, any cyclic trace on
a larger formal differential-operator algebra that restricts to this
formula cannot be positive for an involution restricting to the one above.
This conclusion needs no choice of an involution on the larger algebra.

The finite-dimensional corners $Q_n\mathscr F_0Q_n$ do have positive
traces, and the traces agree under their inclusions.  Their units are
$Q_n$, with masses $n$.  Compatibility of these finite traces supplies
$\tau$ on their union, but supplies no uniform mass bound on a unitization.
The common GNS domain and a vector representing the functional are
therefore separate constructions even in this explicit matrix example.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[Formal completion and Hilbert adjoints]
\label{prop:weil-formal-adjoint}
Conjugate transpose on $\mathscr F_0$ does not extend to a continuous
involution on $\mathscr E$ with its operator topology.
The finite-matrix action on $D_0$ also does not extend by the same
coefficient formula to an action of all $\mathscr E$ on
$\ell^2(\mathbb N)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $T_n=\sum_{i=0}^nE_{i0}$.
Every fixed row stabilizes, so $T_n$ converges in $\mathscr E$ to
\[
 T(h)=h_0(1,1,1,\ldots).
\]
The matrices $T_n^*=\sum_{i=0}^nE_{0i}$ do not converge in
$\mathscr E$.  Their zeroth rows do not stabilize, and an entire output
row is discrete in the operator topology.  Continuity of a proposed
involution would send the convergent sequence $T_n$ to a convergent
sequence, which is impossible.
For the second assertion, $e_0\in\ell^2$ but
$T(e_0)=(1,1,\ldots)\notin\ell^2$.
\end{proof}

There is also a direct norm obstruction.
The projections $E_{nn}$ tend to zero in the formal operator topology,
but each has Hilbert operator norm one and GNS vector norm one for
$\tau$.  Thus the formal completion, operator-norm completion and
GNS completion are different constructions on the same finite matrices.
For the Weil algebra, Theorem~\ref{thm:graph-domain} specifies a fourth
topology when its multipliers are unbounded: the common graph topology.
None of these completions is selected by cyclicity alone.

\section{Gaussian averages and formal coefficients}
\label{sec:weil-gaussian-coefficients}

The Gaussian in the explicit formula also permits an exact comparison
between a numerical integral and formal polynomial elimination.
It is necessary to specify both the observable algebra and the parameter.
For a positive real number $h$, set
\[
 \mathbb E_h(p)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi h}}
       \int_{\R}p(u)e^{-u^2/(2h)}\,du
       =\int_{\R}p(u)g_{h/2}(u)\,du,
 \qquad p\in\C[u].
\]
The polynomial algebra here uses pointwise multiplication and
$p^*(u)=\overline{p(u)}$ for real $u$.
Its involution fixes $u$.  Polynomial growth and Gaussian decay make
every displayed integral absolutely convergent.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[Numerical and formal Gaussian elimination]
\label{prop:weil-gaussian-comparison}
For every polynomial $p$ and real $h>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E_h(p)
 =\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{h^m}{2^m m!}\,p^{(2m)}(0).
 \label{eq:weil-gaussian-moments}
\end{equation}
The sum is finite.  In particular, $\mathbb E_h$ is positive,
$\mathbb E_h(u)=0$ and $\mathbb E_h(u^2)=h$.

Let $\hbar$ instead be a formal central variable of degree zero.
The same finite derivative formula on each polynomial gives a continuous
$\C[[\hbar]]$-linear map
\[
 \mathsf G:\C[u][[\hbar]]\longrightarrow\C[[\hbar]],\qquad
 \mathsf G\!\left(\sum_{j\geq0}p_j(u)\hbar^j\right)
  =\sum_{j,m\geq0}\frac{p_j^{(2m)}(0)}{2^m m!}\hbar^{j+m}.
\]
Continuity refers to the $\hbar$-adic topologies.
On $\C[u,\hbar]$, evaluating this result at $\hbar=h>0$ agrees
with the numerical Gaussian average of the specialized polynomial.
There is no unital $\C$-algebra homomorphism
$\C[[\hbar]]\to\C$ that sends $\hbar$ to a nonzero number $h$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussian} gives $\mathbb E_h(1)=1$.
Reflection makes every odd moment zero.
For $n\geq2$, integrate the derivative of
$u^{n-1}e^{-u^2/(2h)}$ over $\R$.
Its values at both ends are zero, and its derivative is integrable.
It follows that
\[
 \mathbb E_h(u^n)=(n-1)h\,\mathbb E_h(u^{n-2}).
\]
Thus
$\mathbb E_h(u^{2m})=(2m)!h^m/(2^m m!)$.
Expansion of $p$ proves \eqref{eq:weil-gaussian-moments}.
The positive integral of $|p|^2$ proves positivity, and the moments
of $u$ and $u^2$ have the stated values.

In the formal formula, the coefficient of $\hbar^N$ uses only
$j,m\geq0$ with $j+m=N$.  There are finitely many such pairs.
Each derivative is a derivative of a polynomial, so this defines every
coefficient without a convergence assumption.
The map preserves every power of $\hbar$, proving continuity.
Reindexing these finite coefficient sums proves
$\C[[\hbar]]$-linearity.  For a polynomial in $u,\hbar$, all sums are
finite, so numerical specialization commutes with the proved moment formula.

For $h\ne0$, the formal series
\[
 (1-\hbar/h)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq0}(\hbar/h)^n
\]
is an inverse in $\C[[\hbar]]$.
A unital homomorphism sending $\hbar$ to $h$ would send the unit
$1-\hbar/h$ to zero, which is impossible.
At $h=0$, the constant-coefficient homomorphism does exist.
\end{proof}

The comparison at positive $h$ therefore holds on the polynomial
subalgebra.  Extension to a selected class of infinite series requires
an additional summability rule and its compatibility with the operations.
Formal coefficientwise existence alone gives no such rule.

The Gaussian average is not multiplicative:
$\mathbb E_h(u)^2=0$ while $\mathbb E_h(u^2)=h$.
Nor does inserting a Gaussian turn pointwise polynomial multiplication
into convolution.  For $A>0$, the linear map
\[
 J_A:\C[u]\longrightarrow\A_\zeta,\qquad J_A(p)(u)=p(u)g_A(u)
\]
is well-defined, since every derivative is a polynomial times a Gaussian.
But
\[
 J_A(1)\star J_A(1)=g_{2A}\ne g_A=J_A(1),
 \qquad J_A(u)^*=-J_A(u)\ne J_A(u^*).
\]
The first inequality follows already from the values at zero.
The second uses the reflection in the convolution involution.
Thus $J_A$ is neither an algebra homomorphism nor an involution-preserving
map.  Positivity of the numerical Gaussian average cannot pass through
it by Theorem~\ref{thm:dagger}.

\section{The remaining comparison with quantum currents}
\label{sec:weil-current-comparison}

The formal Hamiltonian bracket on $H_{\mathrm{for}}$ is
$[f,g]=f_xg_y-f_yg_x$ modulo constants.
Put $a_f=-f_y\partial_x+f_x\partial_y$ on this quotient.
Changing a representative by a constant changes neither the derivatives
nor the induced operator.  An output through degree $N$ uses only
monomials through degree $N+1$ in each input, because a bracket of
monomials has degree equal to the sum of their degrees minus two.
This proves continuity, including continuity of $f\mapsto a_f$ in the
row topology.  Computing the two derivative coefficients gives
\[
 [a_f,a_g]=-\partial_y[f,g]\,\partial_x
                 +\partial_x[f,g]\,\partial_y=a_{[f,g]}.
\]
Thus these operators give an actual symmetry action on $H_{\mathrm{for}}$.
A numerical positive representation needs further data: a complex
Hilbert space, a common invariant dense domain, and
operators whose restricted adjoints realize the chosen involution.
Proposition~\ref{prop:weil-formal-adjoint} prevents obtaining these data
by completing all finite matrix kernels in the formal operator topology.
Proposition~\ref{prop:weil-gaussian-comparison} also prevents specializing
an arbitrary formal parameter at a positive numerical value.
These are restrictions on the stated carriers and maps.

A unital algebra generated by observables includes the empty product as its unit.
For a positive comparison, one might choose an ordinary degree-zero
subalgebra containing this identity and closed under an involution.
The finite-mass calculation excludes this choice if the positive scalar
functional is to be defined on the entire subalgebra.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[An obstruction for unital observable algebras]
\label{prop:weil-unital-current}
There is no unital complex $*$-algebra $\mathfrak B$, positive linear
functional $\omega:\mathfrak B\to\C$, and $*$-homomorphism
$T:\mathscr D\to\mathfrak B$ such that $L_\zeta|_{\mathscr D}=\omega\circ T$.
In particular, no such map exists on $\cA_\zeta$.
No continuity hypothesis on $T$ or $\omega$ is needed.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose these data exist.  Put $m=\omega(1)$, a finite nonnegative real
number.  Extend $T$ to the algebraic unitization by
\[
 T^+(f+\lambda1)=T(f)+\lambda1_{\mathfrak B}.
\]
The unitization product and the multiplicativity of $T$ show that $T^+$
is a unital $*$-homomorphism.
Thus $\omega\circ T^+$ is a positive extension of
$L_\zeta|_{\mathscr D}$ with mass $m$.
By \eqref{eq:unit-criterion},
\[
 |L_\zeta(a_\epsilon)|^2
       \leq m L_\zeta(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)\qquad(\epsilon>0).
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:weil-compact-mass} makes the ratio on the left to
$L_\zeta(a_\epsilon^*\star a_\epsilon)$ unbounded as $\epsilon$ decreases to zero.
The denominator is positive for all sufficiently small $\epsilon$, by the same
asymptotic.  This contradicts the finite bound $m$.
\end{proof}

A remaining positive arithmetic comparison must therefore choose a
proper nonunital algebra on which the functional is finite.
Its first missing map is an involution-preserving algebra map
\[
 T:\A_\zeta\longrightarrow\mathfrak B
\]
into such a specified algebra of current observables, together with a positive
linear functional $\omega$ on $\mathfrak B$ satisfying
$L_\zeta=\omega\circ T$.
The algebra, involution, domain and positivity of $\omega$ must be
constructed independently of the zeta zero locations.
Theorem~\ref{thm:dagger} would give the isometry
$U:[f]\mapsto[T(f)]$ onto the closed subspace
$K=\overline{[T(\A_\zeta)]}\subset\mathcal H_\omega$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:graph-transfer} identifies the transported common
graph domain with that of the restricted multipliers on
$E=[T(\A_\zeta)]\subset K$, with every closure taken in $K$.
Equality with the full ambient common domain requires a further
hypothesis, such as surjectivity of $T$.
The finite-mass obstruction in Theorem~\ref{thm:weil-compact-mass} still applies:
a finite-norm vector representing this functional, or a positive
functional on a $C^*$-algebra as target, cannot supply this factorization.
A positive functional on a proper nonunital algebra remains a possible
kind of carrier, as the finite-matrix trace demonstrates.  This example
does not supply the map $T$ or the equality with $L_\zeta$.

Any current-field construction must also specify its operator products,
contraction tensors and the scalar trace or weight on those products.
Their convergence with the spatial propagators is an additional analytic
condition.  Neither the strict zero strip nor the equality of the
Gaussian moment formulas constructs this comparison or proves the
positivity of the full Weil form.
The separate two-point requirement in Remark~\ref{rem:weil-two-point}
does not assert this convolution-algebra factorization.



\section{Local prime forms and the full arithmetic functional}
\label{sec:full-prime-comparison}

For the exact comparison with $L_\zeta$, put
$C_X=C_{X,1/2}$ and let $W_X$ have coefficients
$c_n=\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)/\sqrt n$ for $n\leq X$, zero otherwise.
Retain the polar and archimedean functional
\[
 R_\infty(g)=\widehat g(i/2)+\widehat g(-i/2)
  +\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(t)
       \bigl[\operatorname{Re}\psi(1/4+it/2)-\log\pi\bigr]dt.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Subtraction and the remaining arithmetic form]
\label{prop:wp-native-comparison}
For every $g\in\cA_\zeta$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 L_\zeta(g)={}&W_X(g)+R_\infty(g)-2C_Xg(0)\\
 &-\sum_{n>X}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}
                            \bigl(g(\log n)+g(-\log n)\bigr).
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:wp-native-comparison}
\end{equation}
The tail tends to zero as $X\to\infty$.
Consequently
\[
 \lim_{X\to\infty}\bigl(W_X(g)-2C_Xg(0)\bigr)
 =-\sum_{n\geq2}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{vM}}(n)}{\sqrt n}
                            \bigl(g(\log n)+g(-\log n)\bigr).
\]
The functional on the right is strictly negative on every Gaussian
convolution square.
For each nonzero $f\in\cA_\zeta$,
\begin{equation}
 R_\infty(f^*\star f)-2C_X\|f\|_2^2\longrightarrow-\infty.
 \label{eq:wp-residual-negative}
\end{equation}
Thus the full functional cannot be obtained as the increasing limit of
these positive prime forms, or by adding a positive residual to each
sufficiently large cutoff.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substitute the definition of $W_X$ into \eqref{eq:weil-functional}.
This gives \eqref{eq:wp-native-comparison} with its stated signs.
Choose $a>1/2$ such that $g\in\mathcal A_a$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:test-bounds} bounds the absolute tail by
\[
 K_g\sum_{n>X}(\log n)n^{-a-1/2},
\]
which tends to zero because $a+1/2>1$.
The Gaussian $g_{2b}$ is strictly positive at every real argument.
At least the $n=2$ term is nonzero, so the negative prime sum on
$g_{2b}=g_b^*\star g_b$ is strictly negative.
The value $R_\infty(f^*\star f)$ is finite and independent of $X$.
Since $C_X\to\infty$ and $\|f\|_2>0$,
\eqref{eq:wp-residual-negative} follows.
The full residual $L_\zeta-W_X$ differs from this expression on the
chosen square by a tail tending to zero.
It is therefore negative there for every sufficiently large $X$.
\end{proof}

The construction gives an arithmetic $*$-homomorphism and a positive
functional with the required failure of finite mass.
Its pullback is $W_c$, with the exact diagonal compensation specified above.
For every fixed summable coefficient family, the Gaussian asymptotics
also distinguish this pullback from the full Weil functional:
\[
 \frac{W_c(g_a)}{L_\zeta(g_a)}
 \sim\frac{4C}{\log(1/a)}\longrightarrow0
 \qquad(a\downarrow0).
\]
This follows by dividing \eqref{eq:wp-infinite-mass} by
\eqref{eq:weil-gaussian-asymptotic}.
The equality $L_\zeta=\omega\circ T$ still requires a construction that
combines the prime, polar, and archimedean terms before asserting positivity.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wp-native-comparison} gives the required termwise equality.
The current construction above identifies its operators and domains
with these compensated positive forms.  It does not change the remaining
full-functional equality.

\subsection{The full Weil comparison}

For a finite prime set $P$, put $C_P=\sum_{p\in P}C_p$.
Grouping the same local terms by complete prime factors gives, for
$g\in\cA_\zeta$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 L_\zeta(g)-W_P(g)
 ={}&R_\infty(g)-2C_Pg(0)\\
 &-\sum_{p\notin P}\sum_{m\geq1}
 \frac{\log p}{p^{m/2}}
 \bigl(g(m\log p)+g(-m\log p)\bigr).
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:wc-weil-comparison}
\end{equation}
The double tail tends to zero along increasing finite prime sets.
Indeed, the prime series in the explicit formula converges absolutely on this test algebra,
so dominated convergence permits regrouping and exhaustion by primes.
Also $C_P\to\infty$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:wp-prime-limit}.
For a fixed nonzero square $g=f^*\star f$, the residual in
\eqref{eq:wc-weil-comparison} therefore tends to $-\infty$.

In particular, an additional orthogonal positive current sector cannot
turn these cumulative currents into a covariance for $L_\zeta$.
Such a sector would require
$L_\zeta(f^*\star f)-W_P(f^*\star f)\geq0$ for every test $f$.
The preceding limit violates that condition for every fixed nonzero
$f$ once $P$ is sufficiently large.
A positive realization of the full functional requires a covariance
agreeing with the prime, polar, and archimedean terms together.
This obstruction concerns the specified growing orthogonal family;
it does not exclude a different carrier or comparison map.
It requires an actual map $V$ on the same test algebra with
\[
 \langle V(f),V(h)\rangle=L_\zeta(h^*\star f),
\]
together with the desired operator products and their common domain.
The present currents establish that identity with $W_P$ in place of
$L_\zeta$.  Their commutation relations do not provide the displayed
full-functional identity or a comparison with other quantum current algebras.


\section{The independent arithmetic question}

\begin{proposition}[Formal factorization]
For a linear functional $W$ on a complex $*$-algebra $\mathfrak A$, the
following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $W$ is positive;
\item there are a complex $*$-algebra $\mathfrak B$ and maps
\[
 \omega:\mathfrak B\longrightarrow\C,
 \qquad T:\mathfrak A\longrightarrow\mathfrak B,
\]
where $\omega$ is positive, $T$ is a $*$-homomorphism, and
$W=\omega\circ T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Dagger transfer proves $(\mathrm{ii})\Rightarrow(\mathrm{i})$.  For the converse take
$\mathfrak B=\mathfrak A$, $\omega=W$, and $T$ equal to the identity.
\end{proof}

For $W=L_\zeta$, formal factorization is therefore equivalent to the
positivity in \eqref{eq:weil-criterion}, and hence to RH.  The identity
factorization, or a Hilbert space whose spectral data are prescribed from
the zeta zeros, cannot establish that positivity independently.

The compact-window forms give a quantitative version of the same
positivity question.  Theorem~\ref{thm:cofinal-equivalence} gives explicit
indices and finite tail maps with vanishing approximation errors.
Full positivity is equivalent to a vanishing negative part of the
remaining signed matrices.  Proposition~\ref{prop:window-near-null}
excludes positive power-law margins even on a cofinal sequence.
Neither the fixed-window bounds in Theorem~\ref{thm:window-certified}
nor the independently positive prime currents establish the signed estimate.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{problem}[Independent dagger realization of $L_\zeta$]
Construct a complex $*$-algebra $\mathfrak B$, a positive linear
functional $\omega:\mathfrak B\to\C$, and a $*$-homomorphism
$T:\cA_\zeta\to\mathfrak B$ such that
\begin{equation}
 L_\zeta=\omega\circ T.
 \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
Derive the carrier, the map, and the positivity of $\omega$ from the
polar, archimedean, and prime-power structures in
\eqref{eq:weil-functional}, without using RH, the multiset of zeta zeros,
or an assertion equivalent to \eqref{eq:weil-criterion}.  Prove
\eqref{eq:factorization} with all three local terms and the Fourier and
measure conventions above.  Theorem~\ref{thm:weil-compact-mass} and Corollary~\ref{cor:weil-carriers}
exclude a positive functional defined on an entire $C^*$-algebra as the target.
Any admissible positive functional $\omega$ must fail the finite-mass
unitization bound on the image of $T$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:graph-domain} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:graph-transfer} then specify the common invariant
domain supplied by this factorization.
\end{problem}

The resulting representation would still need an additional comparison
to a spectral determinant.  Equality of functionals does not preserve
trace multiplicities.  For example, on $\C$ the positive functional
$W(z)=mz$, with integer $m\geq2$, has a one-dimensional GNS space.
A scalar $\lambda$ consequently has determinant $s-\lambda$ there.
The trace realization $z\mapsto zI_m$ on $\C^m$ has the same functional
and determinant $(s-\lambda)^m$.  Thus a determinant realization must
identify the operator, its domain, and spectral multiplicities separately.
